Living with Craft Feels Good

“The essence of living process... is a movement towards a structure which is precious. Above all, it is a movement toward a structure which makes us feel our own existence most deeply” -C. Alexander

River Kenna
Sep 17
 
READ IN APP
 

I know a guy, a day trader. He spent six years at a monastery in Nepal, training insight meditation, sharpening himself on the three characteristics, on Vipassana meditation. He’s got a very precise mind and presence; when you meet him, it’s somehow unsurprising that a guy who spent years of his life in a monastery ended up in day trading. It feels natural, somehow.

When I hear him tell it, trading feels like an extension of the monastery, like he brought it with him instead of leaving. “It’s a really constant training in craving and aversion,” he was telling someone at the dinner table, “when everything’s happening so fast, and I have to stay on-strategy while also feeling my mind for hints of craving or aversion, greed or fear, allow them to rise and pass away without taking the driver’s seat.”

When he talks about it, a specific brand of jolly concern twists through his facial muscles. It’s not an academic question, or a light-switch binary where ding! fear is present or boop! here comes craving. They seem to be present in everything, and discerning their shades from the shades of the fabric they’re dyed into seems to matter a lot. Following his thread, I could plausibly believe that there’s a way of trading where the primary goal is the persistent polishing of wholesome mental habits. Which isn’t a thing I would have expected myself to find plausible.

I met another guy, months later, also a day trader. He had a similar underlying insight, but went a different way with it.

“I’ve realized that all my worst stretches, they don’t come from bad strategy. They come from me.” His affect was flat, recounting this. “I get greedy and convince myself to abandon strategy so I can make more money. Or I get paranoid and start selling before I should, when I could have made a lot. I’m my own worst liability.” From his face and voice, he could have been talking about a grocery list.

He too had a solution for craving and aversion interfering with his trades: “I’ve been training AI agents to carry out my strategy, so my own bad judgement doesn’t kill my profits.”

Two Paths

There’s two underlying worldviews there, I think. And I think we’re all kind of navigating those two paths. I think our global culture is navigating a collective choice of paths here; and while the technology might be a catalyst and intensifier, the choice is much more foundational than the technological possibilities.

Do we approach our lives and our work as opportunities to develop ourselves and our worlds at every moment? Or do we approach them as ways to extract our stated goals with minimum risk or fuss?

Do I braindump my talking points into Claude and have it generate this essay for me? Or do I use the time, effort, and craft of writing to see myself more clearly? Writing this, I can see the ways that I make this choice seem simple, as if convenience and ease aren’t perfectly understandable and powerful motivations. Can I take on some perspective, notice the ways that I’m belittling the urge to make money by training AI agents to do the work? That I’m glossing over the pervasive precarity and fear that often drive people in a scramble to secure themselves against falling between the cracks? Can I step back and look at my own choices, how backbreakingly, heartshatteringly difficult they’ve been over and over again, and how I’m really not sure I’d recommend my own choices to that many others, even as grateful as I am that I made them?

I think there’s a real hunger for developing deeply human, analog mastery these days. For not stepping back and assuming that if an algorithm can do it, I should let the algorithm do it. For recognizing that the effects of our work are more than just their linear, legible, measurable outcomes.

You are entitled to your work,
but not to the fruits of your work

— Bhagavad Gita

And I also think it’s easy for me to slip into disdain and contempt for the linear, legible, measurable route. I think that contempt is largely my own fear, a fear that if I take on external, prosthetic measures of Value, and let those drive my choices, something essential and Living in me will atrophy, and I won’t notice until it’s too late.

Can writing be a way for me to stand in what feels true to me, while also seeing and scrubbing at the unwholesome shades that get dyed into that fabric?

Nourishing the Whole Person

“The ultimate goal of farming is not the growing of crops, but the cultivation and perfection of human beings.”

— Masanobu Fukuoka

Wendell Berry describes Fukuoka’s natural farming method by saying that “An agriculture that is whole nourishes the whole person, body and soul. We do not live by bread alone.” I think this cross-applies to any domain, any place where conscious, attentive human skill can be developed and put to use.

Day-trading that is whole nourishes the whole person, body and soul.

Sailing that is whole nourishes the whole person, body and soul.

Writing that is whole nourishes the whole person, body and soul.

Christopher Alexander, an architect, found the same thing in creative processes:

“For some reason, the process of making things which are alive enlarges us, deepens our experience. I feel more alive for having done it. It is like food. […] Each person is nourished, made more whole, by making something which has life.”

The same way that the ultimate goal of farming, in this point of view, isn’t it’s stated linear outcome (crops, or the created product), but the cultivation of human beings (and, I would add, the perfection not only of humans but of World) — in that same way, the ultimate goal of any vocation isn’t the stated linear product, but the deep cultivation and perfection of Life and World. The product is, in many ways, an excuse to cultivate that deeper perfectibility. (I’m squeamish about the word perfection here — easily slips into rigid senses of correctness and not-enoughness, rather than the steady naturalness that cultivation feels like to me. Which, that steady naturalness is exactly what feels “perfect” to me.)

The ultimate goal of government isn’t just managing laws and resources, but the cultivation and perfection of Life and World.

The ultimate goal of finance isn’t just making money, but the cultivation and perfection of Life and World.

The ultimate goal of healing professions isn’t just the soothing of symptoms, but the cultivation and perfection of Life and World.

Craft

I’ve been drawn to craft, the past few months. To people who find perfection and wholeness in the practice of their craft.

Fukuoka was one of these, I’ve resonated deeply with the view of Reality that he found in farming. And more relevantly, I’ve resonated deeply with the fact that his view emerged from attentive openness to his craft. To the reality of his craft, not just the habits and received teachings about it. He didn’t accept what everyone knew about agriculture, or impose those ideas on the land — he returned to the land and learned from it. He listened.

One of the last traditional Polynesian wayfinders, Mau Piailug said:

“Everything is there in the ocean for you to learn, but it will take you 20 years to see.”

This is true of the ocean, and it’s true of everything. Any aspect of Reality that you apply yourself to openly — not as an object, but as a dynamic presence that you’re in relationship with — contains everything you need to learn in order to understand it.

Piailug found this in the sea, Fukuoka found it in the land and its plants, Christopher Alexander found it in architecture:

“The connection between the life of a made thing and the healing effect it has on the maker is, I believe, very succinctly put just as simply as this: People are deeply nourished by the process of creating wholeness”

M.C. Richards found her own relationship with a living universe through pottery, and the teaching of pottery:

“There has to be in our pedagogy this dialogue between release of energy and direction of energy between following pleasure and following wisdom; for the fullest release comes when the right direction is found, and the tenderest rapture comes in the depths of law.”

Passages like these feel surprising coming from sailors and architects and craftsmen rather than monks or what have you. I don’t think they should, though — in many ways it’s the least surprising thing in the world.

We’ve built a world of specialization, where The Sacred™ is separated out from the mundane. (Sacred literally means “set apart” and mundane literally means “the world” — our culture only functions by setting the sacred apart from the world.) But in a healthy situation, everyone who cares to can build a deep relationship with Reality by pursuing their vocation. It’s the most undramatic thing there is, no mountaintop caves required — unless your relationship with Reality takes you that way. The gap between the monastery and the trading desk (or the potter’s wheel, or the farmer’s field) is mostly constructed. It can be bridged.

Rasayana

When I look around, most people don’t seem very nourished. I don’t think most people have work that they can relate to as nourishing processes — as opportunities for cultivating Life and World.

Without access to that kind of nourishment, dramatic stuff flares up.

We might inflate ourselves and our experience to make it through the week, or deflate the world around us to relieve the pressure.

Inflation can look like status games, big mythopoetic self-identifications, money obsession, living a story that puts us at the center of The World-Fate… anything that separates an aspect or two of experience and pumps it up into something big, dramatic, separate from or above other aspects of Life. Separate from or above other people, or other lives.

Deflation can look like nihilism, rationalism, stories that reduce the many miracles of experience down to just chemicals, just random chance and inevitable physics, just filling time between birth and death, everything is empty, nothing matters more than anything else.

Inflation and deflation are the same move — to inflate something in a system you have to deflate something else, and vice versa. It’s the same injury, viewed from different sites.

Nourishing vocation or craft can (at least it’s been seeming this way to me) help to correct that injury. When I’m able to feel god in my writing, in my cooking, in my conversations, things get quite simple, quite undramatic. I’ve been fed, I’m satisfied and enthusiastic — what else is there? God is glowing in my work, which makes it easier to see that glow everywhere else, too — what else is there?

I look around at people with higher status, deeper community, cooler skills, more money, better health, leaner bellies, more natural openness — and all of those things feel delightful, I think I’d like to have all of them. I might even get a few. But I don’t need them, I don’t feel lacking or malnourished from them. I can feel the glow of god in those Lives and their blessings, and I can feel the glow of god in my Life and my blessings, and it’s hard too work up too much craving for things to be all that different. Sometimes, yes, the glow feels like desire to pursue something new, to open up to the interest, fun, and luminosity of blessings I don’t already have… but that’s a very different thing from craving.¹

I don’t know; it feels good to me to live with craft. I’m not exactly sure what I mean by living with craft, but it feels right. I’m reticent, when I find something the feels like an opportunity to cultivate Life and World, to turn it over to an algorithm, or a professional, or to skip it in favor of infinite scrolling feeds. (Oh, ha, there’s a tweet in there I’m sure — something about infinite feeding without any nourishing; woe is the modern condition, woe woe.)

I could keep following this thread — craft, nourishment, and the paths we find ourselves choosing — for a very long time. But I’ll close out with another quote from Christopher Alexander, on the creative process.

“Learning to see wholeness, or self, in a thing is not unlike the process of straining one’s ears to catch that haunting tune. I look at the thing which I am making, I keep on looking at it, and slowly I begin to see a spirit in the thing. I become aware of an emerging wholeness, only dimly heard at first. […] I do my best to bring this half-heard whisper of a being out in the material.”

To me, there’s something here that sits close to the core². This attitude of stilling myself to hear the “half-heard whisper of a being”, and doing my best to bring that being into the world, in the way it wants to be brought in… if Life and craft are approached from that place, there’s nothing that Reality won’t reveal to us. Nothing that’s off-limits to Remember about Life and World.

That’s what cultivation often is, at it’s best: continually listening to some half-heard whisper of wholeness that echoes out of the center of my Life, and working with it to craft the corner of Reality I find myself in. Whether that corner is a farm, a pottery wheel, a writing desk, a surfboard, or a conversation with someone I love — someone who’s half-heard whispers I’m happy to listen for alongside my own.

1

That paragraph is interesting to me — I can feel that even a few months ago, I would have rewritten it before publishing. Using vocabulary like “the glow of god” or “blessings” would have felt icky, sticky, cliche, earnest, pablum. Now they feel pretty simple. Like some inflation-deflation injury has softened, and those words are just… the right words for the situation. No need to torture myself (or the sentences) about it. I wouldn’t have noticed this process in myself if I wasn’t approaching editing with an eye towards craft and nourishment.

2

I don’t love the use of “straining” here. I prefer a mode I’ve gotten from Realization Process work, where I notice the ways I’m actively trying to see or hear something — and drop that trying or straining in favor of simply allowing and receiving what’s present to see and hear, without constriction.
