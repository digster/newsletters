Democrats, don’t lie to yourselves: This is not a permanent realignment. The middle is molten.Two years after almost every demographic shifted right, literally every demographic shifted toward Democrats, according to the New York Times. The biggest shifts were among voters under 30 (+32%), men under 45 (+31%), and Hispanics (+28%). Two years ago, some commentators—like me!—speculated that Trump had captured young voters and made serious inroads among Hispanics. But on Tuesday, those groups moved toward Democrats more dramatically than any other. Is it a permanent realignment? I doubt it. I think it’s more likely that, in the 2020s—an era of high inflation, high interest rates, low institutional trust, global crises, and existential fears about climate change and AI—there are a lot of loosely attached independent voters who can toggle from party to party. Young voters and nonwhite voters (who are, themselves, disproportionately younger than whites) are not so much punching tickets to join a party as they are leasing one-year terms to vote for one group, only to punish them in the next election if things don’t improve. Big Money had a bad nightA popular take that I see all the time is that “billionaires are increasingly controlling our elections.” It’s popular in part because it’s somewhat true: The share of campaign financing coming from billionaires has soared in the last few election cycles, which would pose an existential crisis to democracy if it turned out that a handful of individuals could translate their income into political power that further entrenched their corporate power and wealth, creating an endless, vicious cycle of plutocratic self-gifting. But reality is rarely as cut-and-dried as the takes we use to describe it. As the New York Times’ Teddy Schleiffer points out, billionaires just got their asses handed to them. Elon Musk just wasted hundreds of millions of dollars propping up Ken Paxton in a blowout election in Texas. From sea to shining sea, up and down the ballot, the Tech Right lost, Republican donors lost, and AI lost. There’s a funny paradox here, where “Billionaires increasingly control our elections” feels true, but precisely because it feels true, it cannot fully be true, because voters react to the feeling of being bought by revolting against the billionaire class. 9. Nobody knows what the Republican Party is right now.Donald Trump is hideously unpopular in every context, except the context that matters most for Republican presidential hopefuls—which is to say, among die-hard Republicans who show up to vote in primaries. This will create a conundrum for conservatives who recognize that they have to move beyond his policies to win any national election without distancing themselves from a man who still possesses a voodoo-like power over the party faithful. Today, Vice President JD Vance gave us a glimpse of what that might look like, in a long post whose bottom line is that Vance believes that Americans dislike the Iran War, high prices, and America’s overly close relationship with the Israeli government.
To sum things up: Vance wants the GOP to end the war that Trump started; to focus on the cost of living crisis that Trump exacerbated with tariffs and wars; and to change America’s relationship with Netanyahu when it was Trump who embraced Bibi’s theory of bombing Iran. The message here seems to be: I stand for Trumpism, which means doing the opposite of Donald Trump in every way. Ok, good luck, I guess! I see several paths forward for post-Trump GOP politics. The first is a Catholic MAGA fusion represented by JD Vance, which attempts a kind of Trumpless Trumpism, with significantly more Yale Law School theory and significantly less stump-speech charisma. The second is a quiet neoconservative comeback, typified by Marco Rubio doing his best 2000-era George W. Bush impersonation. The third is an off-the-wall, anti-establishment conspiracy movement headlined by RFK, Jr. And the fourth is corporate neo-Reaganism as embodied by someone like Ron DeSantis.
