A lot of what you might want to know about Jim Belosic can be learned from his childhood home. Belosic grew up in an immaculate Victorian house that sits on 2.5 acres in Genoa, Nevada. Genoa is the oldest town in Nevada with the oldest saloon (still operating) and has all of 1,300 residents. Historically, Genoans worked on ranches and farms amid a bucolic setting where the Sierra Nevadas give way to pastoral lowlands. As such, there was not much of anything around the Belosics’ house when they started building it in 1977. Vic and Julie Belosic had fled the bustle of Southern California in an Econoline van looking for something different. (Well, Vic was looking for something different. Julie was quite happy.) They were both good with their hands and decided to construct their Nevada home themselves. The project began with a barn (where the couple and their young daughter lived) and then expanded to the main house a couple of years later. The house itself was a work in progress when Jim arrived in 1980 and remained that way through much of his adolescence. Belosic is the founder and CEO of SendCutSend. Many of you reading this story will not have heard of SendCutSend. But, if you craft or manufacture things as a hobby or at work, then you probably know the company well. SendCutSend operates factories full of machines that cut, bend and shape metal into custom parts. We’re talking objects for people trying to modify their cars right on up to the key components of rockets, robots and defense equipment. Many of SendCutSend’s more than 300,000 customers were turning to China for this type of work until Belosic, who founded the company in 2018, figured out how to make lots of metal parts well, fast and cheap and offered an option right in the United States. As such, SendCutSend and Belosic have become key players in America’s effort to learn how to manufacture things again, although playing a part in a national movement was never really Belosic’s intention. The origin story of this manufacturing phenomenon goes back to the Genoa house and its ongoing construction during Belosic’s childhood. At six, Belosic stood by his dad in the dining room catching squares of wood as they came off a table saw. Belosic would then sand the edges and hand them back to his dad who nailed them up near the ceiling to create dentil molding. At ten, Belosic had a bandsaw-themed birthday party where he and his friends built Pinewood Derby cars. Not long after that, Vic decided he didn’t like the look of the staircase and set to rebuilding it, so the family relied on a ladder to reach their bedrooms for several months. “On Sundays, the few other people that were living here would get in their cars, and they would mosey down into town and have a good time as a family,” says Julie. “But not us. We were banging hammers. It was so quiet around here that we could hear the hammers bouncing off the mountains.” Out back, Vic always had a half-built car project going with nuts and bolts scattered all around it. (Vic has the mangled hands of a proper maker.) Belosic would help out on those efforts too or explore the possibilities presented by the neighboring wood and scrap piles. “You had to keep yourself entertained,” says Belosic. “There was no internet, and just one neighbor three miles away who I could make it to on my Go-kart.” Besides exploring, Belosic made money through garage sales and by mowing lawns so that he could afford to improve his Go-kart or buy a radio-controlled car. This environment bestowed a love of tinkering and building inside of Belosic, and this fit his disposition anyway. School was too easy for him, and he didn’t care much for its structure and regulations. Belosic attended the University of Nevada, Reno for six weeks before dropping out to start his own business. “He had to be an entrepreneur,” Julie says. “He was not going to work for somebody else.” To which Jim adds, “What she’s saying is that I’m a really shitty employee. I was forced to work for myself.” JIM BELOSIC is 46 and can often be found in similar attire – work boots, jeans, a black long-sleeve shirt and a black SendCutSend baseball cap. He’s got a thick, brown beard and glasses. His manner oscillates between straightforward and confident and easygoing and jovial. Mostly, he comes off as focused and is something of an anomaly in this moment of reindustrialization hype and plentiful bullshittery in that he’s quite matter-of-fact when he talks about SendCutSend’s business and about the prospects of the U.S. revitalizing its manufacturing backbone. Part of what makes Belosic different is the winding road he took to starting SendCutSend. In addition to all his building as a kid, he’d been into art and design. Belosic went from creating business cards, painting murals and working at a sign-making shop to running his own marketing and ad agency in 2002 that did website design, graphic design and branding. That business grew as his customers asked for more software and services. Then, in 2009, Belosic created a second company that helped customers run contests on Facebook, and the business – ShortStack.com – really took off. Belosic, though, struggled with the reality that B2B SaaS and lead generation services were not really the things that could nourish one’s soul. “The business came from saying, ‘Yes,’ and trying to put a roof over my head and trying to pay payroll,” he says. “It was not my first love.” [Footnote: Belosic acquired many of the skills needed to run these businesses by spending time at his local Barnes & Noble reading books on making websites and developing software.] The good thing about ShortStack was that it eventually provided Belosic with the time and money he needed to chase after his hobbies. Belosic’s first car had been a 1981 Honda Accord with 256,000 miles on it. To honor that vehicle, he bought another Accord around 2016 and began customizing it. He took a motor from a wrecked Tesla Model S and a Chevy Volt battery pack and turned the Accord into an electric hot rod named Teslonda. This work required the use of plasma torches and CNC machines to make the fittings needed to support the EV add-ons. Belosic would also ask job shops to make parts for him but discovered that no one wanted to do the low-volume work. “I was finding the limits of my tools and getting a small shop to make one or two custom parts was nearly impossible,” Belosic says. Belosic began thinking about starting a business to cater to people like him. He considered creating a machine shop that could be run on a membership model where people would share equipment. And he thought about developing software that would simplify things for job shops and make it easier for them to take on one-off projects. But, when he surveyed his friends and machine shop owners, he received lukewarm feedback. “So, I was like, ‘Screw it,’ I’ll do it myself,” he says. In the middle of 2018, Belosic talked his wife into letting him buy a $750,000 Amada laser cutter – at five percent down and five percent interest for five years – and putting it in his 5,000-square-foot hobby shop. He figured that he could make the payments and maybe some extra money if he hired an operator or two and kept the laser cutter running all the time. He kept ShortStack.com operating just in case. Armed with a fresh website and his marketing skills, Belosic launched SendCutSend, which received its first order, for pickleball-themed keychains, in November. Not long after that, Belosic began going to automotive events to show off Teslonda and managed to pick up some more customers. Those hobbyist customers often worked at large companies and began ordering parts for things at work. The word of mouth kept building. Then, when Covid hit, many of the part-making mom-and-pop job shops closed down, and SendCutSend very much turned into a real business. For most of SendCutSend’s history, the company operated without raising substantial venture capital. Belosic would buy more laser cutters; CNC press brakes, milling machines and routers; and waterjet cutters as he could afford them. The company built out its home base in Reno and then expanded with factories in Texas and Kentucky to get parts to different regions more quickly. Large companies and startups across the United States began turning to SendCutSend, with customers including SpaceX, Apple and Anduril. By early 2026, SendCutSend was pumping out 100,000 parts per day. Cut to October, and it’s set to make about 50 million parts per year. In May 2026, the company finally brought in some serious investment. It raised $110 million from Patrick and John Collison, Sequoia and Paradigm. SendCutSend then doubled its valuation to $2.1 billion this month on the back of another $140 million raise. Belosic A SENDCUTSEND factory is a glorious thing to behold. Out on a spacious concrete floor of one building, there are rows of fiber-laser cutting stations – each about 30 feet long and nine feet wide. They’re flanked by towers full of sheet metal, including steel and aluminum. Workers grab the sheets, take them to their machines and let the lasers go at it, cutting out things like metal brackets and washers right on up to welding jigs for rocket motors and components for General Motors’s assembly lines. Software tells the employees what metal to grab for what job and guides the cutting process. In other parts of the factory, SendCutSend has machines for powder coating, plating and anodizing. There are yet more machines for welding, bending and deburring. Bit by bit, the company adds new machines and new functions after testing them out in an R&D lab. Belosic often sneaks the new services onto the SendCutSend website with little fanfare, hoping to delight customers with a surprise. The company has not gone nuts with automation. You see people at the workstations overseeing the machines and then transferring the metal parts to and fro. Belosic argues that some companies spending millions of dollars on robots are doing so because it looks fancy and helps them raise money. SendCutSend also has a unique business making whatever people want when they want it instead of pumping out tons of the same thing. “I’m not shitting on automation,” he says. “If we made the same part over and over, I would automate this place in a heartbeat, but we’re doing high mix, low volume. All of our parts are wildly different, and they all have different order of operations. There’s so much variance.” The key to SendCutSend’s rise has, in many ways, been its software and shipping. Where mom-and-pop shops have been loath to embrace software, SendCutSend has built an online system that lets customers upload their part designs and find out if the parts can be made and how much they will cost. The software tool knows the capabilities of each machine in SendCutSend’s arsenal and how best to arrange the parts on a sheet of metal for efficient cutting. If an order is accepted by noon, SendCutSend can deliver it the next day (in many but not all cases, for an extra fee). The company guards part of its shipping system as if it’s a state secret and refuses to let people photograph or video that part of its factory. What it will show, however, is the candy station where workers pile handfuls of Sour Punch Straws into every box. “We’re the largest sour punch straw distributor in the state of Nevada,” Belosic says. SendCutSend has plenty of competition. It often remains cheaper to order parts from China and have them shipped all the way across the world than to make them in the United States. OSH Cut and Fabworks are stateside rivals, and so too are all the mom-and-pop job shops. Any time I post about metal cutting on X, people will chime in with their pros and cons for each option, including occasional gripes about the precision of SendCutSend’s parts. For the most part, SendCutSend is appreciated for its speed and low prices. It’s hard to find a robotics or aerospace startup in the U.S. that does not use it, particularly for prototyping new products. You just hop online, design a part, and then it shows up at your door. “Even just a year ago, most companies we talk to and work with would send their design files to a Chinese supplier,” says JX Mo, the CEO and co-founder of SendCutSend is part of a new wave of companies trying to rebuild the American industrial base. There are folks making Belosic is of this movement. He’s invested in many of the new manufacturing startups and does not mind doing some USA, USA, USA stuff as needed. But he’s also kind of stumbled into being a symbol of reindustrialization and has reservations about how real some of these manufacturing startups are. “There are some people doing this who are really loud,” he says, while we sip Old Fashioneds at the Genoa Bar and Saloon. “Some of them will be very successful, but then there’s also a lot of capital getting lit on fire. And I’m nervous that a few bad actors are going to ruin it for everybody. I’ll keep doing this agnostic of any policy of any administration or whatever.” He’d also taken great pride in running the company without the need for tons of venture capital. It was a great story. Random dude bootstraps industrial power. “I didn’t set out to change the world to save America or re-industrialize or whatever,” he says. “I set out for a selfish need. I just wanted equipment that I could use for free so that I could wrench on cars.” Belosic now finds himself under pressure from investors who want their returns and needs to turn SendCutSend into so much more than a part supplier built by a hobbyist for hobbyists. For better or worse, he very much is part of the reindustrialization movement, and the next few years will determine how much substance there is to this renewed manufacturing push and how far SendCutSend can actually grow. “I didn’t want to be the spokesperson for this,” Belosic says. “I like to put my head down and do stuff, but it’s my burden now.” Hopefully, you enjoyed this story.
