Congress Wants To Put Down China’s Robot Dogs

Congress introduced a bill to clamp down on Unitree’s humanoid dominance

Kylie Robison
Jun 4
 
READ IN APP
 

Congress would like you to know that the China-made humanoid robots wandering the streets and warehouses of America might be spying on us.

The Guarding the U.S. Against Adversarial Robotics Dominance (GUARD) Act was introduced last week by Rep. John Moolenaar, who chairs the House Select Committee on China, alongside Reps. Jay Obernolte of California and Jennifer McClellan of Virginia. In a press release published Wednesday, they warn that China-made humanoids and quadrupeds contain backdoors the People’s Liberation Army can fling open at will — a threat one endorser compares to the plot of the 1984 film Red Dawn. Here’s Molenaar’s take:

“Robots made by China are a threat to national security, critical infrastructure, and American workers. They contain backdoors that can be hijacked for espionage, and our legislation will stop these threats now before China can embed them throughout America. Fueled by generous state subsidies, Unitree and other Chinese robotics companies are cheating against American robot makers, flooding the market with artificially cheap products, possibly bankrupting our companies, and leaving us dependent on China. These firms are also closely connected with the Chinese military, and they use their revenue to develop weaponized robots. We must act today to ban malign foreign-made robots to protect the safety of Americans.”

The actual text of the legislation runs seven pages and never mentions a backdoor, never names a company, and never once uses the word “spy.” What it actually proposes is, unfortunately, much more boring. The bill says an “appropriate national security agency” shall determine, within one year, whether a given robot poses an “unacceptable risk to the national security of the United States.” If it does, the Federal Communications Commission adds it to the agency’s Covered List, the same blacklist that already bans Huawei and ZTE telecom gear from American networks. If the agency decides the robot is fine, it files a report and other agencies get six months to second-guess the call.

If no agency makes a determination within a year, the robot gets added to the Covered List automatically. That’s the same mechanism the FCC used to ban the China-owned drone company DJI. No one completed the review; DJI was banned; American drone startups rejoiced.

The GUARD bill applies only to legged robots, in other words, humanoids walking on two limbs and quadrupeds, a.k.a robot dogs, on four. So, the China-owned robot vacuum you ordered from Amazon is safe. (Or buy a Matic vacuum just in case.) It also doesn’t ban “robots” as objects, it’s the “communications equipment” inside them, borrowing the definition from the 2019 Secure and Trusted Communications Networks Act, the law which assisted in the ban of Huawei. In practice, that means you can’t sell the robot if its communication guts are blacklisted.

The bill also never says “China.” It says “country of concern,” and then cross-references a State Department statute to define the term — 22 U.S.C. 2651a — which includes six countries: China, Russia, Iran, North Korea, Cuba, and Syria.

Exactly one of them manufactures legged robots anyone would buy. China, we’re looking at you.

Specifically, it’s a bill about the Hangzhou-based firm Unitree, whose cheap, capable quadrupeds and humanoids have come to dominate the global market and which the Select Committee has been targeting by name for over a year. One report pegs Unitree’s market share at about 26%, just behind Shanghai-based AGIBot at roughly 30%.

Among the bill’s enthusiastic supporters are Agility Robotics, whose CEO Peggy Johnson “welcomes” the legislation and frames it as a market signal for companies investing in domestic manufacturing. Agility, which makes humanoid robots in the U.S, is a direct competitor of Unitree.

THE CENTRAL claim of the bill is that Chinese robots “contain backdoors that can be hijacked for espionage.” In May 2025, researchers found an “undocumented backdoor” in Unitree robots that would allow the manufacturer to remotely control the device whenever they want. Unitree said in a statement to Axios that this is a “common feature among many robots on the market,” but the company patched it anyway. Unitree added that its newer models, like the Go2 quadruped, run on upgraded, more secure software versions that aren’t affected by this security issue.

And yet the robots do remain vulnerable. In September 2025, researchers Andreas Makris and Kevin Finisterre discovered you could hijack a Unitree robot (including the Go2) over Bluetooth. Worse, a hacked robot can infect other Unitree robots nearby on its own.

“A simple attack might be just to reboot the robot, which we published as a proof of concept,” Makris told IEEE Spectrum. “But an attacker could do much more sophisticated things: It would be possible to have a trojan implanted into your robot’s startup routine to exfiltrate data while disabling the ability to install new firmware without the user knowing. And as the vulnerability uses BLE, the robots can easily infect each other, and from there the attacker might have access to an army of robots.”

THE GUARD Act is not yet law, and might never be.

To become enforceable law it has to clear the House committee, pass the House floor, repeat the entire process in the Senate, and get signed by the President. Many bills die during that process, but it helps that robotics is having a moment right now in D.C and Silicon Valley. If you haven’t read my all-time favorite piece I’ve ever written on actuators, and how the government is finally taking notice of the industry, I insist you do.

The GUARD bill is notably bipartisan and comes out of the Select Committee on China, which has been unusually effective at moving China-hawk measures into law. And it rides on the proven mechanism of the FCC Covered List, so there’s no new regime to invent, only an existing one to extend. The realistic paths are two: it limps along as a standalone bill, or — far more likely for a China provision — it gets folded into a must-pass vehicle like the annual defense authorization. The latter is exactly how the DJI drone restrictions passed, through the FY2025 NDAA.

What the bill gets right is that America is, in fact, likely to import a new generation of legged machines from China. They will walk through warehouses and hospitals and eventually homes, and a great many of them will be cheaper and better than the domestic alternatives because Unitree got there first. That’s because the U.S. has totally ceded manufacturing dominance to China in various critical categories: drones, electric vehicles, chips, robotics. We’re playing catch-up, and proposed bills like this one are perhaps the only way for the U.S. to have a chance at playing the game in any meaningful way.

Whether the United States wants to rely on a geopolitical rival for the physical machines that will move through its infrastructure is a legitimate question, and it would remain legitimate even if every Unitree robot were proven clean tomorrow. The GUARD bill works hard to play-up the espionage frame rather than the commercial dependence frame, because “they might be spying” is more frightening than “they outcompeted us,” and fear moves the fastest through Congress. Just ask Nvidia.

The GUARD act, with its beautiful Patrick Swayze references, is ultimately not about humanoid spies. It’s about what America has failed to build itself, and it’s using Congress to force those motorized companions across the rainbow bridge.
