Learning from WatchingOne of the things you realize when you have kids is that babies are learning machines. When they’re really young, they just keep changing, picking up new skills, like little Neos. The thing about babies, though, is that they don’t know how to read. They don’t learn like we learn in school, by reading textbooks, because they can’t. Instead, they watch. They watch how we walk, how we talk, how we smile, how we bring food to our mouths, how we pick up toys and how we put them away, they watch how we shake hands, and hug, and run, and jump, and crawl. Then they try it themselves, clumsily at first, they watch again, they try again, they watch again, they try again, and try and try and try until they’ve gotten it. I’ve tried it, and it simply doesn’t work, at least not for me. Language is compression. We look at the rich, ever-changing world in front of us, and we can’t possibly capture it all. By the time we’ve come anywhere even close to describing what we see at any given time with any amount of precision, it’s changed. And no matter how hard you try, words will never be enough, because you can’t, for example, see each of the atoms in the scene, track the movement of the electrons and protons and neutrons that make up those atoms, see how electromagnetic and gravity waves interact with them… you get it. Everything in the world changes, then changes again, and each time it does we’d have to start describing it again, like verbal Sisyphuses. Instead of running a constant monologue as we walk down the street, describing all of the motion and appearances and interactions we see, we simply say, “The man walked down the street.” Neither approach captures the full picture, but the latter is easier and allows us to actually live our lives. To jack as many subtle details into our brain as possible, therefore, we don’t read about life, we watch it. Our brain has evolved to pull out the relevant information. Made with Runway A picture is worth a thousand words. A video is worth 1,000 times the number of frames plus however many words the motion connecting them and sounds are worth. But the value of video isn’t simply that it contains more information than words. Predicting what happens next and checking how well you predict, over and over again, creates a pressure to learn reusable relationships. Imagine trying to predict the next frame of that street video. To get it right, it isn’t enough to know what the man looks like. You need to know that he’ll keep walking in roughly the direction he was walking, at roughly the speed he was walking, that his left foot comes after his right, that people walking near him will walk behind him and not through him, that when he passes in front of a moving car, that car still exists and will come out the other side a beat later as the same car, that his shadow moves when he moves, etc. When a model is trained on video, nobody tells the model any of this. There’s no line of code that says “objects still exist when something walks in front of them” or “people can’t walk through people.” The model guesses the next frame, sees how wrong it was, and adjusts. Somewhere along the way, the cheapest way for the model to stop being wrong is to learn something like the rules: things fall, and seem to fall in basically the same way at basically the same rate of acceleration (although feathers seem to flutter back and forth while bricks simply drop); glass shatters and rubber bounces; a ball that rolls behind something comes out the other side looking exactly the same, unless there was a puddle of mud. One video with a muddy ball might confuse the model, but over millions of them, it would learn that the mud is an anomaly. These, incidentally, are things that babies learn during their first year. We play peek-a-boo because it makes them laugh, but also because it teaches them object permanence, this idea that things still exist even when you can’t see them. This is how LLMs work, too. Their goal is to predict the next word, by any means necessary. Ilya Sutskever has been making this point for years: predicting the next token correctly requires understanding something about the reality that produced the token. Swap tokens for frames and you have Runway’s thesis. That’s where the first of Runway’s bets comes in: The Bitter Lesson and the Big Blob of VideoIt is practically illegal to write about training models without mentioning The Bitter Lesson. In 2019, Richard Sutton wrote a short essay arguing that the biggest lesson from seventy years of AI research is that “general methods that leverage computation are ultimately the most effective, and by a large margin.” It is bitter because every time researchers have tried to build their own understanding of a domain into a system (chess openings, the structure of grammar, the edges and corners in an image), they’ve eventually been steamrolled by dumber methods that could soak up more compute and more data. For his part, Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei made a similar observation two years earlier, in a The Big Blob of Compute (BBOC) hypothesis, he wrote, “says that creating any given intelligent behavior is mostly about providing a large, minimally structured mass of computational capacity, and then giving it shape and form via interactions with a rich environment and a training process that drives towards behavior appropriate for the task at hand.” Runway’s corollary is that the most scalable source of data tends to win. Call it the Big Blob of Video (BBOV) hypothesis. LLMs won on text because the internet had an absurd amount of it. Robotics has been climbing its own ladder. It used teleoperation data, a specific robot doing a specific task puppeteered by a human. This year, many robotics labs began using a lot more egocentric video from cameras strapped to people’s heads as they perform a task. Runway is betting on something simpler and less human-mediated: vast quantities of video that already exist, and will be filmed (and generated) from here on out. Watching video is the most Bitter Lesson-pilled approach to AI there is. Language is a compression of reality, an incredibly good one that humans have been refining for thousands of years, but a compression nonetheless. As Sutton wrote, “We want AI agents that can discover like we can, not which contain what we have discovered. Building in our discoveries only makes it harder to see how the discovering process can be done.” In other words, we limit agents with our own beliefs. Put more bluntly: if you really believe the Bitter Lesson, you should be working on World Models. There’s a good argument against this thesis, so we might as well make it ourselves. If language is compression, well, compression can be useful! Learning about the world from pixels is less efficient than learning from text. Then there’s Yann LeCun’s critique: predicting every pixel wastes effort on details that don’t matter (every leaf on every tree, every ripple in every puddle), so models should predict in an abstract representation space instead. The simple idea at Runway is next-frame prediction. A language model learns to describe the world by predicting the next token in a sequence of tokens. An autoregressive World Model, as Cristóbal put it, learns to simulate the world by predicting the next frame in a sequence of frames. Autoregressive is a fancy word for a relatively simple idea. The model generates one frame at a time, each based on the frames that came before it, the same way an LLM writes one word at a time. Most video models don’t work like this. They generate a whole clip at once, refining it from static into a picture over dozens of steps, which is great if you’re making a ten-second ad and useless if you’re trying to interact. Interactions stop feeling interactive somewhere around half a second of lag. If you want a model that a person, or an agent, or a robot can actually interact with the way humans do with other humans, it needs to produce the next frame quickly, then the next one, then the next, each taking into account whatever just happened in the previous one and in the reaction to it. That’s what Runway did with GWM-1, which it Teach the model to generate frames sequentially, Distill dozens of denoising steps down to a few, Train the fast model on its own outputs so the quality holds up over a long session.

But robots’ bodies and forms vary wildly, both from each other and from humans’. How can a model that’s mostly watched humans control a robot’s arm precisely? Part of the answer is in how Runway represents actions. Praxis-1 expresses actions in a standard end-effector format: where the gripper (the “hand” at the end of the arm) should be in 3D space, and how it should be oriented. A person’s hand reaching for a mug in a YouTube video can be described in exactly the same terms. So can a humanoid’s hand, a six-axis industrial arm, or a two-armed setup. In one demo, the same policy works in a controlled studio and in a home kitchen without retraining. It may be helpful to think about the three ways World Models can be used in robotics. The first is generating training data. Take a small set of real demonstrations and create variations with new objects, new lighting, and new layouts with which to train the robots on a bunch of different situations they might not see in real world training very quickly. The second is simulation, or testing robot policies inside the World Model before you put them on real hardware. This is where Runway has published its most rigorous evidence. In February, its robotics team took eight different manipulation policies from the RoboArena benchmark, all running on the same Franka Panda arm, and ran each one inside GWM-Robotics, an early version of Praxis-1. Each policy made its own decisions based on what the World Model showed it. Human graders then scored 1,450 simulated rollouts, more than 16,000 ratings in all, about ten per rollout. The ranking of the eight policies in simulation matched their ranking in the real world with a correlation of 0.95, and the graders correctly picked out both the best and the worst policy. It’s a test of ranking (which policy is better than which), not of absolute success rates, but for a robotics team deciding which of your twenty candidate policies deserves time on your three physical robots, ranking is exactly what you need. The theory here, and across the robotics work Runway is doing, is to do as much in relatively cheap simulation as you can before moving to the more expensive physical world. Runway’s model only needs a starting image, compared with competing approaches that require scanning and reconstructing every scene in 3D, which makes it relatively cheaper than even other simulation approaches. The third way is to use the World Model as the robot’s brain. DeepMind’s founding mission was to “solve intelligence, and then use that to solve everything else.” For most of the past decade, that’s been the guiding idea at every AGI lab. In 2024, I wrote an essay called The idea, which we’re pursuing, is to train models on a far wider range of data than we do today. Human-made media, yes, but also observations from scientific experiments and raw sensor data, all at once, with the goal of predicting a larger and larger share of everything that can be observed in the world. Then use those models to run experiments virtually, far faster than we ever could in physical reality. Most visions of the “AI scientist” imagine models that propose experiments for humans to run in a wet lab and then interpret the results. I think that gets the bottleneck backwards. Coming up with experiments is the easy part. Running them is what’s slow. The way to accelerate science is to give it an alternative to the real-world experiment, by simulating it. Large-scale model training also has an anthropomorphic bias. We’ve only really scaled up on data made by humans and legible to humans: text, images, video, sound. That makes sense if what you want is a model that talks to people. It’s far too limiting if what you want is a model that understands the universe. This past July, I extended the argument in an essay called Real-world superintelligence is something else. It means acting better than humans in messy, partially observed systems, where you have to predict how the world will respond when you intervene, choose under uncertainty, and learn from outcomes that show up late and noisy. We’re nowhere close to real-world superintelligence. Frontier models can solve Olympiad math problems and still It’s worse in the real world. Drug trials take many years, trading strategies are expensive to test, and company decisions can take months to play out. In many of these cases, good luck figuring out which inputs exactly led to the outcomes. They are wicked problems. Think about the leadup to Wright Brothers’ famous flight. In 1901, their glider produced only about a third of the lift that the accepted aerodynamic data said it should. Testing a redesign meant building a full-size glider, hauling it to Kitty Hawk, waiting for the right wind, and then trying to fly it. So they built a wind tunnel, a box where they could blow air over small model wings and measure lift and drag, and that fall they tested something like two hundred wing shapes without leaving the shop. AI needs wind tunnels for every domain where experiments are slow and expensive. The labs have started building them by hand. Over the past two years, there’s been a massive build-out of reinforcement learning environments, mostly software clones of things like e-commerce sites and airline booking systems, each one with its own dedicated engineering. That can work for a website, particularly now that AI can build those. For most of the things we actually care about, nobody knows how to hand-build a faithful simulator. To me, our recent releases are wind tunnels.

If I’m able to predict 1,000 different versions of a car crash and I can actually see it, I can learn a lot, and a system and a robot can learn a lot about how to prepare for that. Our next chapter, Chapter 4, is Science. Going beyond human-scale observation means feeding models data from other scales of space and time, plus scientific data. I imagine a single model that spans physics, biology, and everything in between, at scales from the subatomic to the observable universe, and from femtoseconds to the lifespans of stars. It’s prudent to start with related modalities. But our ambition is to tackle the whole thing. You may be asking yourself, “Why would a model that has watched a lot of cat videos be any good at fluid dynamics?” Good question. It sounds like a joke, but there’s growing evidence that it isn’t. I believe physics is the most striking case. In Transfer works for two reasons. The first is that models trained across many domains get good at learning how to learn. The second is that there are common patterns across the scales of the universe. Waves are waves, whether they’re in a pond or a plasma. Things diffuse the same way whether it’s ink in water or heat through a pan. Scientific data today lives in silos, every lab and team with its own slice, which means there’s probably a lot of untapped upside in training on all of it together. The same idea, action-conditioned prediction, is already showing up in other fields. This is where I think World Models will ultimately have the biggest impact: anywhere an agent has to act in an unpredictable environment and learn from the data directly, whether that’s a robot, a cell, or a market. Most of the universe looks like this. The TAM is practically infinite. The endgame of a universal simulator of pixels is a universal simulator of reality.
