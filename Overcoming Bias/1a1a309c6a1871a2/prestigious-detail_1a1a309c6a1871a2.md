Intellectuals get great motivation from big grand questions, and often do work adjacent to them, intending to be relevant to them. Such intellectuals mostly get prestige for the observable impressive detail of their work. Detail such as data collection, stat analysis, game theory models, agent-based models, case studies, phil analysis, engaging public essays, compelling keynote talks, and managing related orgs. When the world changes its mind on such questions, intellectuals often gain prestige from the fact that they had supported those new positions in their work well before the world did. And such intellectuals like to credit their impressive detail for contributing to this intellectual progress. However, intellectual opinions on big grand questions are usually driven more than they like to admit by simple abstract arguments, often processed intuitively. We usually form such opinions soon after hearing of such questions, and then may change our minds as new such arguments occur to us. When forming opinions based on abstract arguments, and opaque intuitions, it is possible to be either sloppy and fast, or careful and slow. Yet it is hard to tell from the form of the results just how much work went into them. The space of abstract arguments is so vast that both approaches result in pretty simple-looking abstract arguments. As a result, it is hard to impress audiences with such arguments. Which is why prestige is given mostly for associated impressive detail. The problem here is that even though abstract arguments are in fact typically relevant and potent for drawing conclusions regarding big grand questions, intellectuals spend most of their efforts on less potent adjacent details. If we would instead think harder on the abstract arguments, we’d plausibly make much faster intellectual progress. Well-subsidized prediction markets re future intellectual opinions on big grand questions would be one way give stronger incentives to think harder about abstract arguments.
