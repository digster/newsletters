​Reality Is Not What It Seems ( https://click.fourhourmail.com/o8u50wr80xfqh62o545fvhqed0rd9uoh75d/7qh7h8h923nwm8szh6/aHR0cHM6Ly93d3cuYW1hem9uLmNvbS9kcC9CMDFONDlHVk0xP2x2PXNodWYmdGFnPW9mZnNpdG9mdGltZmUtMjAmY2hhbm5lbElkPTEmcGxwUmVkaXJlY3Q9bWhGYWxsYmFjaw== ) by Carlo Rovelli ( https://click.fourhourmail.com/o8u50wr80xfqh62o545fvhqed0rd9uoh75d/owhkhqhwm5qg7lbvhr/aHR0cHM6Ly9lbi53aWtpcGVkaWEub3JnL3dpa2kvQ2FybG9fUm92ZWxsaQ== ). ​
“Theoretical physicist Carlo Rovelli takes you on a journey through how our understanding of reality has evolved, from Democritus to Einstein, from Michael Faraday to gravitational waves, and from classical physics to his own pioneering work in quantum gravity. Mind-bending physics made accessible, beautifully narrated by Roy McMillan ( https://click.fourhourmail.com/o8u50wr80xfqh62o545fvhqed0rd9uoh75d/z2hghnhe20lp88fph0/aHR0cHM6Ly93d3cuYXVkaWJsZS5jb20vc2VhcmNoP3NlYXJjaE5hcnJhdG9yPVJveSUyME1jTWlsbGFuJnJlZl9wYWdlbG9hZGlkPXpYcEJPaE5sc2hkVXpheUUmcGZfcmRfcD1mNmRhMGI2My00MzlkLTQ4YTctYTg1OS1iMTg3YjdjNTA3MDUmcGZfcmRfcj04UUs2S0pYQjkzRjFDMzg4RlpaSiZwbGluaz1tV3psWGVIRE9vYlBVUHlCJnBhZ2VMb2FkSWQ9UzhodmV4SDQxSExWZTNTOSZjcmVhdGl2ZUlkPTE2MDE1YmE0LTJlMmQtNGFlMy05M2M1LWU5Mzc3ODFhMjVjZCZyZWY9YV9wZF9SZWFsaXRfcGluX25hcnJhdG9yXzE= ).”

This book pairs incredibly well with When We Cease to Understand the World ( https://click.fourhourmail.com/o8u50wr80xfqh62o545fvhqed0rd9uoh75d/p8heh9h4w8qgl7hqh3/aHR0cHM6Ly93d3cuYW1hem9uLmNvbS9kcC8xNjgxMzc1NjY0P2x2PXNodWYmdGFnPW9mZnNpdG9mdGltZmUtMjAmY2hhbm5lbElkPTEmcGxwUmVkaXJlY3Q9bWhGYWxsYmFjaw== ), which I’ve recommended before.

Research and science I’m following
----------------------------------

“How do bilingual brains navigate between languages? Scientists discover ‘geometric neural map.’ ( https://click.fourhourmail.com/o8u50wr80xfqh62o545fvhqed0rd9uoh75d/6qheh8hl809nd7iohk/aHR0cHM6Ly9uZXdzLnJpY2UuZWR1L25ld3MvMjAyNi9ob3ctZG8tYmlsaW5ndWFsLWJyYWlucy1uYXZpZ2F0ZS1iZXR3ZWVuLWxhbmd1YWdlcy1zY2llbnRpc3RzLWRpc2NvdmVyLWdlb21ldHJpYy1uZXVyYWw= )”
​
This article describes new research just published in Cell ( https://click.fourhourmail.com/o8u50wr80xfqh62o545fvhqed0rd9uoh75d/kkhmh6hn04x63nclh7/aHR0cHM6Ly93d3cuc2NpZW5jZWRpcmVjdC5jb20vc2NpZW5jZS9hcnRpY2xlL3BpaS9TMDA5Mjg2NzQyNjAwNTc5OT92aWElM0RpaHVi ), and it begins with the below paragraph:
​
“Anyone who speaks more than one language knows the feeling of expressing the same thought through entirely different linguistic lenses. A new study by researchers at Rice University and Baylor College of Medicine reveals that the key to this translation ability is a shared geometric map of neural responses in the hippocampus.”
​
It also discusses how neuroscience and AI may be converging on similar conclusions or underlying principles. It’s exciting stuff. That said, the research was based on only four speakers of English and Spanish. I love the exploration, and there are lots of open questions to ponder:
​
- Does the conceptual map somehow exist independent of language, or is it created with the learning of your first language? I would bet on the latter.
​
- English and Spanish share Indo-European structure and Latin-alphabet orthography. Does the mapping differ if you have completely different phonetics (e.g., Chinese) or very different grammar (e.g., Japanese)? Only one or the other? Both? If so, is there a threshold for “different”?
​
- One of the study’s senior authors described the brain as “keeping languages distinct enough to avoid interference.” But that refers to the proposed neural geometry of meaning, not necessarily to every retrieval slip we make while speaking—and multilingual people do mix things up. It happens a lot.
​
Sometimes the reasons seem obvious enough: for instance, a native English speaker confuses their Spanish and Portuguese, which are incredibly similar. Other times, it’s less clear what’s going on under the hood. In my case, I have retrieval errors between Spanish and Chinese only when both proficiency levels are near equal, but not when one is lagging behind the other. This is also true if my Japanese approaches my Spanish or I’m relearning German and it gets close to another beginner-level language.
​
But here is the stranger part – it doesn’t need to be current proficiency! Any point in my life will do.
​
For instance, if I haven’t studied a language in 20+ years but had a “purple belt” in proficiency at my peak—as was the case with Chinese study ending around 2000—when a new language approaches purple belt, I accidentally recall Chinese words or phrases (e.g., proverbs) that I would not be able to recall deliberately, even with a gun against my head. I’d be curious if anyone else has experienced this bizarre phenomenon (let me know @tferriss ( https://click.fourhourmail.com/o8u50wr80xfqh62o545fvhqed0rd9uoh75d/58hvh7hgdl6kzka6h4/aHR0cHM6Ly90d2l0dGVyLmNvbS90ZmVycmlzcw== )).
​
The research is still at the top of the first inning, but I’m thrilled that people are taking swings.
