Jonathan the tortoise didn’t get his name until he was almost a century old. He was anonymous until the 1930s, when the governor of his island decided that he should henceforth be called by one of the most common English names.
By the time he received his name, Jonathan had lived more than 4,000 miles from home for half a century. In reality, his birthdate is unknown, but he was estimated to be at least 50 when he was taken from his birthplace in the Seychelles Islands in the Indian Ocean in 1882 and transported more than 4,000 miles as the crow flies — in reality, much farther, because his route took him around southern Africa — to his new home on the island of St. Helena. Why was Jonathan taken from one of the world’s most remote island chains to an even more remote island? Well, it seems that tortoises were commonly taken from their homes and spirited around the world in the Victorian era. Since they survived [ https://substack.com/redirect/cd21416b-9aa8-40e6-ad30-850808bdf665?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] well without food or drink, they made good shipboard pets and were often given as diplomatic gifts. And if things went really badly at sea, they were a meal of last resort.
Luckily, Jonathan survived his voyage to St. Helena and continues to live at the governor’s residence there. He’s now closing in on his 200th birthday, hanging out with his companion Frederik (recent reports [ https://substack.com/redirect/75b20d29-f540-4bdf-aca5-1e137edd6bf2?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] of his death turned out to be a hoax).
Jonathan has lived through a lot of history. His purported birth year is the year that Greece became independent, the year that Charles Darwin traveled the world on the Beagle. He was born in an age without electric power, plastic, radio, photography, automobiles, and the telegraph, and now he lives in the age of AI. It’s remarkable!
But you might think that it’s too bad that Jonathan had to spend his long life in such an out-of-the-way place. St. Helena is in the middle of the South Atlantic, 1,200 miles from the African coast and more than 2,000 miles from South America. It’s more than 700 miles from the nearest island, Ascension Island to the northwest. It’s one of the most isolated inhabited locations on earth.
But history happens in even the most remote places, and St. Helena has actually had more than its share.

For about 10 million years after it rose out of the sea as an outcropping of volcanic rock, St. Helena was (perhaps blessedly) free of people. It was populated by birds, many of them flightless like the St. Helena Hoopoe, and some interesting insects like a giant species of earwig. Hundreds of species on St. Helena were found nowhere else in the world.
Then, in 1502, a Portuguese ship under the command of João de Nova, making the long trip back from the Indian Ocean, made the welcome discovery that there was an island way out in the middle of the Atlantic (some dispute the date of discovery, but we’ll go with the standard account here). The legends [ https://substack.com/redirect/a4ec121d-0dd7-4db2-9b0b-93da5d23ad0f?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] say that they made the discovery on St. Helen’s Day; hence the name.
The island was a welcome rest stop for sailors on long voyages, with fresh water and animals to eat. Subsequent ships deposited [ https://substack.com/redirect/a4ec121d-0dd7-4db2-9b0b-93da5d23ad0f?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] livestock and planted fruit trees on St. Helena so that the island would become a supply depot for ships. These efforts helped feed desperate sailors, but they wreaked havoc on the island’s ecosystem. Dozens [ https://substack.com/redirect/a4f4194c-79e6-4776-8939-d6bbfb4654fc?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] of the island’s unique species — including the hoopoe and earwig — are now extinct.
St. Helena became a bit of a hot commodity by the 1600s, as rival nations fought to make it their exclusive depot. Pirates used the island as a base for attacks on shipping coming to and from Asia, and various countries made claims of ownership. Portuguese forces sank a Dutch ship there in 1613, and the Dutch themselves claimed the island in 1633, but the British East India Company eventually made the island its own in the 1650s. British settlers arrived in 1659 and, though they lost control of the island to the Dutch briefly in 1673, established a permanent presence there.
The British held the island in 1673 with the help of an enslaved guide whose name was recorded as “Black Oliver.” Oliver’s role in the operation demonstrated how quickly slavery became a key part of St. Helena’s economy during this era. The island was under the complete control of the East India Company, not the British government, and it was run largely as a profit-making enterprise.
Though the company at first thought [ https://substack.com/redirect/a4ec121d-0dd7-4db2-9b0b-93da5d23ad0f?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] of the island as a “commonwealth of market gardeners,” it soon became a plantation economy, growing sugar for export. Sven Beckert [ https://substack.com/redirect/794da68c-6e57-45e0-968e-23b229c32581?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] writes that British ships were required to “return with nine slaves for every 500 pounds of British goods traded. Consequently, in 1722, of the 924 people recorded as living on Saint Helena, about 400 were enslaved Africans, of whom the company owned half.” The small society was fractious, too: Black Oliver and many others were killed in a violent 1685 revolt against the governor, which ended in a declaration of martial law. Over the next decade, a band of mutineers killed the governor and stole the company treasury. A planned slave revolt was discovered and thwarted.
After those difficult early days, St. Helena thrived in the 1700s. As the British Empire grew, it was visited by hundreds of ships a year and gained a reputation as a place where sailors would cut loose after weeks at sea. It also saw its share of famous visitors making their way around the empire. Edmund Halley (of comet fame) stopped there, as did Captain James Cook and Charles Darwin on the Beagle.
Its most famous visitor was, however, French. If you know anything about St. Helena, you know that it was Napoleon’s island prison between 1815 and 1821. This was after he had escaped from Elba, an island near Italy, and then escaped to recruit a new army. St. Helena was chosen as his new home because of its remoteness and the fact that volcanic cliffs limited the places where ships could land on the island.
Napoleon landed at St. Helena after a journey of over two months on a British ship. When he arrived, he expected to receive treatment befitting a former emperor; instead, he was made to stay in a small farmhouse, humiliated by the governor, and not allowed to travel freely around the island. Over time, he became increasingly isolated (his mistress got tired of island life and went back to Europe in 1819). Napoleon grew more depressed and obese. In the end, he succumbed to a painful illness — probably stomach cancer — while refusing help from British doctors.
But Napoleon was not the only visitor to arrive at St. Helena as the historical winds changed. The British had brought slavery to St. Helena, but, after 1840, the island served as a base for the British Navy’s operations against the slave trade (slavery was abolished [ https://substack.com/redirect/5917ab93-f178-446d-8039-c05b0650f7b7?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] in St. Helena itself on Christmas Day, 1818, in response to the savage whipping of a 14-year-old enslaved girl). Hundreds of slave ships were brought to St. Helena, and more than 27,000 African people [ https://substack.com/redirect/69c775e4-9917-4e7a-aed8-704a52d4f420?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] — some of the last victims of the Middle Passage — set foot on St. Helena. Their slavers ended up there, too, and were put on trial.
What happened to the enslaved people who ended up on this remote island in the middle of nowhere? They were sent to a settlement called Rupert’s Valley, where they awaited their fate. Perhaps a third of them are buried there [ https://substack.com/redirect/5917ab93-f178-446d-8039-c05b0650f7b7?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ], having died either on the journey to St. Helena or soon after arriving in the squalid refugee camp. Most of the others were sent “back” to British African colonies like Sierra Leone, even though they were usually from other parts of Africa. A few hundred of the freed people elected to stay in St. Helena.
Toward the end of the 19th century, St. Helena’s history repeated a bit. It was once again a place of imprisonment for a captured king — this time Dinuzulu KaCetshwayo of the Zulu nation, who had fought the British in South Africa. He and an entourage stayed for seven years in St. Helena, where they traveled freely and became part of the local community (Dinzulu learned the piano in his free time) before being allowed to return to Africa.
Soon after Dinzulu left St. Helena, the island became a place for the British to put other people they didn’t know what to do with. But this time, the new arrivals were white South Africans who had been taken as prisoners in the Boer War. They represented something like a third of the island’s population during their two-year stay, but were closely watched [ https://substack.com/redirect/2e80c731-667c-4944-95f8-4a345009ec9b?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] behind the barbed wire that surrounded their encampment. Despite this, some of the prisoners managed to marry local women and chose to stay on the island after the war had ended.
While the Zulu and Boer prisoners lived on St. Helena, Jonathan the tortoise was already somewhere on the island, placidly munching a leaf.
Unlike most of them, he stayed, living through the next century and change, as St. Helena became a quieter, less populated place. The British no longer ruled the seas, nor did modern ships need to stop so often on their voyages. St. Helena became a curiosity, an answer to a trivia question, rather than a node of empire.
Take another look at that picture of Jonathan at the beginning of this piece. You might not have paid much attention to the people in it the first time, but you’ll notice that they’re a rather diverse group. Perhaps some are descendants of people originally enslaved on the island, or of those who landed there after being rescued from the Middle Passage, while others may have come over with the East India Company.
Nobody knows who the four men are who stood behind Jonathan, but they reflect the complex story of this remote but surprisingly central island.
