Jonathan the tortoise didn’t get his name until he was almost a century old. He was anonymous until the 1930s, when the governor of his island decided that he should henceforth be called by one of the most common English names. By the time he received his name, Jonathan had lived more than 4,000 miles from home for half a century. In reality, his birthdate is unknown, but he was estimated to be at least 50 when he was taken from his birthplace in the Seychelles Islands in the Indian Ocean in 1882 and transported more than 4,000 miles as the crow flies — in reality, much farther, because his route took him around southern Africa — to his new home on the island of St. Helena. He was born in an age without electric power, plastic, radio, photography, automobiles, and the telegraph, and now he lives in the age of AI. It’s remarkable! But you might think that it’s too bad that Jonathan had to spend his long life in such an out-of-the-way place. St. Helena is in the middle of the South Atlantic, 1,200 miles from the African coast and more than 2,000 miles from South America. It’s more than 700 miles from the nearest island, Ascension Island to the northwest. It’s one of the most isolated inhabited locations on earth. But history happens in even the most remote places, and St. Helena has actually had more than its share. For about 10 million years after it rose out of the sea as an outcropping of volcanic rock, St. Helena was (perhaps blessedly) free of people. It was populated by birds, many of them flightless like the St. Helena Hoopoe, and some interesting insects like a giant species of earwig. Hundreds of species on St. Helena were found nowhere else in the world. Then, in 1502, a Portuguese ship under the command of João de Nova, making the long trip back from the Indian Ocean, made the welcome discovery that there was an island way out in the middle of the Atlantic (some dispute the date of discovery, but we’ll go with the standard account here). Subsequent ships St. Helena became a bit of a hot commodity by the 1600s, as rival nations fought to make it their exclusive depot. Pirates used the island as a base for attacks on shipping coming to and from Asia, and various countries made claims of ownership. Portuguese forces sank a Dutch ship there in 1613, and the Dutch themselves claimed the island in 1633, but the British East India Company eventually made the island its own in the 1650s. British settlers arrived in 1659 and, though they lost control of the island to the Dutch briefly in 1673, established a permanent presence there. The British held the island in 1673 with the help of an enslaved guide whose name was recorded as “Black Oliver.” Oliver’s role in the operation demonstrated how quickly slavery became a key part of St. Helena’s economy during this era. The island was under the complete control of the East India Company, not the British government, and it was run largely as a profit-making enterprise. As the British Empire grew, it was visited by hundreds of ships a year and gained a reputation as a place where sailors would cut loose after weeks at sea. It also saw its share of famous visitors making their way around the empire. Edmund Halley (of comet fame) stopped there, as did Captain James Cook and Charles Darwin on the Beagle. Its most famous visitor was, however, French. If you know anything about St. Helena, you know that it was Napoleon’s island prison between 1815 and 1821. This was after he had escaped from Elba, an island near Italy, and then escaped to recruit a new army. St. Helena was chosen as his new home because of its remoteness and the fact that volcanic cliffs limited the places where ships could land on the island. Napoleon landed at St. Helena after a journey of over two months on a British ship. When he arrived, he expected to receive treatment befitting a former emperor; instead, he was made to stay in a small farmhouse, humiliated by the governor, and not allowed to travel freely around the island. Over time, he became increasingly isolated (his mistress got tired of island life and went back to Europe in 1819). Napoleon grew more depressed and obese. In the end, he succumbed to a painful illness — probably stomach cancer — while refusing help from British doctors. But Napoleon was not the only visitor to arrive at St. Helena as the historical winds changed. It was once again a place of imprisonment for a captured king — this time Dinuzulu KaCetshwayo of the Zulu nation, who had fought the British in South Africa. He and an entourage stayed for seven years in St. Helena, where they traveled freely and became part of the local community (Dinzulu learned the piano in his free time) before being allowed to return to Africa. Soon after Dinzulu left St. Helena, the island became a place for the British to put other people they didn’t know what to do with. But this time, the new arrivals were white South Africans who had been taken as prisoners in the Boer War. Unlike most of them, he stayed, living through the next century and change, as St. Helena became a quieter, less populated place. The British no longer ruled the seas, nor did modern ships need to stop so often on their voyages. St. Helena became a curiosity, an answer to a trivia question, rather than a node of empire. Take another look at that picture of Jonathan at the beginning of this piece. You might not have paid much attention to the people in it the first time, but you’ll notice that they’re a rather diverse group. Perhaps some are descendants of people originally enslaved on the island, or of those who landed there after being rescued from the Middle Passage, while others may have come over with the East India Company. Nobody knows who the four men are who stood behind Jonathan, but they reflect the complex story of this remote but surprisingly central island.
