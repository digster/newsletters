New Men and New Minds

How the discovery of the Neanderthal changed how people thought about themselves — and foreshadowed modern debates

George Dillard
Oct 28
 
READ IN APP
 

The Prussian economy was booming in the middle of the 1800s, and the builders in Berlin needed more limestone. They found it in a valley near Düsseldorf, where businessmen like Wilhelm Beckershoff and Friedrich Wilhelm Pieper set up a quarry.

The men directed their workers to hack away at the walls of the Neander Valley, separating the stone from the clay and other elements that had been deposited by the Düssel River over the millennia. The valley had been named after Joachim Neander, a Calvinist hymn writer. Neander means “New Man,” a Hellenized version of the German Neumann.

As workers dug into a cave on the side of the valley, they found abundant clay deposits dotted with bones. The men threw the remains down almost 65 feet onto the valley floor, but somebody thought that they might be the remains of an ancient cave bear, so they gathered some of the bones and summoned a local teacher named Johann Fuhlrott to take a look. What Fuhlrott saw would change the way people understood their origins — and themselves. But not quite yet.

As you have probably gathered, Fuhlrott was looking at the bones of a new man — a Neanderthal, an ancient human who had lived in the region 40,000 years before the 1856 discovery.

Fuhlrott quickly realized that the remains — a skullcap, half a pelvis, some ribs, and a few other fragments — were human, and he could see from the distinctive brow shape on the skull that this human would not have looked very much like him. However, Charles Darwin had not yet published his On the Origin of Species, which established the theories of natural selection and evolution. The idea that ancient humans, quite different from us, had existed long ago and then disappeared from the Earth was not an easy one to process, but ideas about evolution and extinction were beginning to filter through the scientific community. He didn’t know what he had, but the framework was in place for him to figure it out.

In fact, Fuhlrott’s discovery was mostly notable because other Neanderthal remains had been misunderstood due to the fact that people had no framework for understanding them. A Belgian scientist had found a Neanderthal child’s skeleton in a cave in 1829, but had chalked it up as the “Ethiopian type.” It was not recognized as Neanderthal until the 1930s. Another skull had been discovered in 1848 in Gibraltar, but its finders had simply thought of it as a very ancient human and filed it away in a museum collection.

This meant that Fuhlrott’s find — not the first specimen of this ancient species to be found, but the first to be found at a time when people had the language to understand what it was — would give its name to the species. These people would be known as Neanderthals, not Engissians or Gibraltans, although it would take a while.

Whose bones had been found in that cave above the Neander Valley?

Fuhlrott took the remains to a local anatomy professor, Hermann Schaaffhausen. The two men came to some prescient but controversial conclusions: that the strange shape of the skull was not the result of some kind of intentional deformation; that it predated the Germanic and Celtic settlement of the area; and was in fact from the time of the last ice age more than 10,000 years ago. They concluded that they were in possession of the bones of an unknown type of early human.

The German scientists’ findings had spread widely by 1861, and their discoveries, arriving at roughly the same time as Darwin’s, captivated much of the scientific world. Could there really have been other humans who looked and behaved differently from us and went extinct long ago? And, if so, what had those people’s minds been like?

Some experts tried to fit the find into their pre-existing worldview. Since it was impossible to imagine other types of humans walking the earth, some people speculated about the ways in which a “normal” human could have ended up looking like the Neanderthal. One prominent doctor, Rudolf Virchow, argued that this skeleton with an unusual bone structure belonged to a “Mongolian Cossack” who had suffered from arthritis or rickets, spent a life on horseback, and suffered a number of blows to the skull, only to die in a German cave after a battle in the Napoleonic Wars. His study of the remains — motivated by his dislike for the theory of natural selection — was detailed, exhaustive, and dead wrong. Others came up with less florid explanations, dismissing the bones as those of someone suffering from birth defects.

The idea that there had been different types of humans was not exactly unknown at the time, even before Darwin’s ideas and the Neanderthal fossils became widely known*.* Most of the previous speculations in this area, however, focused on ranking groups of people according to their intelligence, usually in a racist way. For example, a Frenchman named Jean-Baptiste-Geneviève-Marcellin Bory de Saint-Vincent had developed an elaborate system of human classification in 1827, proposing 15 human species, each broken into “races.” It will not surprise you to find that darker-skinned people were, in his eyes, variously “stupid,” “savage,” and “warlike.”

Many observers saw the Neanderthal in this framework: it was an inferior being, perhaps from another part of the world. The geologist William King — who finally named the species Homo neanderthalensis in 1864 — declared that it looked “eminently simial… I feel myself constrained to believe that the thoughts and desires which once dwelt within it never soared beyond those of a brute.”

As Darwinian ideas permeated the global consciousness, the Neanderthal seemed to many like a “missing link.” If people were descended from apes, perhaps here was the species that connected the two. The growing conviction that there had been other species of humans in the past further opened the door to the possibility that contemporary humans might belong to different species, each occupying its own place on the evolutionary ladder. In short, the idea of the Neanderthal became a weapon for racists, who insisted that colonized peoples, like Neanderthals, must have been biologically inferior to white people.

It wasn’t until the middle of the 20th century, when archaeologists found a Neanderthal burial littered with flower pollen, that attitudes started to change. Researchers concluded that Neanderthals had used the site, in northern Iraq, for elaborate funerals in which they laid flowers at the graves of their dead. This find — along with other, later research, like the discovery that many of us have Neanderthal DNA — caused a widespread reconsideration of Neanderthals. No longer were they savage, unthinking brutes. They were, perhaps, quite like us.

A few years ago, researchers tore a hole in the flower burial story, arguing that the pollen was much more likely to have been deposited by bees or rodents than by humans. Maybe the people who found that burial in the 1960s had wanted to believe that the Neanderthals had minds more like ours than they really were.

Even as they got credit for busting that particular myth, Neanderthal scholars were careful to insist that the site still showed signs of cultural traditions (people were buried in the cave over several generations) and care for the dead. I found it interesting how important this seemed in the framing of the story. There was a palpable desire to avoid completely undermining the narrative that had emerged about Neanderthals, lest they bring back stories of “brutes” and “idiots” that used to characterize Neanderthal scholarship.

It seems that, much of the time, modern speculation about Neanderthals has reflected the values of the people writing the scholarship. Nineteenth-century writing on Neanderthals focused on their inferiority and looked for parallels between Neanderthals and non-white people. Neanderthals helped to justify the oppression of people around the world.

Then, more recently, we heard very different stories about Neanderthals as people who, though very different from Homo sapiens, lived alongside us, interacted with us, and even reproduced with us.

Along the way, there was an implicit set of questions: where do you draw the line between people and non-people? Which minds are really thinking and therefore worthy of moral status, and which minds aren’t? And what do the answers to these questions say about us?

Now, some technologists believe that they are effectively creating a new man. Others say that these beliefs are deluded. There’s a new set of arguments about what constitutes a mind. These debates, like those around the Neanderthals, will probably tell us more about ourselves than they do about the “minds” of these “new men.”
