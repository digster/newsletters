The setting of this showdown — in a building that had recently hosted mid-level college basketball games — may have seemed undignified, but Gromyko’s walkout and the issue over which he walked out were critically important, both for the world in 1946 and the world we live in today. The dispute over Iran was the first major test for the United Nations. It helped to shape the trajectory of the Cold War. And it helps to answer a set of questions you may have asked recently: How did Iran become a major preoccupation of American foreign policy, and how did Iran come to view the United States as its main geopolitical enemy? In many ways, the dispute at the UN was the continuation of a long colonial struggle over Iran, but there was a new set of issues emerging, as well. As Persia (the country’s official name before 1935) weakened in the 19th century, Britain and Russia had encroached on it. When, in 1940, Reza began to exhibit sympathy with the German cause in World War II, the British and Soviets decided that he needed to go. Both countries, along with the Americans, invaded Iran in August 1941 to prevent the Germans from gaining access to Iranian oil reserves. They replaced Reza with his son, the more pliable Mohammad Reza. Iran became a major corridor for the Lend-Lease trade between the United States and the Soviet Union, and Iranian oil helped keep Allied navies afloat. Though the Allies insisted that the invasion was not an occupation and that Iran was their ally, it took a terrible toll. The Allied takeover of Iran’s resources and transportation networks unleashed economic chaos in the country. The Americans and British left on schedule, but the Soviets did not. Instead, they used their influence to prompt local leaders in northern Iran to declare “independent” communist states, the Azerbaijan People’s Government and the Kurdish Republic of Mahabad. In both cases, the Soviets presented the movement as a simple nationalist movement for independence, but the Americans saw an attempt to establish Soviet puppet states in an oil-rich region. The showdown over Iran took place before the Iron Curtain Speech, containment, the Berlin Airlift, and most of the other events we usually associate with the beginning of the Cold War. The United States defended the Iranian government against Soviet attempts to undermine it. They called Stalin’s bluff. American diplomats shepherded Security Council Resolution 2, urging the USSR to resolve the crisis and leave Iran, and Resolution 3, which called out the Soviets for stalling their withdrawal, through the council. The Soviets did withdraw, partially because Iranian Prime Minister Ahmad Qavam promised oil concessions to the Soviets in exchange for a withdrawal (he secretly planned to renege on the deal as soon as Soviet troops were out of the country). In the end, the United States and Iran teamed up to push the Soviets out of Iran. How could this have helped create the long history of tension between America and Iran? Here’s the answer: the United States and Iran understood the incident very differently. To the Iranians, the crisis was all about defending Iranian sovereignty. They got involved, making high-handed promises to the Iranian people about their rights. Then they gave Iran a front-row seat to a demonstration of American hypocrisy. The Iran Crisis was the beginning of the two countries’ long, intense relationship with one another. For the first few decades, the Americans were Iran’s key ally, but the Americans were transparently interested in their own strategic needs over those of Iran. Then the Iranian people threw off their American-sponsored leader and empowered a government that, to this day, has found political benefit in making the United States out to be the “Great Satan.” Neither country knew it in those confusing months in early 1946, but the Iran Crisis would be the beginning of a strange and ugly 80-year entanglement.
