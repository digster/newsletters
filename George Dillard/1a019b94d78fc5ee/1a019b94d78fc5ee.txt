Eighty years ago, the seeds of tension between the United States and Iran were planted in an outer borough of New York City.
No, I’m not talking about the birth of Donald John Trump in Queens (although that happened in 1946, too). The event I’m thinking of took place across town, in the gymnasium of what’s now Lehman College in the Bronx (back then it was the Bronx campus of Hunter College).
Before the UN headquarters was finished in 1952, the young organization met in several temporary locations. The very first meeting of the Security Council, for example, was at the headquarters of the Church of England in London. It also met for a few years in the headquarters of the Sperry Gyroscope Company [ https://substack.com/redirect/2433ed65-f243-49cd-82c5-b719c2663681?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] on Long Island. And, in between, it convened in that converted gym in the Bronx.
It was here that the United Nations, only a few months old, took on one of the most controversial geopolitical issues of the moment: the Soviet presence in Iran. The Soviet ambassador, Andrei Gromyko, claimed [ https://substack.com/redirect/c6ce22d7-23a5-4069-84ac-2b4ae9b3e860?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] that the Soviets were welcome in Iran. When a Dutch delegate pushed him on the issue, he snapped, “What is there here that isn’t clear?”
The meeting did not go well for Gromyko. The Time [ https://substack.com/redirect/c6ce22d7-23a5-4069-84ac-2b4ae9b3e860?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] magazine account [ https://substack.com/redirect/c6ce22d7-23a5-4069-84ac-2b4ae9b3e860?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] tells us how the meeting ended in dramatic fashion:
When the debate made it clear that only Poland’s Oskar Lange agreed with him, and that Iran’s Hussein Ala would be heard in spite of the Russian objection, Gromyko announced: “I am not able to participate further in the discussions . . . and I therefore leave the meeting.” He waited for the French and English translations of his statement before rising. Then he pushed back his chair, straightened his black double-breasted coat and walked briskly toward the far door, followed by his three advisers. His normally pale face was flushed.
It was the first (but not the last) time Soviet delegates stormed out of the UN. The setting of this showdown — in a building that had recently hosted mid-level college basketball games — may have seemed undignified, but Gromyko’s walkout and the issue over which he walked out were critically important, both for the world in 1946 and the world we live in today.
The dispute over Iran was the first major test for the United Nations. It helped to shape the trajectory of the Cold War. And it helps to answer a set of questions you may have asked recently: How did Iran become a major preoccupation of American foreign policy, and how did Iran come to view the United States as its main geopolitical enemy?
In many ways, the dispute at the UN was the continuation of a long colonial struggle over Iran, but there was a new set of issues emerging, as well.
As Persia (the country’s official name before 1935) weakened in the 19th century, Britain and Russia had encroached on it. To the imperialists, it was all part of the “Great Game [ https://substack.com/redirect/6bfc42b9-93be-4162-90d5-d8fa91cff4cc?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ],” in which they vied for influence in the lands between British India and the southern parts of the Russian Empire. To the people who lived there, the period was a parade of humiliations and deprivations as their country’s resources were sold off for peanuts to foreigners and their land was divided up into zones of influence: the southeast was controlled by the British, and the northern areas were under Russian sway, with a “neutral” area in between.
In 1921, the British helped an army officer named Reza Khan overthrow the royal family and establish himself as king, or shah. When, in 1940, Reza began to exhibit sympathy with the German cause in World War II, the British and Soviets decided that he needed to go.
Both countries, along with the Americans, invaded Iran in August 1941 to prevent the Germans from gaining access to Iranian oil reserves. They replaced Reza with his son, the more pliable Mohammad Reza. Iran became a major corridor for the Lend-Lease trade between the United States and the Soviet Union, and Iranian oil helped keep Allied navies afloat.
Though the Allies insisted that the invasion was not an occupation and that Iran was their ally, it took a terrible toll. The Allied takeover of Iran’s resources and transportation networks unleashed economic chaos in the country. Inflation rose into the triple digits, and a terrible famine [ https://substack.com/redirect/41d8728f-803d-4082-906b-2a6e88694db0?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] in 1942–43 killed hundreds of thousands of people.
So, by the end of World War II, Iran had endured decades of humiliation and suffering, and its leaders had only intermittently exercised authority inside their own borders. International powers, especially the USSR and Britain, saw Iran as an important asset to be controlled (Winston Churchill had once called [ https://substack.com/redirect/9f32d494-73d9-4604-add8-7fac9ba322a6?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] Iranian oil “a prize from fairyland far beyond our brightest hopes”).
The Allies had agreed in 1942 to leave Iran within six months of the end of the war. The Americans and British left on schedule, but the Soviets did not. Instead, they used their influence to prompt local leaders in northern Iran to declare “independent” communist states, the Azerbaijan People’s Government and the Kurdish Republic of Mahabad. In both cases, the Soviets presented the movement as a simple nationalist movement for independence, but the Americans saw an attempt to establish Soviet puppet states in an oil-rich region.
The showdown over Iran took place before the Iron Curtain Speech, containment, the Berlin Airlift, and most of the other events we usually associate with the beginning of the Cold War. The Iran Crisis took place in the first few months of 1946, against a backdrop of worsening tensions. Stalin gave an angry speech [ https://substack.com/redirect/6b5f5c64-4bf9-4d28-820d-791c2b5ef23b?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] blaming the capitalist countries for World War II, George Kennan sent his “Long Telegram [ https://substack.com/redirect/e425c722-dd64-4462-bf4f-4af7727b7996?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ]” proposing a hostile stance toward the USSR, and Churchill gave his “Iron Curtain [ https://substack.com/redirect/6c199a3c-42af-42ac-9859-0a095875c127?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ]” speech.
When the Iranian government brought its complaint before the UN Security Council, it was the first matter the body ever took up.
The United States defended the Iranian government against Soviet attempts to undermine it. They called Stalin’s bluff. American diplomats shepherded Security Council Resolution 2, urging the USSR to resolve the crisis and leave Iran, and Resolution 3, which called out the Soviets for stalling their withdrawal, through the council. The Soviets did withdraw, partially because Iranian Prime Minister Ahmad Qavam promised oil concessions to the Soviets in exchange for a withdrawal (he secretly planned to renege on the deal as soon as Soviet troops were out of the country).
In the end, the United States and Iran teamed up to push the Soviets out of Iran. How could this have helped create the long history of tension between America and Iran?
Here’s the answer: the United States and Iran understood the incident very differently.
To the Iranians, the crisis was all about defending Iranian sovereignty. The major powers had pledged [ https://substack.com/redirect/26df46c5-e1f0-4f43-ac87-41d90ef45430?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] in 1943 to maintain the “independence, sovereignty, and territorial integrity of Iran,” and the Americans appeared to be behaving with integrity by adhering to those principles. The incident left many in Iran with very positive perceptions of the United States as a nation devoted to its principles.
But to the Americans, this was not primarily about protecting Iranian sovereignty. It was about countering the Soviets in a global game. George Kennan, in his Long Telegram, had flagged [ https://substack.com/redirect/e425c722-dd64-4462-bf4f-4af7727b7996?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] Iran as a possible flashpoint for the emerging Cold War. To him and his superiors in Washington, Iran was most important as a battlefield against communism.
The moment the Iranian government stopped being an asset against the Soviet Union, the Americans were happy to violate its sovereignty. That moment came only seven years later, when the United States and Great Britain overthrew the Iranian government in a coup after the nationalization of Iran’s oil.
What happened in 1953 was more than a coup; it was to many Iranians a betrayal [ https://substack.com/redirect/d3a66564-6af1-4c4e-944b-b37bb35c335d?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ]. Wasn’t the United States the champion of Iranian independence? Even worse, the Americans helped Mohammad Reza Shah become an absolute ruler whose secret police arrested and tortured dissidents. Instead of being a champion of Iranian sovereignty, the Americans turned out to be just another colonial meddler. Their behavior in 1946 had raised Iranian hopes, which just made their dashing all the more painful.
In the 1940s and 1950s, the Americans, who had never shown much interest in Iran before, locked on to the country as a Cold War flashpoint. They got involved, making high-handed promises to the Iranian people about their rights. Then they gave Iran a front-row seat to a demonstration of American hypocrisy.
The Iran Crisis was the beginning of the two countries’ long, intense relationship with one another. For the first few decades, the Americans were Iran’s key ally, but the Americans were transparently interested in their own strategic needs over those of Iran. Then the Iranian people threw off their American-sponsored leader and empowered a government that, to this day, has found political benefit in making the United States out to be the “Great Satan.”
Neither country knew it in those confusing months in early 1946, but the Iran Crisis would be the beginning of a strange and ugly 80-year entanglement.
