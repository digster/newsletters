That’s what a brother and sister named Phrixus and Helle did when they were threatened with becoming human sacrifices. But things did not go according to plan. First, Helle fell off the back of the ram as they passed between Europe and Asia. She died. This painting from Pompeii shows Helle sinking into the waters, just out of her brother’s grasp: Then Phrixus landed in his destination, met the local king and ungratefully sacrificed the poor ram in thanksgiving to the gods. The skin of the ram became the famous golden fleece, which Jason and the Argonauts pursued tirelessly. The place where Helle fell — the narrow body of water between Europe and Asia, between the Sea of Marmara and the Aegean Sea — was named the Hellespont. At other points in history, this thin body of water has had other names. It’s been called the Dardanelles, after the ancient city of Dardanus, which sat on its western shore. Today, Turks call it Çanakkale Strait, after a fortress that the Ottomans built there in 1462. Whatever it’s been called, this body of water has long been one of the most important water passages in the world. For a stretch of almost 40 miles, the water passing between the Black Sea and the Aegean moves through this narrow passage only about a kilometer wide, lined by cliffs. You can see the treacherous nature of the strait in this 1788 map: And its position at the northeastern end of the Aegean in this lovely map from 1654: The Dardanelles’ geographical circumstances mean that passage through it is quite hazardous. The fact that these circumstances allow humans to attack ships from the shore over such a long distance means that control of the Dardanelles has long been a great strategic prize. In fact, one of the very first wars we know about was probably over control of the Dardanelles. The city of Troy overlooked the Dardanelles from a place that the Turks later called Hisarlik, “the place of fortresses.” Homer’s stories tell us that a dispute over a woman caused the Trojan War, but it’s more likely that Greek armies were tired of paying tolls to the Trojans, who, in the words of archaeologist Manfred Korfmann, operated a “ Ancient leaders obsessed over the Dardanelles, and the strait decided many wars. The Persian emperor Xerxes, whose father Darius had ordered a bridge built over the Bosporus, commissioned his own bridge over the Dardanelles. This one didn’t go as well.
Eventually, Xerxes’ army built a working bridge that allowed his huge invading army to march on Greece. While Herodotus’s story is likely designed to make Xerxes seem erratic, emotional, and therefore un-Greek, it highlights the strategic significance of the Dardanelles in the ancient world. The story of his audacious attempt to bridge the Hellespont would become one of the best-known bits of lore about Xerxes. In this Dutch book from the 1640s, he and Darius are seen with the bridge in the background: And in this French manuscript from the 1400s, Xerxes’ army crosses the bridge into Europe: The Dardanelles featured heavily in later Greek history, too. The decisive Battle of Aegospotami, which decided the Peloponnesian Wars in 405 BCE, took place over control of the strait. The Spartans wanted to cut off the trade routes that sustained the Athenian economy, and what better place was there to do this than the Dardanelles? The Athenian fleet could not afford to lose the strait; it challenged the Spartans there and was destroyed, sealing Athens’ fate. Seventy years later, Macedonian forces under the control of Philip II tried to take control of towns along the strait. The Persians retaliated, beginning a conflict that Philip’s son Alexander would settle decisively. When Alexander victoriously crossed the Dardanelles with his army into Asia, he threw a spear into the Asian soil as a way of claiming his new territory. He then visited Troy so that he could walk where the great heroes of old — who, as we’ve seen, may have merely been a bunch of pirates and raiders — had fought. Then again, maybe Alexander was just a supremely skilled raider himself. After Alexander, the Dardanelles became a fortified node in a series of larger empires. First the Romans, then the Byzantines, ruled over the strait. As the Ottomans worked to establish their first foothold in Europe, an earthquake leveled pretty much every building in Gallipoli, the long peninsula on the northern side of the strait. Ottoman forces quickly moved in and seized the area, giving them a foothold that they would use to expand into Europe and eventually conquer Constantinople. In the Ottoman era, the Dardanelles became the fortified gauntlet that ships would have to run in order to approach the capital at Istanbul. Ottoman mastery of gunpowder artillery made this one of the most tightly controlled waterways in the world. Thus, the Ottomans’ enemies tended to fight them elsewhere: for the Venetians in the 1500s or the Habsburgs in the 1600s, it would have been suicidal to sail a fleet up the Dardanelles. In fact, there was a lot of talk about the “Straits Question” — would the Russians be allowed to use the Dardanelles (and Bosporus), and, if so, would Russia’s European enemies (England and France, mostly) be allowed to use the straits to attack them? There were lots of negotiations and several treaties, which were followed by the signatories (unless it was inconvenient, in which case they ignored them). Ottoman control over the Dardanelles was directly challenged for the first time in 1915, when Allied forces attacked the Gallipoli Peninsula during the First World War. The thinking was that the Ottomans would crumble if they couldn’t control the Dardanelles. The British and French navies tried to force their way up the straits. Though the Ottoman Empire fell, it was fitting that its successor, the nation of Turkey, would be headed by the hero of the Dardanelles campaign, Mustafa Kemal (later known as Atatürk). One last story about the Dardanelles. There’s a Greek myth about a man named Leander who swam across the strait night after night to woo a woman named Hero. She was a priestess and a virgin. He cleverly argued that Aphrodite, the goddess to whom she had devoted her life, would want her to have some sex. So the couple consummated their affair — again and again for an entire summer. When winter came, Leander could no longer make the swim, but he gazed longingly across the strait. One night, he could take it no more. He tried to make the swim, guided by the light from Hero’s tower. A storm blew out her torch; he got lost and drowned. Distraught, Hero leapt into the strait to be with Leander in death. Lord Byron decided that this story made swimming across the Dardanelles seem like a good idea, so he made the swim in 1810. The stunt bolstered his reputation as a dreamy romantic hero. He was incredibly proud of having completed the swim.
