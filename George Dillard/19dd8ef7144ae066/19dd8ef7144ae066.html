Ancient Robots, Modern Fears

What Greek myths can tell us about AI

George Dillard
Apr 29
 
READ IN APP
 

Melania Trump mostly stays out of the spotlight. When not appearing in documentaries for which she is suspiciously well-compensated, she skips a lot of public appearances at which one might expect to see the First Lady. But when she does grace the public stage, Melania often makes it weird. There were the Christmas decorations that looked like they were from a Kubrick movie. There was the “I don’t really care, do u?” jacket, which she wore to visit a children’s shelter, of all places.

And there was, recently, the spectacle of her appearance with a humanoid education robot, which addressed the crowd as she stood, well, robotically behind it.

The First Lady seems to think that robots will be the future of education, serving as intelligent home tutors who have more energy and knowledge than human teachers like me. I hope (and believe) she’s wrong that robots and AI can replace the human aspect of education, but this event represented a core belief of at least some people in the tech industry and the wider society: that artificial intelligence will be a benign force, serving humanity in useful ways.

Melania Trump is not the first person to envision that artificial people in some form would be helpful servants. In fact, we’ve been imagining such a thing for almost 3,000 years.

Homer casually tosses a similar vision into the Iliad. When Thetis, Achilles’ mother, visits Hephaestus to try to get him to make her son new armor, Homer describes the god of metalwork and fire this way:

Grasping a thick staff he limped from the forge, supported by servants made of gold, fashioned like living girls, who attended swiftly on their master. As well as the use of their limbs they had intellect, and the immortals gave them skill in subtle crafts. They supported Hephaestus as he limped towards Thetis, and seated himself on a gleaming chair. Then he took her hand and spoke to her: ‘Thetis of the long robe, honoured and welcome, though unaccustomed, guest, what brings you here? Say what you need, and my heart prompts me to fulfil it, if it can be done, and I can do it.’

Hephaestus’ maidens are such a fun, unnecessary detail. In a poem of over 15,000 lines, there was really no reason to squeeze in this little tidbit about artificial servant girls, but there it is. In the midst of his tale of warfare and grief and rage, Homer imagines autonomous maidens who can think, move on their own, and seem happy to serve their master. It’s not that far from the dreams of the AI boosters these days.

The First Lady isn’t the only AI enthusiast in the current government. The U.S. military also seems quite keen to use artificial intelligence for warfare. AI is apparently sifting through data to make targeting recommendations to the Air Force and running simulations to predict possible future scenarios.

It’s not hard to imagine a future in which autonomous drones and fighting robots go to war with one another — or malfunction in comical or devastating ways.

The ancient Greeks imagined this, too. Talos — who classicist Adrienne Meyer calls the “first ‘robot’ to walk the earth” — was a giant bronze man created by Hephaestus and given to King Minos of Crete. The “animated statue” walked around the shores of the island several times a day. It could identify enemy ships at sea and invaders who came ashore. Talos could break off pieces of the cliffs to throw large rocks at the ships. The unlucky soldiers who fought him experienced his superpower: Talos’s body could get incredibly hot, and the metal man would embrace his opponents until they burned to death.

Like so many fictional terrors, Talos had a convenient weakness: his circulatory system consisted of one vein filled with ichor (the fluid that flowed in the veins of the gods). When Talos faces off against Jason, his Argonauts, and the sorceress Medea, he reaches down for a rock to throw but punctures his vein at the ankle (in some versions, the Argonauts puncture the vein). He crumples to the ground, deprived of his life force.

A lot of people — including some of those who are helping to create these systems — think AI is going to bring about the apocalypse. AI researchers trade their estimates of P(doom), the probability that AI will end life as we know it. AI skeptics tell reporters that they’re not bothering to save for retirement because they think that new technology will wipe out human society before they reach old age. There’s a book called If Anyone Builds It, Everyone Dies.

Will AI be like Skynet in the Terminator films, a malevolent force that tries to eliminate humans? Or will it just create massive unemployment and social dysfunction?

Sounds like we’re opening a real Pandora’s Box with AI, huh?

There are two things that a lot of people don’t realize about the Pandora story. First, Pandora’s “box” was really a jar (a medieval scribe messed up the text, and we’ve been calling it a box ever since). Second, Pandora was an artificial life form created by Zeus to torture the human race in revenge for Prometheus’ theft of the secret of fire. All of the gods pitched in, making Pandora a convincing replica of a real woman. Zeus said:

“I will give men as the price for fire an evil thing in which they may all be glad of heart while they embrace their own destruction.”

So said the father of men and gods, and laughed aloud. And he bade famous Hephaestus make haste and mix earth with water and to put in it the voice and strength of human kind, and fashion a sweet, lovely maiden-shape, like to the immortal goddesses in face; and Athene to teach her needlework and the weaving of the varied web; and golden Aphrodite to shed grace upon her head and cruel longing and cares that weary the limbs. And he charged Hermes the guide, the Slayer of Argus, to put in her a shameless mind and a deceitful nature.

Prometheus’s brother, Epimetheus, accepts the gift even though he knows better. Soon Pandora reveals her secrets, bringing death and destruction on mankind, unleashing everything but hope on people:

For ere this the tribes of men lived on earth remote and free from ills and hard toil and heavy sickness which bring the Fates upon men; for in misery men grow old quickly. But the woman took off the great lid of the jar with her hands and scattered all these and her thought caused sorrow and mischief to men. Only Hope remained there in an unbreakable home within under the rim of the great jar, and did not fly out at the door; for ere that, the lid of the jar stopped her, by the will of Aegis-holding Zeus who gathers the clouds. But the rest, countless plagues, wander amongst men; for earth is full of evils and the sea is full. Of themselves diseases come upon men continually by day and by night, bringing mischief to mortals silently; for wise Zeus took away speech from them. So is there no way to escape the will of Zeus.

I find it fascinating that the ancient Greeks imagined all sorts of automata and robots even though they were far from being technologically capable of producing them. It’s even more fascinating that their tales anticipated many of the concerns we have with our own humanlike technologies today.

Other societies in history indulged in similar feats of imagination. The ancient Chinese imagined an artificial man so lifelike that people couldn’t tell the difference. Ancient Buddhists told stories of humanlike machines that guarded sacred texts. Medieval Muslims and Christians spoke of humanoid automata that could answer any question or play music. It seems that people have been thinking about what it would mean to create convincing facsimiles of ourselves for thousands of years.

Many of these tales contain a mixture of wonder and worry, not unlike what we’re experiencing today, as we stand on the cusp of actually producing the machines that our ancestors dreamed of for so long. Will they be cheerful servants like Hephaestus’ maidens? Bringers of violence like Talos? Or will they unleash chaos on the world like Pandora? Unlike the ancient people who came up with these myths, we may actually find out.
