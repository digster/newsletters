As so often happened with noblewomen in the ancient world, she had been married out to improve the political status of her father. She’d been the wife of King Lysimachus of Thrace, who was 45 years her senior, and then, when he died, she married her half-brother Ptolemy Keraunos. She turned on Ptolemy Keraunos (who in revenge killed two of her sons) and fled to Egypt. Once in Egypt, she engineered the ouster of Arsinoë I, her brother’s first wife (who was not her blood relative, but was a child of her first husband Lysimachus and the woman he had married before Arsinoë II… confused yet?). The Ptolemaic pharaohs were Greek — Ptolemy I was one of Alexander the Great’s generals, and after Alexander died, he took control of the Egyptian portion of the empire. They had to impress two audiences: the Greek elites in Alexandria (and the wider Mediterranean world) and the Egyptian people that constituted the bulk of their subjects. Hence, they appeared in traditional Egyptian portraits along with the more Greek-looking ones above. One of the things that Egyptian pharaohs sometimes did was marry their siblings. So the Ptolemies, eager to convince Egyptians that they were legitimate rulers, entered into all kinds of incestuous relationships. Historian Ager argues that marrying your sister had several other purposes: it kept all power inside the family, prevented princesses from having babies with anyone but the king, and demonstrated that the pharaohs were set apart from ordinary people. The pharaohs didn’t have to follow the same rules as everyone else, behaving instead like gods (siblings Isis and Osiris slept with one another). The result: over time, the Ptolemaic pharaohs became “increasingly inbred.” The whole sister-marriage thing was just one part of what makes Ptolemaic Egypt so interesting. To the Romans and Greeks, the society was simultaneously familiar and strange, a Greek layer on top of a very old society that they found intimidating and mysterious. In some ways, it was the site of a culture clash. In other ways, it was a graceful merger. And sometimes, as in the case of the Ptolemies’ incest, it was all a little clumsy and strange. Soon after the Ptolemies took power in Egypt, this guy with the strange hat started to appear: He was Serapis, a god that may not have been introduced by Egypt’s new Greek overlords, but certainly gained popularity under them. Unlike many Egyptian gods, who were shown with animal heads, he was fully anthropomorphic, in the style of the Greek gods. But he had the characteristics of several popular Egyptian gods, like Apis, a sacred bull: And Osiris, the god of fertility and the afterlife: Serapis often wore a basket for collecting food on his head, a symbol of abundance and fertility: He sometimes held a scepter like Osiris did, and a serpent to remind people of the Egyptian symbol of rulership. He looked a bit like Zeus or Hades. The Ptolemies promoted the cult of Serapis as a new hybrid form of worship that combined what they saw as the best of the two religions.
