A Catholic order formed by Teresa of Ávila and John of the Cross (who were later sainted by the Catholic Church), they seal themselves into small cloistered monasteries and live lives of quiet prayer. They’re sworn to simple poverty (“discalced” means “without shoes”). When the revolutionary government in France ordered all convents closed in 1792, you can imagine what a shock it must have been to the Carmelite nuns of Compiègne. They were abruptly pushed out of the only life many of them had ever really known into a society in tumult, where the church that had guided everything about their lives was under extreme scrutiny. The nuns of Compiègne decided not to abandon their vows as the new government had ordered them to. They no longer wore their habits, as that was illegal, but they continued to live together in communal apartments and keep up their ritual of quiet prayer. The group’s prioress, Mother Teresa, told the group to prepare to sacrifice their lives for the church. The twenty nuns lived like this for more than a year, but by 1794, an increasingly radical government had decided that loyalty to the old ways of the church was tantamount to sedition. Agents looked to snuff out any last remnants of traditional practice. They raided the nuns’ quarters and arrested them. Mother Teresa’s prediction would come true. The nuns were sent to the same prison as Marie Antoinette. Their apartments were searched for evidence that they held counterrevolutionary beliefs. The nuns were sentenced to death. They sang hymns in the cart that took them to the guillotine and reaffirmed their vows as they stood on the scaffold. One by one, they were decapitated. Sure, people had questioned the Catholic Church sometimes, seeing it as corrupt or hypocritical, but executing nuns for the crime of being nuns? That would have shocked almost anybody. But what happened to the shoeless nuns of Compiègne was just one aspect of the religious whiplash that France experienced in the 1790s. The year before the nuns were arrested and killed, there was an uprising in the Vendée, an area in western France. The people there supported the traditional arrangements in France: they wanted the restoration of the king and the church to their previous positions of supremacy. The uprising turned into an outright civil war. Vendeans formed a “Catholic Army,” a ragtag outfit that took on the republican forces. Their symbol was a sacred heart, and their slogan was “God, the king.” The makeshift Catholic Army fought hard but was no match for the regular troops. Officials arrested anyone suspected of royalist tendencies. Being a priest or a nun was enough to seal your fate. The government arrested so many people in the Vendée that it became inefficient to guillotine them. Instead, leaders started to load dozens of prisoners at a time onto special barges on the Loire River in Nantes. The barges would be floated out into the middle of the water, sunk, and then floated again once everyone had drowned. The mass drownings killed thousands of people, a disproportionate number of whom were members of the first estate. But even the most die-hard atheists in the revolutionary government understood that it wasn’t enough to kill all the priests and nuns who refused to swear an oath to the new regime. They needed something to replace religion in people’s lives. As revolutionary leaders drowned nuns in the Loire, they held a Festival of Reason at the Notre Dame Cathedral in Paris. Women dressed like Roman goddesses and people celebrated “philosophy,” that sort of thing: Nevertheless, it did allow people to gather in worship of something: For the next few months, the government attacked religion itself, attempting to dechristianize France and replace it with something much colder: respect for logic. Then, at the height of the anti-religious frenzy, Maximilien Robespierre, leader of the radical faction, reversed course. Rather than attacking the very idea of belief in God, he tried a compromise, endorsing a Cult of the Supreme Being. This would allow French people to worship a divine force, find a reason for moral order, and reconcile with the inevitability of death without all that illogical old-man-in-the-sky, Jesus-walking-on-water stuff. This idea has been floating around for a while. A 1791 illustration made an attempt to relate all Western religious and philosophical thought to the vague idea of the Supreme Being. Religious freedom was partially restored the next winter, partially as a way to get the Vendean rebels to agree to peace. But the religious innovation didn’t come to an end. Intellectuals continued to look for a way to reconcile the Enlightenment values of the revolution with their desire to practice something like a religion. They eventually settled on something called Theophilanthropy, which tried to strip all of the “superstitions” out of Catholic belief. Theophilanthropists worshipped a Supreme Being and carried out rituals like baptism, but focused more on utilitarian ethics than Bible stories. They held their rites in churches with garlands and fruits on the altar instead of a crucifix. Not everybody was on board with theophilanthropy.
