How Marie Antoinette Lost Control of Her Image

A PR disaster with world-historical consequences

I feel pretty confident that the so-called Affair of the Diamond Necklace is one of the dumbest, weirdest political scandals in history.

Louis XV, the King of France, wanted to give his mistress a nice present in 1772, so he hired a Parisian jeweler to make her the fanciest diamond necklace in the world. The jewelers set off on their task, which took several years. Unfortunately for them, Louis XV died while they were sourcing the diamonds, and the jewelers were stuck with a very expensive necklace (it cost more than $15 million in today’s money) that almost nobody could afford.

The necklace might have looked something like the reconstruction below; a necklace containing some of the original diamonds was recently sold at auction for $5 million.

The jewelers hoped to sell this glittering albatross to the next king, Louis XVI, to give to his new bride, Marie Antoinette, but she turned it down. Depending on which account you believe, she refused the necklace either because she thought it was a waste of money (“we have more need of ships than necklaces,” she supposedly said) or because she hated her father-in-law’s mistress and thought her necklace was tacky.

So the jewelers had invested in this incredibly valuable thing that nobody wanted to buy.

Luckily for them, Louis-René-Édouard de Rohan, the Cardinal of Strasbourg, came calling in 1785. The cardinal came from an illustrious noble family but had fallen out of favor both in Austria, Marie Antoinette’s homeland, where he had been the French ambassador, and in France itself. The cardinal thought he’d found a way back to relevance when his mistress (he was cardinal because of his influence, not his ethics), Jeanne la Motte, told him that he could reclaim his influence by buying the necklace.

Jeanne la Motte told the cardinal that Marie Antoinette wanted to secretly buy the necklace without Louis knowing about it. She said that, if Rohan bought the necklace, the queen would reimburse him, and he would be back in the good graces of both Marie Antoinette and her relatives in Austria.

Unfortunately, the whole thing was a scam. Jeanne la Motte was not the noblewoman she claimed to be, and she didn’t know the queen. She had another lover, a forger, who created the messages that Jeanne brought to the cardinal from “Marie Antoinette.” The scammers arranged a nighttime meeting between the cardinal and a prostitute who looked kind of like the queen. She was apparently convincing enough because the cardinal agreed to buy the necklace and gave it to La Motte, who immediately started selling off the individual diamonds in London.

The caper fell apart when the cardinal failed to make his payments and also could not return the necklace to the jewelers. When the jewelers asked Marie Antoinette to return the necklace, she had no idea what they were talking about.

The aftermath of the scandal was predictable: the cardinal, because he was a powerful man, was allowed to go into genteel exile, while Jeanne La Motte was arrested and sent to prison. That, you might think, would be the end of the story — a grubby little episode orchestrated by some deeply flawed people, but nothing terribly important. But you’d be wrong, because this scandal — that Marie Antoinette had nothing to do with, mind you — helped to turn the French public against their queen.

Many French people, already deeply dissatisfied with the monarchy, preferred florid conspiracy theories about the necklace affair to the truth. They spun tales about how Marie Antoinette had orchestrated the whole thing to destroy the cardinal and other political enemies. It didn’t help that the wily La Motte escaped, fled to London, and published a memoir in which she implausibly claimed that Marie Antoinette had been in on the scam from the beginning and had hung her co-conspirators out to dry. Even the fact that a prostitute (clothed in the type of chemise dress that Marie Antoinette had infamously worn for a portrait) had so easily fooled people into thinking that she could be the queen made Marie Antoinette seem small and tawdry.

The diamond necklace affair is the sort of thing that seems like it could only have happened to Marie Antoinette. No celebrity or leader can control what the public thinks of them, but it’s hard to think of a historical figure who so completely and catastrophically lost control of her own public image — with such significant historical consequences — as Marie Antoinette.

It’s worth keeping in mind that Marie Antoinette was only 14 years old when she was shipped off to marry the 15-year-old heir to the French throne, and only 18 when she became queen. She struggled in the same way that pop stars or actresses who become rich and famous too young can struggle to manage their private behavior, their public image, and the intersection of the two.

There are lots of ways to see Marie Antoinette. If you’re sympathetic to her, you’ll forgive her trespasses against propriety and good sense — what young person thrust into such a situation wouldn’t overspend on clothes or act visibly bored at tedious court ceremonies? If you’re not, you’ll interpret her obsession with fashion and frivolity as a symbol of the French aristocracy’s clueless self-absorption. Was she a girlboss who didn’t care what people thought? A tragic figure trapped in an impossible situation? A model of elegance? A monster? You be the judge.

Many of these images of Marie Antoinette were for elite consumption, displayed at the Paris Salon or in the halls of Versailles. Sometimes, they reinforced the images the queen wanted people to see; sometimes, they reinforced the idea that she was “Madame Deficit,” spending the country into crisis. But the general French public wasn’t seeing these images. They were getting very different versions of their queen.

Most of the popular portrayals revolved around gender and sexuality. The royal couple’s sex life was under extreme scrutiny, and it was widely understood that they did not consummate their marriage for some time. Speculation about why the king and queen weren’t having sex spawned no end of salacious rumors and innuendo, and the fact that the couple eventually had children together did nothing to stem the tide.

While Louis came in for some criticism in this area — people supposed he was impotent — Marie Antoinette got the worst of it. Publishers put out pamphlets attacking the queen as the revolution got underway.

Many artists dispensed with the allegory and went straight to smut. Assuming that Marie Antoinette had many lovers outside of marriage, revolutionaries referred to her as the “Austrian whore” and published all sorts of libels against her. The idea that Marie Antoinette was a libertine man-eater served a number of purposes. It was entertaining, titillating celebrity gossip of a kind we still see today. It was good business for the publishers who put this stuff out. And it served the revolutionary cause, framing the royal couple as unworthy of the throne: the king was a weak, befuddled man dominated by his unfaithful and immoral wife. These images helped convince French people that their monarchs were grubby, flawed people who did not have a divine right to the throne.

Over time, the walls closed in on the royal family. They ended up under virtual house arrest, and a hilariously ill-conceived escape attempt engendered even more ill will from the revolutionaries who now ruled the country. Eventually, they were imprisoned. Louis was put on trial and executed for his crimes against the French people.

But the government struggled a bit more with what to do with Marie Antoinette? Would she live out her years as the faintly tragic figure of the “Widow Capet,” as she was now called, or would she be treated like the “Austrian she-wolf” of the gossipy pamphlets?

The former queen’s fate hung in limbo for nine months after Louis had been executed in January 1793. When the radical Committee on Public Safety formed in April, it arrested many of her fellow aristocrats and put bars on the window of her chambers. There was talk of swapping the former queen to the Austrians in exchange for French prisoners of war, and the revolutionaries took her son, Louis-Charles, away from her so that they could mold him into a republican. Meanwhile, a vicious civil war raged against rebels who were fighting to restore the royal family to its thrones.

Finally, in October, the Widow Capet was woken up in the middle of the night to appear before the Revolutionary Tribunal. She was given no time to prepare a defense. She sat, exhausted and haggard, through a show trial in which dozens of witnesses testified against her. Fitting her public image, many of them told dubious stories about the orgies and even incest that she had supposedly participated in.

But the trial didn’t have the desired effect. Many of the charges were improbable, to say the least, and the allegation that she had been incestuous with her young son appalled even many of the revolutionaries who wanted to see her dead. And when the incest charges were made, she rose to her feet and appealed to the good sense of all of the parents in the room:

Marie Antoinette held out some hope that she might be spared execution, but the truth was that the trial was just a formality. She would be executed, as had been decided before the proceedings began.

When she mounted the platform around noon on October 16, 1793, the crowd bayed for the death of their 37-year-old former queen. They got what they wanted.

But an odd thing happened after she died. The undignified circumstances of Marie Antoinette’s execution and her status as a widow and mother offended traditional ideas about womanhood just as the salacious rumors about her had. It turned out that only in death could Marie Antoinette convince people that she was a normal person, just like them.
