The Comet That Wasn’t a Comet

The Mexican Revolution and the end of the world

George Dillard
Nov 4
∙
Preview
 
READ IN APP
 

José Guadalupe Posada lived a depressingly familiar artist’s life. He struggled as a printmaker and illustrator, commenting on Mexican politics and society in magazines and posters. He often used the calavera, a skeletal figure associated with the Day of the Dead, to make his points, as in this 1903 example:

Posada was one of those artists who only gained respect after his death. He died in squalor and was buried in an anonymous grave in 1913, but artists like Diego Rivera and Frida Kahlo rediscovered his work and used it as inspiration for some of the great art of the 1930s.

We saw one of Posada’s illustrations in Sunday’s post on Mexico City in 1910, created during the Mexican Centenario and just before the outbreak of the Mexican Revolution. Posada seems to have been struck, like a lot of Mexican people, by the appearance of Halley’s Comet in 1910. It must have seemed like the heavens were marking a significant year.

The text says that the comet should be called El Centenario, a harbinger of a significant year.

But this image was recycled from 1899, when Posada had accompanied the picture with a far more alarming text. Here, he states that the whole world will be roasted like a pork rind, that a comet arriving in 1899 will be the end of the world.

At first glance, this seems like a joke, and it probably is, on some level. What’s strange is that there was not even a comet. But many people in Mexico did think that the world would end in 1899, thanks to a crackpot Austrian and an irresponsible press. ...
