Friar Bernardino de Sahagún was one of the few Spanish priests to show an interest in the culture of the people that his nation was conquering. While he was dedicated to converting the Aztec people to Christianity in the 16th century, he was also deeply curious about their culture. He compiled the Historia general de las cosas de la Nueva España (General History of the Things of New Spain), a book of over 2,000 pages that tried to record as much as possible about the rapidly changing culture of the region. Some people think of him as the father of anthropology. One of Sahagún’s observations was that the Mexican people considered comets to be important harbingers of bad times. In fact, they connected a comet that crossed the sky in 1519 to the subsequent spread of European diseases and Spanish conquest of Mexico. This image from the Codex Durán shows the moment: Sahagún
Almost four hundred years later, folk beliefs about comets had not entirely disappeared, because the appearance of Halley’s Comet in the Mexican skies in 1910 (it was actually the second major comet to appear that year, after another in January) made some people wonder whether it, too, was an omen. A humorous engraving republished by José Posada (he originally drew the one on the right in 1899) showed panicked people fleeing a comet: If this were fiction, the appearance of a celestial omen at the beginning of 1910 in Mexico would be a little on-the-nose. But sometimes reality isn’t very subtle. Mexican people knew that the comet’s appearance heralded a big year — 1910 was the 100th anniversary of Mexico’s war of independence — but they didn’t know that 1910 would also see the beginning of a revolution that would tear Mexico apart. So what was it like in Mexico City as the country approached both a celebration of national pride and a dangerous rupture? If you’ve ever been to Mexico City, you may have found yourself crawling along in traffic on the Paseo de Reforma around the Angel of Independence, which sits atop an impressive column. It’s one of the city’s most significant landmarks, full of symbolism: the angel holds a broken chain symbolizing freedom and extends a crown toward the Mexican people. At its base is a lion gently tending to a child, symbolizing that Mexico is fierce when necessary and gentle when possible. You can see the lion statue here in this photo of the inauguration ceremony: Here’s the monument today, now atop a platform of 14 steps because the city is sinking around it: The monument was commissioned by Mexican President Porfirio Díaz in 1900, in anticipation of the Centenario, or 100th-anniversary celebration of Miguel Hidalgo’s revolt. Díaz was still president ten years later when the Centenario came around. Mexico’s big anniversary was, on the one hand, a great celebration of national pride. Starting on September 1, the government hosted a series of events that culminated on the 16th, the anniversary of Hidalgo’s rebellion against the Spanish. During this time, people in Mexico City could visit exhibitions of art, displays of new technology, and the opening of new parks and government buildings. The government tried to keep poor residents out of sight. A new opera house (today’s Palacio de Bellas Artes) had been planned for the celebration, but construction fell behind schedule. It was a reminder that the modernization of Mexico City was not as far along as Díaz claimed. Imagine celebrating a big national anniversary, but having that celebration tainted by an unpopular authoritarian who insists on placing himself at the center of the whole thing. It would really be a bummer, as I’m sure it was in Mexico City. Porfirio Díaz had been president of Mexico since 1884. But Díaz went back on his promise and ran against Francisco Madero, a democratic reformer: The resistance had enjoyed widespread popular support: Díaz jailed Madero soon after Halley’s Comet passed over Mexico and then stole the July election, claiming to have won 99% of the vote. This was the cloud that hung over the celebrations in Mexico City, the tail of the comet that had foretold dark days ahead. Within a couple of weeks of the big Centenario celebrations, everything fell apart.
