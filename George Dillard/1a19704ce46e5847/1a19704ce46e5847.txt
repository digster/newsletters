Friar Bernardino de Sahagún was one of the few Spanish priests to show an interest in the culture of the people that his nation was conquering. While he was dedicated to converting the Aztec people to Christianity in the 16th century, he was also deeply curious about their culture. He compiled the Historia general de las cosas de la Nueva España (General History of the Things of New Spain), a book of over 2,000 pages that tried to record as much as possible about the rapidly changing culture of the region. Some people think of him as the father of anthropology.
One of Sahagún’s observations was that the Mexican people considered comets to be important harbingers of bad times. In fact, they connected a comet that crossed the sky in 1519 to the subsequent spread of European diseases and Spanish conquest of Mexico. This image from the Codex Durán shows the moment:

These people called the comet Citlalin Popoca, which means smoking star. They considered it a harbinger of the death of a prince or king, or of war, or of famine. The common people would say: this is our famine. They called the comet’s flare Citlalin Tlamina , which means arrow-shooting star. And they said that whenever that arrow fell upon a living thing, a hare or rabbit or other animal, and where it struck, a worm would immediately grow, rendering that animal unfit for consumption. For this reason, these people took care to keep warm at night, so that the flare of the comet would not fall upon them.
Almost four hundred years later, folk beliefs about comets had not entirely disappeared, because the appearance of Halley’s Comet in the Mexican skies in 1910 (it was actually the second major comet to appear that year, after another in January) made some people wonder whether it, too, was an omen. A humorous engraving republished by José Posada (he originally drew the one on the right in 1899) showed panicked people fleeing a comet:
If this were fiction, the appearance of a celestial omen at the beginning of 1910 in Mexico would be a little on-the-nose. But sometimes reality isn’t very subtle. Mexican people knew that the comet’s appearance heralded a big year — 1910 was the 100th anniversary of Mexico’s war of independence — but they didn’t know that 1910 would also see the beginning of a revolution that would tear Mexico apart. 
So what was it like in Mexico City as the country approached both a celebration of national pride and a dangerous rupture?
If you’ve ever been to Mexico City, you may have found yourself crawling along in traffic on the Paseo de Reforma around the Angel of Independence, which sits atop an impressive column. It’s one of the city’s most significant landmarks, full of symbolism: the angel holds a broken chain symbolizing freedom and extends a crown toward the Mexican people. At its base is a lion gently tending to a child, symbolizing that Mexico is fierce when necessary and gentle when possible. You can see the lion statue here in this photo of the inauguration ceremony:
Here’s the monument today, now atop a platform of 14 steps because the city is sinking around it:
The monument was commissioned by Mexican President Porfirio Díaz in 1900, in anticipation of the Centenario, or 100th-anniversary celebration of Miguel Hidalgo’s revolt. Díaz was still president ten years later when the Centenario came around.  
Mexico’s big anniversary was, on the one hand, a great celebration of national pride. Starting on September 1, the government hosted a series of events that culminated on the 16th, the anniversary of Hidalgo’s rebellion against the Spanish. During this time, people in Mexico City could visit exhibitions of art, displays of new technology, and the opening of new parks and government buildings. The government tried to keep poor residents out of sight.

The message was that Mexico City was a modern metropolis, having been redesigned [ https://substack.com/redirect/34fcb98f-ff6b-484a-b08b-d86ab1ebd11d?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] in recent decades along the same lines as Buenos Aires and Paris, with wide boulevards and open public spaces. One visitor praised [ https://substack.com/redirect/99ab9320-445a-48d0-a362-66c9ea539ea3?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] it as a “beautiful, clean, grand and Europeanized city… [its streets are] lively as those of Madrid, urban as those of Vienna, and populous as those of New York.” He remarked on the streetcars and automobiles that crowded the roads, a sure sign of material progress.
But not everything went off without a hitch during the Centenario. A new opera house (today’s Palacio de Bellas Artes) had been planned for the celebration, but construction fell behind schedule. It was a reminder that the modernization of Mexico City was not as far along as Díaz claimed. During the Centenario, it still looked like this — a sure-to-be-beautiful structure, but one that would not be finished for another 24 years:
Another thing drawing attention away from the celebrations in 1910 was the man presiding over them. Here he is, on the left, carrying out his official duties. 
Imagine celebrating a big national anniversary, but having that celebration tainted by an unpopular authoritarian who insists on placing himself at the center of the whole thing. It would really be a bummer, as I’m sure it was in Mexico City.
Porfirio Díaz had been president of Mexico since 1884. He was responsible [ https://substack.com/redirect/99ab9320-445a-48d0-a362-66c9ea539ea3?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] for many of the changes in Mexico City, making it a safer and healthier place even as its population ballooned. But he was also seen as too pro-American, allowing investors from north of the border to buy up much of Mexico’s land and natural resources. The new, modern lifestyle he introduced was available only to a select elite, and inequality was getting worse. It seemed like he was never going to give up power. 
Díaz had won a series of rigged elections up to that point, but had told a journalist in 1908 that he intended to retire rather than run in 1910. He would, after all, be 80 years old by then. But Díaz went back on his promise and ran against Francisco Madero, a democratic reformer: 
The resistance had enjoyed widespread popular support:
Díaz jailed Madero soon after Halley’s Comet passed over Mexico and then stole the July election, claiming to have won 99% of the vote. This was the cloud that hung over the celebrations in Mexico City, the tail of the comet that had foretold dark days ahead. 
Within a couple of weeks of the big Centenario celebrations, everything fell apart. Madero escaped from prison in San Luis Potosí (his influential father had convinced his guards to let him out of prison on a day pass); he fled to the United States. In San Antonio, he wrote a manifesto [ https://substack.com/redirect/4e78bd86-572b-42f6-b8e2-931c26ca5bcc?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] (called the Plan of San Luis Potosí, to avoid violating American neutrality, and because it would have been a little embarrassing to name it after the American city where it was written):
Peoples, in their constant efforts for the triumph of the ideal of liberty and justice, are forced, at precise historical moments, to make their greatest sacrifices.
Our beloved country has reached one of those moments. A force of tyranny which we Mexicans were not accustomed to suffer after we won our independence oppresses us in such a manner that it has become intolerable. In exchange for that tyranny we are offered peace, but peace full of shame for the Mexican nation, because its basis is not law, but force; because its object is not the aggrandizement and prosperity of the country, but to enrich a small group who, abusing their influence, have converted the public charges into fountains of exclusively personal benefit, unscrupulously exploiting the manner of lucrative concessions and contracts.
He called for a revolution to begin in November. It did, and would quickly spiral out of control. Neither Díaz nor Madero nor many of the other people who took part in the early days of the revolution would make it out alive. Between one and three million people [ https://substack.com/redirect/3e96126e-79f7-410a-82ea-a9ef5ead443f?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] would die in the chaos, and the country would be consumed by fighting for a decade. 
For those who believed in such things, the omen of the comet was incredibly prescient. What should have been Mexico City’s big moment of celebration was fleeting and tainted by foreboding. In the end, the year of the comet would come to stand for something else in Mexican history: the beginning of a very dark decade.
