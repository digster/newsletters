The Politics of the Playground

The evolution of playground design -- and what it says about our evolving ideas about childhood

George Dillard
Jun 10
 
READ IN APP
 

In 1851, the Prussian government banned kindergartens. The Kindergartenverbot order was part of a larger response to the social and political upheavals in central Europe that had happened a few years before. It seems the government was wary of anything that smacked of social reform, and the idea that children should be allowed to play, explore, and learn in self-directed ways was too much for the establishment to bear.

Friedrich Froebel, the man who had helped to invent the kindergarten, pleaded with the King of Prussia:

Your Majesty, an old man of seventy who does not ask favours for himself, pleads with you in the name of childhood: do not allow the seed of a new education for morality, law and religion to be trampled on. Take it under your all powerful protection, so that it may flourish and contribute to the, so much needed, rebirth of humanity. Do not allow the matter of childhood to become a tool of political parties . . .

Kindergartens were eventually allowed to operate again, but Froebel’s wish — that play should not be subject to politics — would not come true. You probably don’t think much about playgrounds when you visit them. I certainly didn’t pay much attention to the places I took my kids when they were young, other than to wonder if they were crowded or full of bees. But the playground has actually been a battlefield where different ideas about society, virtue, and even politics have fought for supremacy.

Froebel was one of the first influential people to celebrate children’s play. He said that

Play is the highest expression of human development in childhood for it alone is the free expression of what is in a child’s soul.

Before the 19th century, when Froebel wrote his books and opened his kindergarten, nobody seemed to care very much about whether children could express what was in their souls. People didn’t think that childhood was necessarily a distinct stage of life from adulthood, or that children were much different from adults.

Children in those days certainly played, but there weren’t dedicated public spaces for them to do so. Most children lived in the countryside, where there were abundant opportunities for them to have some fun. They could climb trees or splash in a creek in the brief period of life before they had to take on more adult duties. But as industrial cities boomed, often with few safe areas for children to enjoy themselves, adults started to worry about the fate of kids. Jacob Riis’ photography in the late 1800s captured the desperate situation in which many street children found themselves.

So adults started to think about how to enable child’s play in urban areas. Some of the first playgrounds were just large sandboxes. The idea started in Germany and was first adopted in the United States by Boston philanthropists who provided a sandpit for the kids of that city. The idea spread; soon, there were ten sandpits. Most of them were located near tenement houses, to keep the children in the slums busy.

These early playgrounds were a part of the larger progressive movement. The progressives had loftier goals than just keeping kids busy. They wanted to clean up cities, improve health and safety, and alleviate poverty. The playground was a place where immigrant kids would become assimilated, healthy play would be encouraged, and wayward youths would be diverted from a life of crime, making cities better for everyone. Progressive reformer Jane Addams explained the moral dimensions of play: “Recreation is stronger than vice and recreation alone can stifle the lust for vice.”

By the early 1900s, the Playground Association of America had formed, with progressive icon Theodore Roosevelt as its honorary president. In 1907, Roosevelt said that

City streets are unsatisfactory playgrounds for children because of the danger, because most good games are against the law, because they are too hot in summer, and because in crowded sections of the city they are apt to be schools of crime. Neither do small back yards nor ornamental grass plots meet the needs of any but the very small children ... since play is a fundamental need, playgrounds should be provided for every child as much as schools.

By this point, playgrounds were evolving well beyond a pile of sand; they featured slides, climbing equipment, and rings to swing from. These early pictures of playgrounds give a sense of what they were like.

The St. Paul playground, you may notice, was just for girls — gender segregation was a common feature of early playgrounds. It was thought best to keep boys and girls separate, partially because girls were supposedly too dainty to handle playing with rowdy boys. These early playgrounds were, unsurprisingly, racially segregated, too. Black children generally had many fewer opportunities for play than white children did.

If you’re a modern parent, you may have gasped a bit looking at the early-20th-century playgrounds above because they allowed children to climb to vertiginous heights and swing wildly on the long ropes of a “giant stride.” These playgrounds were the first — but certainly not the last — to play a part in the debate over how safe childhood should be.

In 1912, safety advocates persuaded New York City to stop building big climbing towers for children because kids were falling and injuring themselves. But playgrounds remained quite unsafe by today’s standards, with steel structures that allowed kids maximal freedom of movement.

During the Second World War, not many playgrounds were built; metal was needed for the war effort. But some of the destruction of the war opened up a new type of playground, the so-called “adventure playground.” In bombed-out European cities, adults noticed kids playing in the rubble. They realized that children could make play out of pretty much anything, and that free, creative play might be more rewarding than more programmed play.

The first adventure playground’s name — the Emdrup Junk Playground in Copenhagen — says it all. It was a big sandpit full of, well, junk, that children could play with however they wished. The idea was that kids could make their own fun in urban areas, in much the same way that they might do in the countryside. These seem to have been more popular in Europe than the United States, although some American cities provided them as well. “The Yard” in Minneapolis opened in 1949, allowing kids to do what they wished with old lumber, bricks, tiles, and even a decommissioned boxcar.

Most American playgrounds went in a different direction from the free-play ethos of adventure playgrounds. Many of the playgrounds built in postwar suburban parks tried to capture the imagination of kids with themes — a playground might resemble a rocket or a pirate ship. Playgrounds got more standardized, too, as playground manufacturers offered parks pre-designed kits. Play areas started to look similar in different areas of the country, reflecting the increasing homogenization of American culture.

By the late 20th century, safety-conscious parents were starting to push for safer playgrounds. When a one-year-old fell 12 feet from a slide onto asphalt in 1978 — and his parents won a $9.5 million lawsuit — parks departments reconsidered the level of risk that children could take. Designers started to use more plastic instead of skin-scorching metal, and took out tall slides, centrifugal merry-go-rounds, and vertiginous climbing structures in favor of “post-and-platform” designs that kept kids more contained. The playground became a more programmed place, where kids completed a set of preordained tasks rather than making their own fun.

These playgrounds represented a new emphasis on child safety — kids were taught about stranger danger, kept inside more often, and not allowed to roam freely as previous generations did. This kept the kids safer, but seems to have led to other problems, including anxiety, risk-aversion, and dependence on screens.

Now, as the generations who grew up on safe-but-boring playgrounds have their own kids, there’s a backlash to all of this safety focus. The newest playgrounds allow kids more freedom and, as part of the backlash against the dominance of screens in our lives, often include natural elements like water and rocks to help alleviate “nature-deficit disorder.” Today’s playgrounds are more likely to provide children with more options — moving parts that don’t have a clear purpose, allowing the kids to use more imagination.

Each generation of design reflected the prevailing ideas about children. Are children miniature adults? Are they wild? Are they fragile? Are they resilient? Society’s answers to these questions took shape on the playground.

I look back with a little bit of nostalgia and wonder at the playgrounds of my youth. I loved climbing on jungle gyms and getting profoundly dizzy on the merry-go-rounds, but I can’t quite believe my parents let me get on those burning-hot, towering metal slides. I understand why they got replaced by safer, more boring structures, but I wonder what was lost when they were.

Someday, the kids playing on today’s playgrounds will look back with a similar mix of nostalgia and head-shaking at the new playgrounds that are being built today. They, too, will seem impossibly quaint, as they’re replaced by new playgrounds that reflect different political and social understandings.
