Some societies were so big and powerful that they still command attention (as the meme told us, The Huns have shown up in film as marauding barbarians: And served as an effective propaganda image against the Germans in World War I: The story most people hear about the Huns — the one on which these pop-culture depictions are based — is pretty simple: The Huns emerged from the steppes into Eastern Europe. Their fearsome armies pushed other Germanic groups, who had been living on the fringes of the Roman Empire, to attack the civilized Romans. After creating indirect havoc in Rome for decades, the Huns came under the rule of Attila, who demonstrated his ruthlessness by killing his brother and directly invading the empire. Hapless Roman armies failed to stop him, and soon his armies were poised to strike at Italy, in the heart of the empire. Only the Pope, who traveled out to meet with Attila and somehow persuaded him to withdraw, could save the Eternal City from destruction. Soon after, in 453 CE, Attila suffered a hemorrhage, saving Rome from further attacks, but the damage was done. Rome never recovered. The visual representations of the Huns have remained pretty consistent over time. A medieval Hungarian book shows their hordes besieging the Roman city of Aquileia: In 1514, Raphael painted the meeting between Pope Leo and the wild Hunnic hordes (note Saints Peter and Paul appearing in the sky to scare off Attila): Eugene Delacroix put Attila in a wolfskin, riding over the bodies of his enemies: Ulpiano Checa painted the Huns in 1887 as a swirling horde, almost a force of nature: This early 20th-century painting by Georges Rochegrosse shows the vicious Huns hauling away a Roman maiden: And here, in a painting by Ferenc Paczka, Attila drinks himself to death in a bed strewn with animal skins on his wedding night: These depictions all tell the standard story. The Huns were fearsome barbarians. Their military prowess was impressive, no doubt, but they serve mostly as a foil for the weakening but noble Roman Empire. But how do we know all this? The Huns are mentioned in passing by a few ancient chroniclers, but detailed stories come from only two contemporary sources: Ammianus Marcellinus, a Roman soldier who wrote about the history of Rome in the late 300s, when the Huns first appeared in Europe, and Priscus, a Roman official who visited Attila on a diplomatic mission.
