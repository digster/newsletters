What were the Pillars of Hercules? The myths told that the Oracle at Delphi told Hercules that he had to atone for having killed his wife and child. So Hercules set out to do some hard stuff. He fought a lion and a hydra. He cleaned out some really dirty stables. He took an Amazon queen’s belt. And, in his tenth labor, he rustled some cattle owned by a three-bodied giant named Geryon. This task took Hercules to the end of the known world. In honor of his travels, Hercules erected pillars that, in some later traditions, were marked with the words “Non Plus Ultra” — there’s nothing more beyond this. After Spain established an empire in the Americas, its kings cheekily depicted the pillars with “plus ultra” — further beyond — as in this emblem of Charles V: The “pillars” were, in reality, giant rocks: Monte Hacho in Ceuta, a peninsula that juts out from North Africa into the Mediterranean, and its more famous cousin, Gibraltar, which does the same from the Spanish side. The Rock of Gibraltar looms imperiously over the strait of the same name, dominating one of the most significant chokepoints in world history. For centuries, Gibraltar marked the limits of the world known to Europeans. You can see it represented as the Pillars of Hercules at the western edge (which here is at the very bottom) of this 1475 “world map” by Lucas Brandis: And the pillars guard the western end of the Mediterranean in this 1460 map from Nicolaus Germanicus: But once Europeans began to set sail beyond the Mediterranean, Gibraltar was portrayed a different way. There’s almost always a ship passing through, as on the title page of Francis Bacon’s Novum Organum. Here, the Pillars of Hercules represent not a boundary but a gateway to exploration: This 19th-century illustration makes the straits look dramatic, with choppy water and the towering rock dwarfing the ships floating in the water: This 18th-century depiction seems to show ships peacefully floating in the harbor, except for a puff of smoke coming from the ship in the center: It’s a drawing of one of the most significant battles over Gibraltar, the siege of 1704, in which, as part of the War of the Spanish Succession, Anglo-Dutch and Spanish forces engaged in a back-and-forth struggle over the rock. It was much more chaotic than the placid scene above makes it look. First, the British and Dutch took control, looting the town and forcing out its Spanish inhabitants. Though the Spanish returned with French allies and launched another siege, they failed to take the territory back. Other depictions make the battle look more intense: You can see here some of the fortifications that made this natural chokepoint even more difficult to conquer: At the end of the war, Spain formally ceded Gibraltar to Great Britain. The British didn’t necessarily want to keep it — it was a strange little outpost far from the British Isles, expensive to defend and a possible trigger for further conflict with Spain. Meanwhile, the loss of Gibraltar — and the presence of a British base on the coast of Spain — was an intolerable insult to Spanish dignity. The British expected to negotiate handing the rock back to Spain at some point, but, as so often happens with these things, never did. Britain was a rising power in the 18th century, and Spain was fading. Spain could not compel the British to cede the territory, and the British could not see a reason to hand land to a weaker power, even if it was land the British didn’t really want. Over time, Gibraltar came to seem like a key node in the British Empire. And so the British built improved fortifications: And defended them against attack: After attack: The “Great Siege” of Gibraltar, depicted in the three images above, lasted for four years between 1779 and 1783. It was part of the global conflict stemming from the American Revolution, and ended only when the revolution did. After the Napoleonic Wars, Gibraltar ceased to be an active war zone, but the British still used it to interfere with their enemies’ trade. Once the Suez Canal opened, Gibraltar became even more important; Gibraltar and Suez served as twin gates that allowed quick passage to India. So Gibraltar remained in British hands as a small, cosmopolitan outpost of the empire. It had a significant Italian population that had fled there during the Napoleonic conquests. There were large populations of Portuguese people, Spaniards, Jews, and British citizens as well. These groups did not necessarily live in harmony, and Gibraltar’s British rulers often discriminated against other populations.
