The Weirdest World Cup

How a fragmenting world met on the field in 1938

George Dillard
Jun 24
 
READ IN APP
 

One of the strangest events in Olympic history took place in the quarter-finals of the soccer tournament at the 1936 Games.

These were Hitler’s Olympics, and part of his goal that summer was to present an image of strength and stability that would glorify his fascist regime. The quarter-final game between Peru and Austria was, contrary to the goals of the Nazi propagandists, a mess.

Austria went ahead 2–0 in the first half. Peru tied the game in the second. When the game went to extra time, the referee (from Mussolini’s Italy) disallowed three Peruvian goals. Eventually, the Peruvians scored two goals that the referee allowed. They were ahead 4–2 with one minute left to play when chaos erupted. Fans stormed the field, and the referee abandoned the match.

The Peruvian team was incensed. They had been ahead by two goals in the 119th minute when the referee blew the whistle. The Peruvians alleged that the pitch invaders were associated with Nazis who did not want to see a South American team (half of whose players were black) defeat the Aryans from Austria. The Austrians said that it was Peruvians who had stormed the field, and that some of them were armed. An Austrian player had been injured in the scrum.

The tournament’s organizers held a hearing about possibly replaying the game. The Peruvians didn’t show up to the hearing, although there is confusion about why. Some versions of the story say that the Peruvian delegation was delayed by a Nazi parade and could not make the meeting on time. Others say that Peru refused to attend because its delegation knew the decision would be rigged against them.

The organizers told Peru it had to replay the game. The Peruvians refused, and their entire Olympic team went home in protest. The incident led to anti-German boycotts in Peru. Meanwhile, the Austrians made it to the finals of the tournament; they lost to the Italians in a final that the Nazi organizers must have found more palatable than the prospect of a Peruvian victory.

Peruvians, and many of their South American neighbors, were incensed by the way they had been treated. A representative of the Peruvian Olympic Committee said, “We’ve no faith in European athletics. We have come here and found a bunch of merchants.”

The chaotic 1936 Olympic soccer tournament set the scene for an even messier 1938 World Cup. From the 1934 World Cup in Mussolini’s Italy to the 1978 tournament held in the midst of Argentina’s “Dirty War” to the 2018 version in Putin’s Russia to this year’s Trump-centric edition, the World Cup has often been both a mirror and a stage for global politics. But never more so than in 1938, when a world on the brink of war tried to get along for a few weeks of friendly sports.

The first notable thing about the 1938 World Cup was who wasn’t there.

The first edition of the World Cup took place in 1930 in Uruguay, followed by a tournament in Italy in 1934. There were three bids to host in 1938: Argentina, France, and Germany. Many assumed that the World Cup would continue to alternate back and forth between Latin America and Europe, at the time the two great hotbeds of soccer culture. Therefore, Argentina expected to be named host. FIFA, however, chose France because European teams were unwilling to travel long distances during a period of political chaos and economic depression.

As a result, South American teams, already outraged over the treatment of Peru in 1936, mostly boycotted the tournament. Of the major South American teams (including Peru, Argentina, and Uruguay), only Brazil made the journey to France.

Some other nations boycotted, too. Egypt refused to take part because the tournament was held during Ramadan. The British nations, as they had in 1930 and 1934, declined to participate because they thought the World Cup was beneath them: for them, the British Home Championship between England, Scotland, Wales, and Ireland was the real world championship.

But many of the absentees were kept out because of deteriorating global conditions. Austria had qualified for the tournament, but it no longer existed after Germany annexed it in March of 1938. The Austrians sent a note to FIFA apologizing: “Sorry to cancel World Cup enrolment. Austrian football federation is gone.” Spain was in the midst of a civil war, so its team did not travel to France. Neither did Japan, since the country had launched a bloody invasion of China the year before.

By the time it got underway in early June, the World Cup was a bit of a mess. It only had 15 teams for a 16-team bracket after Austria ceased to exist. It was missing many of the world’s best teams, with minnows like Cuba, Romania, and the Dutch East Indies taking their place. And the darkness spreading across Europe overshadowed much of what happened at the World Cup once it was underway.

FIFA had chosen France as host over Germany in the hopes that it would serve as a politically neutral site. There would be no fascist propaganda in the French stadiums. This did not, however, keep politics at bay.

Adolf Hitler had ordered the German team to absorb members of the now-defunct Austrian team as a show of united Germanness. This turned out to be a mistake, as the Austrians and Germans did not have much time to practice together. The new hybrid German team lost to Switzerland before a jeering crowd in the first round, marking the worst German performance at the World Cup until 2018.

Italy’s team was another target for public derision. Other countries’ fans despised the Italians for several reasons. First, Italy’s association with fascism and its invasion of Ethiopia had begun to turn it into an international pariah. France was home to thousands of exiles from Italy who had been forced out by the new fascist order; many of them showed up to matches to express their anger at Mussolini. And, for the more sports-minded fans, rumors swirled about whether Mussolini had rigged the previous World Cup, which Italy had won in Rome.

Thousands of French protesters met the Italian team at midnight when it got to Marseille for its opening match. The Italians responded at the game by making not one but two fascist salutes before the game started despite deafening boos from the crowd.

Later in the tournament, facing France, the Italians had to wear something other than their usual blue uniforms because the hosts wore blue, too. The Italians pointedly chose black uniforms with large fasces (the ancient Roman symbol that had been adopted by Mussolini as an emblem of fascism) in an echo of the blackshirted thugs who had propelled Mussolini to power back in Italy.

Mussolini exerted other types of influence on the Italian team, as well: he demanded that the manager play Eraldo Monzeglio, a backup defender who gave tennis and soccer lessons to the dictator’s children. A story went around that he sent a message to the team before one match that read “Win or die” (Italian players later denied that anything like this had happened).

After all of the politics and controversy, the 1938 World Cup moved toward its final match on June 19th. The Italians had defeated Brazil in the semifinals; Brazil’s coach had left his best player, Leonidas, on the bench, perhaps in a cocky attempt to save him for the finals. On the other side of the bracket, Hungary smashed Sweden 5–1.

The final was an opportunity for the Italians to show that they could win without Mussolini putting his thumb on the scales and for Mussolini to crow about the superiority of fascist soccer. Italian striker Silvio Piola scored twice as his team won 4–2, providing the Duce with plenty of propaganda material.

The 1938 World Cup is notable for the degree to which the politics of the time infected the competition. It is also historic because the Italian victory was the first World Cup final from which TV footage survives.

But most of all, it’s significant because it was the last World Cup for 12 years. The forces that warped this supposedly friendly sporting competition soon degenerated into the worst war the world has ever seen. From 1938 to 1950, there was no time for frivolous pursuits like soccer.

The 1938 World Cup shows how sports, especially international competitions, can reflect the more serious tensions that surround them. It also demonstrates how supposedly cheerful sports competitions can be infected by more negative strains of nationalism. No World Cup is free from politics, and the current one seems particularly affected by it.

I suppose we should be glad that, however weird this World Cup gets, things aren’t yet quite as bad as they were in 1938. But then again, I wonder what people told themselves as they watched the 1938 World Cup?
