Earthset is part of a long chain of images that have transformed people’s understanding of themselves and their surroundings. We don’t know much about Martin Waldseemüller’s life. He lived in Germany, studied with humanists, and made maps. Though he never left Europe, his greatest creation helped people understand just how vast the world was. Waldseemüller’s Universalis Cosmographia, published in 1507, pulled together existing cartographic knowledge of the Eastern Hemisphere, which had been growing over the preceding centuries, with the sketchy and inconsistent reports coming from Europeans who had traveled to the Americas since 1492. Waldseemüller had to reconcile ancient Ptolemaic geography with shocking new evidence about the planet’s size. Every map at this point in history was a remarkable act of imagination, the abstraction of a bunch of ground-level information into a god’s-eye overhead perspective. Waldseemüller’s was one of the greatest maps ever made, a massive collection of panels that Waldseemüller was one of the first to understand that lands of the Western Hemisphere were their own landmass, not a part of Asia as Columbus and others had assumed. He called the new lands America. The map was remarkably detailed, with little notes about the sources he used to make his judgments. Waldseemüller printed 1,000 copies of the map, and it was widely imitated by other cartographers. He improved and refined it in later versions. His map and its descendants illustrated to Europeans for the first time just how much of the world lay out there beyond their understanding. Author Toby Lester While Waldseemüller made the world seem bigger, Copernicus made it seem smaller. His diagram of a heliocentric universe would, over time, reconfigure how people thought about their place in the cosmos. Copernicus’s vision, which was not published until the end of his life, moved humanity out of the center of the solar system. We were no longer the axis around which everything else orbited; instead, our planet was just one of several orbiting the sun. It took a while for Copernicus’s diagram to be accepted. While his book De Revolutionibus Orbium Coelestium circulated rather widely after its publication in 1543, it was just one of several models of the solar system that competed for primacy in the 16th century. Only after Galileo and other astronomers found proof of the heliocentric view did it become standard. Galileo’s struggle against the Church, in which he was accused of heresy and confined to house arrest for supposedly contradicting the Bible, showed just how explosive Copernicus’s model of the solar system was. In many ways, it served as an opening salvo in the battle between religious and scientific views of the world. In the long run, Copernicus’s diagram would become a reminder of the fact that we’re not the center of the universe after all, a first step toward an understanding that we’re floating through the hostile vastness of space. The images created by Waldseemüller and Copernicus were acts of imagination, abstractions that represented what the world must look like from some position that humans could never occupy. But eventually, people developed the ability to actually ascend into the heavens.
