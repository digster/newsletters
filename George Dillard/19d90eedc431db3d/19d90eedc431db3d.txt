They’re calling it “Earthset.”
It’s one of many stunning images beamed down from the Artemis II capsule as it circled the Moon. The NASA mission — the first to bring humans to the vicinity of the Moon in more than 50 years — had some scientific benefits [ https://substack.com/redirect/f21694d2-904a-4114-9e01-95d2ac7b2412?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ], but its main importance was, honestly, emotional.
In the midst of a dark period, a time of war and cynicism in which many people feel like our world is in a tailspin, here was a reminder that we can do ambitious things. A lot of people I know followed the news from space with a palpable sense of relief, grateful to pay attention to the achievements of capable and impressive people for once.
As stunning as the astronauts’ photos of the Moon [ https://substack.com/redirect/06bb0d21-4578-460e-8f03-b1baa5063d42?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] are, I was not alone in being especially awed by images like “Earthset” that showed the Earth from afar. Since we seem to be marinating in ugliness and division a lot of the time these days, it was useful to be reminded that we’re all living together on this oasis in the darkness of space.
NASA titled the photo “Earthset” in a conscious nod to “Earthrise,” a photo taken by Apollo 8 astronaut William Anders in 1968.
Earthrise, the first color photo of the Earth taken from such a distance — 238,000 miles — became one of the most influential images in history. Captured during another period of division and war, it changed people’s perspectives on their place in the universe, reminding them of their own smallness and fragility. Anders, who abandoned his religious beliefs after seeing the Earth from the capsule, later wrote:
That photograph, shared globally and always in the public domain, has since served to educate and inspire: The Earth we saw rising over the battered grey lunar surface was small and delicate, a magnificent spot of color in the vast blackness of space. Once-distant places appeared inseparably close. Borders that once rendered division vanished. All of humanity appeared joined together on this glorious-but-fragile sphere.
Another vision made the hair on the back of my neck stand up. I held my fist at arm’s length. That stunning vision disappeared. From one lunar distance our world was easily obscured. At 10 lunar distances Earth would have been but the size of a ladybug. And at 100 — then and now far beyond human reach — Earth would no longer be visible to the naked eye. Here was everything humans had been, everything we were, and everything we might become — and yet our home planet was physically insignificant in space…
We set out to explore the moon and instead discovered the Earth.
Historian Robert Poole, author of a book on the photo, writes [ https://substack.com/redirect/b8ff5da3-0cbc-4d7b-8dd1-d198c505409b?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] that the image helped to launch the environmental movement:
It is possible to see that Earthrise marked the tipping point, the moment when the sense of the space age flipped from what it meant for space to what it means for Earth.
Earthset likely won’t be as transformative as Earthrise was, but it may still inspire a new generation to shift its perspective.
Seeing the Artemis photos reminded me of the power images have to shatter our preconceptions and change how we see the world. Earthset is part of a long chain of images that have transformed people’s understanding of themselves and their surroundings.
We don’t know much about Martin Waldseemüller’s life. He lived in Germany, studied with humanists, and made maps. Though he never left Europe, his greatest creation helped people understand just how vast the world was.
Waldseemüller’s Universalis Cosmographia, published in 1507, pulled together existing cartographic knowledge of the Eastern Hemisphere, which had been growing over the preceding centuries, with the sketchy and inconsistent reports coming from Europeans who had traveled to the Americas since 1492. Waldseemüller had to reconcile ancient Ptolemaic geography with shocking new evidence about the planet’s size.
Every map at this point in history was a remarkable act of imagination, the abstraction of a bunch of ground-level information into a god’s-eye overhead perspective. Waldseemüller’s was one of the greatest maps ever made, a massive collection of panels that measured [ https://substack.com/redirect/bef05637-7244-4ef2-a775-257924950d05?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] 4 1/2 by 8 feet, encapsulating all available knowledge about the geography of the world into one image.
Waldseemüller was one of the first to understand that lands of the Western Hemisphere were their own landmass, not a part of Asia as Columbus and others had assumed. He called the new lands America. The map was remarkably detailed, with little notes about the sources he used to make his judgments.
Waldseemüller printed 1,000 copies of the map, and it was widely imitated by other cartographers. He improved and refined it in later versions. His map and its descendants illustrated to Europeans for the first time just how much of the world lay out there beyond their understanding. Author Toby Lester argues [ https://substack.com/redirect/861e0bb5-bb05-4a6e-9fd2-e7d8d13ce82e?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] that “It’s a birth certificate for the world that came into being in 1492 — and it’s a death warrant for the one that was there before.” The Earth, it seemed, was far vaster and more mysterious than it had seemed just a few decades before.
While Waldseemüller made the world seem bigger, Copernicus made it seem smaller. His diagram of a heliocentric universe would, over time, reconfigure how people thought about their place in the cosmos.
Copernicus’s vision, which was not published until the end of his life, moved humanity out of the center of the solar system. We were no longer the axis around which everything else orbited; instead, our planet was just one of several orbiting the sun.
It took a while for Copernicus’s diagram to be accepted. While his book De Revolutionibus Orbium Coelestium circulated rather widely after its publication in 1543, it was just one of several models of the solar system that competed for primacy in the 16th century. Only after Galileo and other astronomers found proof of the heliocentric view did it become standard.
Galileo’s struggle against the Church, in which he was accused of heresy and confined to house arrest for supposedly contradicting the Bible, showed just how explosive Copernicus’s model of the solar system was. In many ways, it served as an opening salvo in the battle between religious and scientific views of the world. In the long run, Copernicus’s diagram would become a reminder of the fact that we’re not the center of the universe after all, a first step toward an understanding that we’re floating through the hostile vastness of space.
The images created by Waldseemüller and Copernicus were acts of imagination, abstractions that represented what the world must look like from some position that humans could never occupy. But eventually, people developed the ability to actually ascend into the heavens.
The first aerial photographs were taken by Nadar [ https://substack.com/redirect/72221323-2b14-4759-8194-b02bb95a6011?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ], the remarkable Parisian photographer who captured Paris from a tethered hot air balloon equipped with a darkroom in the basket. The first, taken in 1858, looked like this:
While there had long been a tradition of artists painting a city or landscape from a nearby hill, aerial photos like these kicked off a 19th-century craze for “bird’s eye [ https://substack.com/redirect/8e3f2ff9-b102-4093-9497-b40a3b547b96?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ]” images. Some were photographic, while others were drawn or painted. But all of them helped to transform people’s understanding of the places where they lived.
While a map is divorced from our experience of the world and a ground-level view obscures everything outside of our immediate surroundings, the aerial photo combines the two in a satisfying way. Aerial photos — now easy to summon on the internet, but so novel back then — made it possible for people to understand the places where they lived in a new way.
At the same time, the work of nature photographers like Carleton Watkins [ https://substack.com/redirect/4dfc4aff-c41e-416f-b1f3-c4e51ae05ca2?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] — who lugged heavy camera equipment into the mountains of California to create “mammoth” pictures of Yosemite and other remote areas — allowed people to imagine and appreciate places they would likely never visit.
Watkins’s photos, like his “best general view” of Yosemite, above, circulated [ https://substack.com/redirect/4dfc4aff-c41e-416f-b1f3-c4e51ae05ca2?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] through Congress and the White House in the 1860s, eventually inspiring the government to protect the valley, beginning the national parks program in the United States.
One similarity between all of these images is that they transport the human eye to a place it’s unlikely to be able to go on its own. Our vision is often limited to the few hundred yards around us; we can’t take it all in. Images like the ones we’ve discussed have supplied us with new perspectives.
This process can be revelatory, but it can also be a little dangerous, as Laura Kurgan writes [ https://substack.com/redirect/463e703f-6fe6-4e56-ac73-c9a0ef16fed5?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ]:
This drive to locate, to coordinate, however revelatory and even emancipatory it can be, also has its price. It seems as though in the end, maps — the successful ones, the ones that show us where we are and get us from here to there — risk offering only two alternatives. They let us see too much, and hence blind us to what we cannot see, imposing a quiet tyranny of orientation that erases the possibility of disoriented discovery, or they lose sight of all the other things that we ought to see. They omit, according to their conventions, those invisible lines of people, places, and networks that create the most common spaces we live in today.
Still, at their best, images like these can change our understanding of ourselves in important and humbling ways. Carl Sagan sensed this in 1990, when he convinced NASA to use Voyager 1’s last photo session to turn back toward Earth and take a picture of home. The result was the famous “pale blue dot [ https://substack.com/redirect/8f044ae7-33b2-46f7-a0c6-a1bd4546b2c7?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ]” photo, in which the Earth appears as a mere speck in a sunbeam.
A few years later, Sagan explained [ https://substack.com/redirect/95e44851-1938-4527-8624-b725c6e05734?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] what that photograph meant to him, and to a lot of other people:
Look again at that dot. That’s here. That’s home. That’s us. On it everyone you love, everyone you know, everyone you ever heard of, every human being who ever was, lived out their lives. The aggregate of our joy and suffering, thousands of confident religions, ideologies, and economic doctrines, every hunter and forager, every hero and coward, every creator and destroyer of civilization, every king and peasant, every young couple in love, every mother and father, hopeful child, inventor and explorer, every teacher of morals, every corrupt politician, every “superstar,” every “supreme leader,” every saint and sinner in the history of our species lived there — on a mote of dust suspended in a sunbeam.
The Earth is a very small stage in a vast cosmic arena. Think of the rivers of blood spilled by all those generals and emperors so that, in glory and triumph, they could become the momentary masters of a fraction of a dot. Think of the endless cruelties visited by the inhabitants of one corner of this pixel on the scarcely distinguishable inhabitants of some other corner, how frequent their misunderstandings, how eager they are to kill one another, how fervent their hatreds.
Our posturings, our imagined self-importance, the delusion that we have some privileged position in the Universe, are challenged by this point of pale light. Our planet is a lonely speck in the great enveloping cosmic dark. In our obscurity, in all this vastness, there is no hint that help will come from elsewhere to save us from ourselves.
Occasionally, we need a new perspective to jolt us out of our old ways of understanding the world. May the Artemis photos serve that purpose; we certainly need the help.
