Singapore: Straits and Shadows

How the opium trade built one of the world’s great cities

French Admiral Charles-Alexandre Linois was almost 7,000 miles from home, patrolling the eastern end of the Strait of Malacca in the service of Napoleon’s imperial ambitions.

His goal was to disrupt British shipping through the strait. The French understood that the British economy depended on the shipping that moved through that vital chokepoint, and it was his job to intercept British vessels that tried to travel between India and China. Dutch informants told Linois when and where to expect the East India Company’s China Fleet, which would be carrying millions of pounds worth of tea and porcelain.

On Valentine’s Day, 1804, the French captain saw his quarry: thirty ships moving past the Island of Pulo Aura, which sits east of the strait. This was his moment.

The commander of the British fleet, Nathaniel Dance, knew he was in trouble. He had no warships, only merchantmen. The British had some cannons (and dummy cannons designed to fool would-be attackers), but nothing to match the armaments of the French. Dance decided to try a bluff. From afar, some of the merchantmen that he commanded looked like warships. So he organized the fleet to look as if the larger ships were defending the smaller ones, and had some of his vessels raise the blue flag of the British Navy.

It was an audacious gamble, and it worked. Linois’ informants had told him to expect 23 merchant ships; he assumed that the other seven were naval vessels. Dance ordered his larger ships, which were armed with a few cannons, to open fire on the French; they exchanged shots for a while, to little effect. Then he sent his smaller vessels toward the safety of the strait and had his larger ships charge at the French squadron. Linois panicked and ran. The East India Company fleet made it to safety and rendezvoused with some actual British warships, which escorted them out of the region.

The action wasn’t one of the most important battles of the war, but the boldness of Nathaniel Dance’s gambit captured people’s imaginations. The Battle of Pulo Aura became the subject of a fair number of paintings and drawings:

Linois tried to defend his action by claiming that the convoy had been escorted by ships of the line, but he soon became a laughingstock. Napoleon, as you might imagine, was not happy. He wrote an aide a furious note:

All the enterprises at sea which have been undertaken since I became the head of the Government have missed fire because my admirals see double and have discovered, I know not how or where, that war can be made without running risks ... Tell Linois that he has shown want of courage of mind, that kind of courage which I consider the highest quality in a leader.

Linois’ unfortunate naval career came to an end in 1806 when, near the Canary Islands in the Atlantic, he demonstrated that he had overlearned the lessons of Pulo Aura. There, he once again spotted a British convoy. He assumed it was a bunch of merchant ships. This time, it wasn’t. Linois was captured. Napoleon refused to swap any British prisoners for him.

This week’s subject is not “amusing French naval blunders” (sorry). Instead, it’s the piece of geography that Charles-Alexandre Linois was trying so clumsily to secure.

Last week, we looked at the early modern history of the Strait of Malacca, centering on the city of Malacca itself. By the 19th century, Malacca was a Dutch colony, but its influence (and that of the Dutch) was in decline. The British Empire was on the rise, and it was looking for ways to control the strait. After Napoleon had been safely deposited on St. Helena, the British began looking for the best place from which to control trade between the Indian Ocean and China. They found it at Singapore.

Some British colonialists had names that were so, well, British-colonialist, that it feels a little on the nose. So it was with Sir Thomas Stamford Bingley Raffles, an official at the East India Company colony at Bencoolen, on the west coast of Sumatra. He was frustrated by continued Dutch control over Malacca and searched for a spot where the British could establish a colony to rival it.

Raffles arrived on an island at the southern end of the Strait of Malacca and discovered not only a suitable colonial possession with a good harbor but an advantageous political situation. Two brothers were locked in a succession battle over control of the area. Abdul Rahman, the sibling who had the upper hand, had aligned himself with the Dutch, so Raffles worked with his brother, Tengku Hussein. In 1819, Raffles helped Tengku outmaneuver his brother and then signed a treaty paying him a stipend in exchange for allowing the British to control Singapore Island. The island had a few hundred residents, but this drawing by a British sailor that same year portrays it as peaceful and wild:

As the British conquered more strategic points along the strait (including Malacca in 1824), the East India Company folded them all into one colony called the Straits Settlements, of which Singapore would be the capital until 1946:

Singapore might have remained a sleepy outpost had it not been for one trade good in particular: opium. The young colony needed revenue, and William Farquhar, an early governor, raised it by licensing opium sales. This led to a spat with Raffles, who was vehemently opposed to opium use (and the slave trade that Farquhar was effectively allowing as well). Raffles ousted Farquhar and tried to establish order, both legally and spatially, as he devised a “rational” city plan for the settlement.

If you look closely, you will see that part of his strategy was the ethnic segregation of the city. In these early plans, there’s a “European Town,” along with areas designated for Arabs, “Natives,” and Chinese people:

Raffles may have despised opium, but he couldn’t get rid of it. Some Chinese immigrants brought the habit with them, and the use of the drug continued in the city. More significantly, Singapore exploded in importance because of what the East India Company euphemistically called the “China Trade.”

We saw at the beginning of this post that the Company’s ships coming back from China were often full of valuable items like porcelain. But the British didn’t have much to sell to the Chinese government. By the 1830s, the East India Company had solved that problem by sending thousands of tons of opium into China. Most of the opium was moving through the Strait of Malacca, often stopping in Singapore. Perhaps it’s no coincidence that, by the 1830s, Singapore had become the most significant trade port in Southeast Asia.

Singapore boomed as its control over the Strait of Malacca made it a key node in global trade. By the 1860s, it had a bustling harbor:

The city was expanding into the hills:

And had established wide roads and fine houses for the British officials:

You wouldn’t know from these peaceful scenes that much of this lovely city was funded by an opium trade that the British would fight two wars with China to protect.

Singapore was a staging ground for merchants who were preparing to smuggle the drug into China, but local “opium farmers” (the local name for the opium traders) also bought a lot of the stuff and sold it to people in Singapore. These “farmers” were not furtive criminals; they were among the city’s most respectable citizens. The profits from the opium trade funded the growth of some of Singapore’s other industries, seeding the banks and other financial institutions that would make the city rich.

Large opium syndicates competed with each other for supremacy in the city’s politics and trade. The government tolerated them because half of Singapore’s government revenues during the 19th century came from the fees that syndicates paid for their share of the opium trade.

The prosperous city in these photos from the 1890s was built, to some extent, on the drug trade:

Singapore became an important city because of its position on a geographical chokepoint, but also because the drug trade that moved through the strait was so lucrative for so long. The opium that moved through Singapore was a key component of British colonial aims in Asia. It took Singapore a long time to shake the impact of opium. British imperial officials held the racist belief that Asian people weren’t affected by opium the same way that whites were, and did not do much about the sale of the drug until Asian people in Singapore held anti-opium protests.

It was only after World War I that the British government formed an Opium Commission:

But the battle against opium was a long one. It was still a big problem in the 1950s, and the government that took over after independence in the 1960s continued to struggle against addiction.

Singapore today continues to leverage its enviable geographical location. It’s the world’s second-largest port, widely understood to be a clean, efficient hub of global finance and shipping. But its modern significance — and that of the British Empire of which it was a cornerstone for more than a century — is built both on its history as a chokepoint and on the shadow economy that ran through it.
