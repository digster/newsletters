French Admiral Charles-Alexandre Linois was almost 7,000 miles from home, patrolling the eastern end of the Strait of Malacca in the service of Napoleon’s imperial ambitions. His goal was to disrupt British shipping through the strait. The French understood that the British economy depended on the shipping that moved through that vital chokepoint, and it was his job to intercept British vessels that tried to travel between India and China. Dutch informants told Linois when and where to expect the East India Company’s China Fleet, which would be carrying millions of pounds worth of tea and porcelain. On Valentine’s Day, 1804, the French captain saw his quarry: thirty ships moving past the Island of Pulo Aura, which sits east of the strait. This was his moment. The commander of the British fleet, Nathaniel Dance, knew he was in trouble. He had no warships, only merchantmen. The British had some cannons (and dummy cannons designed to fool would-be attackers), but nothing to match the armaments of the French. Dance decided to try a bluff. From afar, some of the merchantmen that he commanded looked like warships. So he organized the fleet to look as if the larger ships were defending the smaller ones, and had some of his vessels raise the blue flag of the British Navy. It was an audacious gamble, and it worked. Linois’ informants had told him to expect 23 merchant ships; he assumed that the other seven were naval vessels. Dance ordered his larger ships, which were armed with a few cannons, to open fire on the French; they exchanged shots for a while, to little effect. Then he sent his smaller vessels toward the safety of the strait and had his larger ships charge at the French squadron. Linois panicked and ran. The East India Company fleet made it to safety and rendezvoused with some actual British warships, which escorted them out of the region. The action wasn’t one of the most important battles of the war, but the boldness of Nathaniel Dance’s gambit captured people’s imaginations. The Battle of Pulo Aura became the subject of a fair number of paintings and drawings: Linois tried to defend his action by claiming that the convoy had been escorted by ships of the line, but he soon became a laughingstock. Linois’ unfortunate naval career came to an end in 1806 when, near the Canary Islands in the Atlantic, he demonstrated that he had overlearned the lessons of Pulo Aura. There, he once again spotted a British convoy. He assumed it was a bunch of merchant ships. This time, it wasn’t. Linois was captured. Napoleon refused to swap any British prisoners for him. So it was with Sir Thomas Stamford Bingley Raffles, an official at the East India Company colony at Bencoolen, on the west coast of Sumatra. He was frustrated by continued Dutch control over Malacca and searched for a spot where the British could establish a colony to rival it. Raffles arrived on an island at the southern end of the Strait of Malacca and discovered not only a suitable colonial possession with a good harbor but an advantageous political situation. Two brothers were locked in a succession battle over control of the area. Abdul Rahman, the sibling who had the upper hand, had aligned himself with the Dutch, so Raffles worked with his brother, Tengku Hussein. In 1819, Raffles helped Tengku outmaneuver his brother and then signed a treaty paying him a stipend in exchange for allowing the British to control Singapore Island. The young colony needed revenue, and William Farquhar, an early governor, raised it by licensing opium sales. This led to a spat with Raffles, who was vehemently opposed to opium use (and the slave trade that Farquhar was effectively allowing as well). Raffles ousted Farquhar and tried to establish order, both legally and spatially, as he devised a “rational” city plan for the settlement. If you look closely, you will see that part of his strategy was the ethnic segregation of the city. In these early plans, there’s a “European Town,” along with areas designated for Arabs, “Natives,” and Chinese people: Raffles may have despised opium, but he couldn’t get rid of it. Some Chinese immigrants brought the habit with them, and the use of the drug continued in the city. More significantly, Singapore exploded in importance because of what the East India Company euphemistically called the “China Trade.” We saw at the beginning of this post that the Company’s ships coming back from China were often full of valuable items like porcelain. But the British didn’t have much to sell to the Chinese government. By the 1830s, the East India Company had solved that problem by sending thousands of tons of opium into China. Most of the opium was moving through the Strait of Malacca, often stopping in Singapore.
