Coca: a Stimulant That Made the Modern World

Carried on the wings of coca

George Dillard
May 17
 
READ IN APP
 

I’m going to be traveling for a little while, so you probably won’t see a new post until after next weekend.

Meanwhile, our next entry in the catalog of modern stimulants is a late bloomer that had a briefer heyday and generated more controversy than things like coffee and tea…

Did you know that dozens, perhaps hundreds, of tons of coca leaves — the raw material for cocaine — are shipped into New Jersey every year? And that it’s happening right under the nose of the Drug Enforcement Administration, even though the coca leaves are a Schedule II substance and thus illegal in the United States?

That’s right. The only people who can legally bring coca into the country work at the Stepan Company, which processes the leaves for the Coca-Cola Company. The New Jersey industrial facility has special permission from the government to import these leaves and process them to create a non-psychoactive flavoring for the soda (the leftover narcotics are sent to a drug manufacturer, which uses it in a dental anesthetic).

It wasn’t always this way. A former Confederate colonel named John Pemberton devised a drink that would reduce his dependency on morphine, which he had begun taking to alleviate the pain caused by an old war wound. The beverage included kola nut, which provided caffeine, along with a mixture of cocaine and alcohol. It was called Pemberton’s French Wine Coca, and Pemberton marketed it more as a medicine than a refreshment:

Pemberton was likely trying to capitalize on the success of Vin Mariani, a very popular product created by a Corsican chemist named Angelo Mariani. It promised to make drinkers feel like this:

I quite like this ad drawn by early sci-fi artist Albert Robida showing sad people entering the “Temple de la Coca” and leaving with a spring in their step, while also touting the beverage’s supposed medicinal qualities:

Vin Mariani was pretty mainstream. Ulysses S. Grant drank it to help with his throat cancer. Thomas Edison liked it too (I’m starting to understand why he only slept 4 hours a night). Heck, even the Pope partook. Leo XIII carried a flask of Vin Mariani at all times and gave a medal to Mariani:

So John Pemberton back in Georgia had formulated his own cocaine wine to compete with Mariani’s, and it was quite popular. But he had one problem: the temperance movement was growing more powerful in the United States. In 1886, when Atlanta’s Fulton County turned dry, Pemberton needed to make a beverage that would avoid the stigma of dependence that came with alcohol. His answer was Coca-Cola, which got rid of the booze and kept the cocaine. He marketed it as an “intellectual beverage” and a “temperance drink.”

At first, Coca-Cola presented itself as a medicinal beverage, relying on the cocaine (or, more technically, ecgonine, an alkaloid precursor to cocaine) as its active ingredient. When the press criticized the drink as an intoxicant (consumers called it “dope”), the company changed the marketing to focus on refreshment rather than healing. Around the turn of the century, a backlash against cocaine prompted the company to remove the psychoactive elements of coca from its drinks.

For most of the 20th and 21st centuries, cocaine has been considered a heavy-duty, dangerous drug. But the story of Vin Mariani and Coca-Cola shows us that it wasn’t always so. How did the coca leaf travel out of the Americas? Why was it accepted at first? And what caused society to finally reject it?

I’ve never chewed coca leaf (or used cocaine, for that matter), so I’ll have to take Wade Davis at his word that chewing coca leaves is a pretty nice experience and rather unrelated to the intense experience of snorting coke:

COCA IS LESS A HIGH than a meditation. When travelers meet in the mountains of the southern Andes, they pause and exchange k’intus of coca, three perfect leaves arranged to form a cross. They then turn to face the nearest of the Apus, the protective mountain deities that hover over every community and direct the destinies of all those born in their shadows. With eyes lifted toward the summits, they bring the leaves to their mouths and blow softly, a ritual invocation that sends the essence of the plant back to the earth, the community, the sacred places, and the souls of the ancestors. The exchange of leaves is a social gesture, a way of acknowledging a human connection. But the blowing of the phukuy, as it is called, is an act of spiritual reciprocity, for in giving selflessly to the earth, the individual ensures that in time the energy of the coca will return full circle, as surely as rain falling on a field will inevitably be reborn as a cloud.

Coca has been chewed in South America for at least 5,000 years. Its leaves were thought to be sacred, and it was one of the first plants that humans domesticated in the region. The Inca gave bales of coca leaves to their vassals.

When the Spanish arrived in South America, they took notice of the many uses of coca. At first, they tried to quash its use; when that didn’t work, they tried to use it to their own advantage, giving rations of coca leaves to farm workers (like chocolate, coca had often been reserved for nobles and special occasions before the Europeans showed up).

Though they took note of coca’s energy-giving properties, Europeans didn’t seem to widely adopt the drug for a long time. This is interesting because, as we’ve seen, Europeans were very enthusiastic about adopting other drugs, especially stimulants, from other parts of the world in the early modern period.

Even though archaeologists recently found cocaine in the remains of Italian hospital patients who died in the 1600s, historians generally agree that the drug was not widespread outside of South America until the 1800s. It seems that logistics — coca leaves didn’t travel well and were often not in usable condition by the time they reached Europe — made it difficult for people to ship coca across the ocean, and thus it was not worth it for Europeans to establish the kind of plantation system for coca that they used for sugar, coffee, and tobacco.

So what happened in the 1800s? Well, in addition to faster transatlantic transportation for coca leaves, Paolo Mantegazza happened. He was the type of guy we don’t see much of anymore: a medical doctor, anthropologist, and science-fiction author. Mantegazza wrote a popular novel envisioning the year 3000, conducted an anthropological study of the Sami people in Norway, and introduced cocaine to the wider world. Returning from an anthropological trip to South America, where he had sampled coca chewing, he wrote in 1859 that:

I sneered at the poor mortals condemned to live in this valley of tears while I, carried on the wings of two leaves of coca, went flying through the spaces of 77,438 words, each more splendid than the one before…An hour later, I was sufficiently calm to write these words in a steady hand: God is unjust because he made man incapable of sustaining the effect of coca all life long. I would rather have a life span of ten years with coca than one of 10,000,000,000,000,000,000 000 centuries without coca.

Mantegazza’s coked-up enthusiasm was contagious. He believed that coca was a drug that would fundamentally transform human life, and his evangelism inspired people to try coca chewing — and to isolate the key compounds in the coca leaf. By 1860, a German chemist named Albert Niemann (who later invented mustard gas!) had published his dissertation on the extraction of cocaine. The drug soon spread through Europe.

Cocaine had, as we’ve seen with the stories of Coca-Cola and Vin Mariani, several decades — roughly between 1860 and the 1910s — when it was seen as a beneficial medicine. Cocaine was a very effective anesthetic, especially when dropped in the eyes:

But in other cases, manufacturers were quite clear about the presence of cocaine:

It even featured in perfumes:

Within a few decades, however, cocaine’s drawbacks became clear. In 1886, the British Medical Journal wrote that “we have already found out that this sweet rose of our therapeutic bouquet has its bitter thorn.” An Irish doctor noted in 1892 that “cocaine is more seductive than morphia; it fastens upon its victim more rapidly, and its hold is at least as tight.” And the drug was victimizing even the doctors that prescribed it: “up to the present time the largest number of its victims appear, unfortunately, to have been medical men.”

It took a little while, but cocaine crossed the line from medical miracle to dangerous drug. Not only were the addictive effects of the drug becoming clear, but, at least in the United States, cocaine — and its presence in Coca-Cola — became associated with racial stereotypes and crime. Historian Grace Elizabeth Hale writes that

Middle-class whites worried that soft drinks were contributing to what they saw as exploding cocaine use among African-Americans. Southern newspapers reported that “negro cocaine fiends” were raping white women, the police powerless to stop them.

So in 1902, the Coca-Cola Company decided to remove psychoactive cocaine from its signature beverage. It compensated by mixing in more sugar and caffeine. Cocaine’s brief moment in the sun was coming to an end. What had once seemed a promising stimulant and medicine was now a dangerous drug.
