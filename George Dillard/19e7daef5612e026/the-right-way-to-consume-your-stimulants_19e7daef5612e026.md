They drive old cars and buy some of their clothes at thrift shops. But a surprising number of them are coffee snobs. These people believe very firmly that there is a right way, a best way, to make and consume coffee. I must admit that I don’t quite get it, as a person who grew up in a household that drank Folgers by the gallon and (perhaps because of this) never developed much love for coffee myself. Maybe I’m missing out on the profound pleasures of coffee, but I guess my aversion to the stuff has saved me a lot of money. All of my coffee-aficionado friends have elaborate setups — expensive espresso makers, strange pots, silly-looking little cups. And, surprisingly often, they buy more equipment at what seems like significant expense, eager to try the latest coffee gear. In many ways, these people are following in a grand tradition of purchasing elaborate paraphernalia for their stimulants of choice. A lot of people, both today and in the past, have spent a lot of money to consume relatively simple substances (tea, coffee, sugar, tobacco) in the “best” way. Men would admire one another’s snuffbox collections the way that women might be encouraged to enjoy the more feminine-coded tea sets. All that expense and effort just to sip your morning tea or take a bit of snuff. The stimulants that made the modern world were clearly more than just useful chemicals to Europeans. They were symbols of a certain type of respectable lifestyle. The paraphernalia that went with them was a way to signal your social status. Just as people do today, the Europeans of the 18th and 19th centuries saw these substances as worthy of investment. And they definitely thought there was a “right” way to consume them.
