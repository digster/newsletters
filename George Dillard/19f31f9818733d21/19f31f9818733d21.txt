We’ve been looking at the “other guys” in ancient history, the societies that show up on the fringes of the Greek and Roman world. So far we’ve investigated [ https://substack.com/redirect/27c124c9-81f8-4bed-b1e3-f36baacf4218?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] Carthage [ https://substack.com/redirect/3b0f76ab-a099-45ba-86c5-51442313f80c?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] and Scythia [ https://substack.com/redirect/ab22a63d-7ee8-4e22-badc-50b1279444b2?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ]. There’s this guy who keeps popping up in the stories I want to write about. 
Josiah Wedgwood, the pottery entrepreneur, was one of the most prominent Englishmen of his time. His ideas and obsessions — and the wealth they created — had an impact on the world of the late 18th century. He helped pioneer modern consumer practices [ https://substack.com/redirect/61f22088-596e-4346-94a2-81edc107eb9f?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] by offering money-back guarantees for his products, sending out mail-order catalogs, and offering buy-one-get-one-free sales. He was a prominent abolitionist [ https://substack.com/redirect/462c1873-76af-4b59-98c9-a59b49bdff0e?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] who helped to popularize the cause. He was the grandfather of Charles Darwin! His son was a pioneer of early photography [ https://substack.com/redirect/ebbc5940-7951-45c8-8bd7-29c8d18e4d5e?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ]! 
And he really loved the Etruscans. Today, the Etruscans, to the extent that anyone thinks about them at all, are regarded as also-rans. They dominated parts of Italy alongside the early Romans until Rome absorbed their lands — an area roughly between modern-day Milan and Rome — into its growing empire. The consensus seems to be that their art wasn’t as good as the Greeks’ and their military wasn’t as powerful as the Romans’, so they generally get a polite nod from historians on the way to the civilizations that get much more attention.
This wasn’t always the case. In the late 1700s, there was a period of what historian Lucy Shipley calls “Etruscomania [ https://substack.com/redirect/235f85af-0e2e-4a0d-9ed0-ada194fc0459?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ],” in which Europeans became obsessed with the civilization. Collectors came back from their Grand Tours [ https://substack.com/redirect/30556f7c-cc42-40f5-be91-757525107e71?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] with crates full of Etruscan artifacts. Rich people styled entire rooms of their homes in the “Etruscan” style.  
Josiah Wedgwood drove a lot of this interest. His company manufactured a great deal of Etruscan-style pottery. It was such a big part of his business that he built a factory called the Etruria Works; he lived next door in Etruria Hall. The motto of the place was Artes Etruriae Renascuntur — Etrurian Arts are Reborn. The Etruria Works pumped out neoclassical vases for people who couldn’t afford to bring back the real thing from Italy. They generally featured silhouettes of mythological figures:
Although sometimes they mimicked red-figure pottery:
Wait, you might say — this stuff looks like an attempt to recreate Greek pottery, not whatever the Etruscans may have made. You’d be at least partially correct: a great deal of Greek pottery ended up in Etruscan tombs, and Europeans in the 1700s thought it was Etruscan-made rather than imported. Until Johann Winckelmann came along. 
Winckelmann was one of the first serious art historians and archaeologists; he wrote a very influential tome called The History of Art in Antiquity that surveyed and not-so-subtly judged the art of various classical civilizations. In it, he argued that much of what was thought to be Etruscan was probably Greek. He was right about that, but he used this idea to dismiss Etruscan art altogether. The Etruscans made art that was simplistic and derivative. The good stuff was from Egypt, Rome, and Greece. 
Etruscomania shuddered to a halt, and the Etruscans became a second-class civilization, useful only for providing a baseline against which to measure the achievements of Rome and Greece. 
But this gives short shrift to the Etruscans. They’re more interesting and more mysterious than that.
Sometimes you come across a face from the ancient world and want to know more about it than you can. 
This is an Etruscan cosmetics container in the shape of a female head. Who was she? Archaeologists speculate that she may have represented Turan, the Etruscan love goddess. Or maybe Lasa, a winged goddess who often accompanied Turan. Perhaps it’s meant to be a portrait of the woman who owned it. We’ll never know for sure.
This sort of uncertainty comes with the territory when you start looking at Etruscan artifacts. We can’t read much of their writing because Latin eventually replaced it, and only short inscriptions on stone survived. As far as anybody can tell, Etruscan was — very unusually — unrelated to any other known languages, making it difficult for linguists to decode. Much of our understanding of the Etruscan language comes from the 1964 discovery of three gold tablets with a bilingual inscription in Etruscan and Phoenician:
But scholars still only understand a few hundred words of the language. They can sound out the syllables, but don’t know what they mean. All other information about the Etruscans comes from Roman sources, which are likely biased. So we have to rely on artifacts to make sense of Etruscan culture.

Several Mediterranean cultures used anatomical votives to the gods, offering up a representation of a body part in the hopes that the gods would heal it.  But only the Etruscans offered these entire torsos, demonstrating their facility with art and their knowledge of anatomy.

Because we don’t know much about the Etruscans, people have tended to project their own wishes onto them. This helps to explain the fierceness of the debate over the Etruscans’ origins. Some scholars have speculated that they were native Italians, while others, noting the uniqueness of the Etruscan language (and following the lead of ancient authors like Herodotus), have argued for an origin much farther east. 
When Napoleon’s brother Lucien, an amateur archaeologist, opened an Etruscan tomb near Vulci, he was quick to associate what he found there with the Egyptian culture his brother had conquered. The “Tomb of Isis,” as he called it,  did contain some Egyptian goods, but they’d been imported through trade and the statue of “Isis” turned out to just be a depiction of an Etruscan woman:
In the 1930s and 1940s, the Mussolini government pushed archaeologists to study the Etruscans, hoping to boost nationalism by finding proof that they were native Italians. Massimo Pallottino — who discovered the gold Etruscan-language plates above — published an important work arguing that the Etruscans were both historically significant and, indeed, Italy’s forefathers. 
The debate over who the Etruscans were and what they meant raged on in the second half of the 20th century. Italian communists of the 1950s led a backlash against this nationalist interpretation, calling it a fascist fantasy and demoting [ https://substack.com/redirect/0e8a793d-68a5-4af7-bd1e-2856f7caee3c?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] the Etruscans to a second-tier status. Pallottino tried to re-interpret the Etruscans in a Cold War context, presenting them as the origins of Western European culture. Others like Elie Wiesel saw the Etruscans as an oppressed people to whom downtrodden 20th-century Jews might relate.
Recently, scientists have found DNA evidence [ https://substack.com/redirect/0cc14ff9-1bf7-499e-bcc5-6627e9d73642?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] that seems to indicate that the Etruscans were, indeed, native to Italy. But there’s so much else we don’t know, and probably never will. So much ink has been spilled ranking the Etruscans in comparison to the main characters of the ancient world, the Greeks and the Romans, or trying to project modern sensibilities onto them. Perhaps now it’s time to simply appreciate the beautiful things they left behind.
