Lloyd’s of London famously got its start as a coffee shop in London where sailors and merchants caffeinated themselves and traded news about the shipping industry. Shipping was a notoriously risky business, requiring large upfront investments that could be lost due to a single storm or act of piracy. Therefore, the people who gathered at Lloyd’s began to trade not just news about the ships coming into port but insurance contracts.
In 1769, a group of people established a rival coffeehouse called, uncreatively, “New Lloyd’s [ https://substack.com/redirect/ee8921e7-827f-48f2-8bf9-68196aa76b85?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ].” They felt the need to start over because the insurance industry (and therefore “Old Lloyd’s”) had become synonymous with ghoulish gambling.
Today, the insurance industry is about as staid and boring as it gets, but in its early days, the line between prudent risk mitigation and wild speculation was not so clear. While some of the people at Lloyd’s were soberly hedging the risk of their business ventures, other people were simply betting on which ships would sink out at sea and which would not. It was all part of the same market.
An 1891 history summarized [ https://substack.com/redirect/6e2c0174-34c7-4f4a-a4c9-9d7cba7f9196?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] the problem:
At this time and far on into the 18th century Lloyd’s Coffee-house was a free place of meeting, without any controlling authority or established regulations, and consequently in the speculative era engendered by the South Sea and kindred schemes there grew up alongside the legitimate merchants and brokers a set of speculative adventurers and gamblers which threatened to bring discredit upon all who frequented the place.
The blurred distinction between gambling and insurance was especially problematic when it came to what today we’d call life insurance. It was not uncommon for people to buy into schemes that would pay out on a person’s death. But, for centuries, you could take out a life insurance policy on anyone, even if you didn’t know them. There was no effective legal or ethical boundary in those days between betting on a stranger’s death and preparing for the financial burden of losing your family’s breadwinner.
This is not to say that all betting on who lived or died was considered morally acceptable. In the 1400s, the Venetian Senate [ https://substack.com/redirect/132e950f-74ac-47f0-b85a-ff8935587edd?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] banned betting on when the Pope would pass away, and the Genoese government went even further, telling citizens they could not bet on the lives of kings or nobles, either. In some places, life insurance was so connected with gambling — and the possibility that it might be in a policyholder’s financial interests to hasten the death of someone whose life they had insured — that it was banned altogether.
But not in England, where the punters or investors (whatever you want to call them) at Lloyd’s were free to speculate on the survival of crews at sea or the demise of a prominent person. Some people took out policies on the lives of people who owed them money, so that they’d get paid either way. Others simply made a bet on the likelihood that an unhealthy-seeming stranger or acquaintance would keel over soon.
Even in England, this left a bad taste in people’s mouths. There were practical problems in this time of imperfect recordkeeping because it was difficult to prove that someone had died. And was it really Christian to have a financial incentive to root for the death of another human being?
This was the concern that drove the founders of New Lloyd’s (which eventually grew into the Lloyd’s of London that exists today). They wanted to separate the insurance industry from its reputation as a bunch of unethical death-gamblers. The reformers eventually got help from Parliament, which passed [ https://substack.com/redirect/75c3c510-7938-4058-908a-bee4250ee8db?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] a law in 1774 that required someone to have an “insurable interest” in the subject of their life insurance policy:
Whereas it hath been found by experience that the making insurances on lives or other events wherein the assured shall have no interest hath introduced a mischievous kind of gaming… after the passing of this Act no insurance shall be made by any person or persons, bodies politick or corporate, on the life or lives of any person or persons, or on any other event or events whatsoever, wherein the person or persons for whose use, benefit, or on whose account such policy or policies shall be made, shall have no interest, or by way of gaming or wagering.
The government had finally ended the unsavory practice of profiting from someone else’s death. Sort of.

In the seventh season of The Simpsons, Grandpa Abe becomes paranoid that Mr. Burns is trying to kill him. You see, the World War II unit that both men had served in, the Flying Hellfish, had discovered a cache of priceless Nazi paintings. They’d agreed to hide the paintings until all but one of them had died, leaving the last man to reap the windfall. Abe and Burns were the last two men left:
As Ox explains in the video, the men were agreeing to enter into a “tontine,” in which a group of investors pools its money and, as people die, their shares devolve to the surviving members. The last man standing gets it all.
The scheme was invented by a 17th-century Italian banker named Lorenzo De Tonti, who proposed [ https://substack.com/redirect/2e80d192-465a-45b5-8fb5-9ef2e58c212e?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] it as a way for Louis XIV to raise money for his wars. The original plan envisioned a group of people investing principal and drawing interest from the investment as long as they lived. The rate of interest would go up as the number of investors declined, but the king would keep the principal when the last man had died. People were essentially betting on who would live the longest.
De Tonti never actually saw his idea happen in real life, but soon after he passed away in 1684, France began implementing tontines. England and Ireland followed suit. Some of them followed De Tonti’s formula, and others looked like the pact between Abe Simpson and his fellow Hellfish.
As tontines got more lucrative in the 1700s, they became more baroque. Some tontines allowed investors to nominate someone besides themselves who might live a long time. Others were based on random groups of people (one Genevan issue was tied to the life expectancies of 50 girls) or prominent figures (the lives of George III and Marie Antoinette were both included [ https://substack.com/redirect/2e80d192-465a-45b5-8fb5-9ef2e58c212e?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] in tontines). People could buy and sell their shares or pass them on to their descendants as long as the tontine was not based on their lives. Savvy investors consulted doctors to give them the odds — did this four-year-old girl look like she was going to live a long time?
For a long time, the tontine was considered a reputable financial instrument. It crossed the Atlantic to the new United States; Alexander Hamilton proposed a tontine to help pay down the debt from the Revolutionary War. The “Tontine Coffee House [ https://substack.com/redirect/0f0b05c2-942f-4b41-a099-494f0c4317c4?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ],” named after the type of investment that made it possible, was founded as a sort of New York version of Lloyd’s. It was the site of what would become the New York Stock Exchange.
But there were problems. People tried to conceal deaths from the government lest the payouts stop. Investors complained when it seemed like participants were living too long. There were works of fiction about murder plots connected to tontines, even though there seem to have been few actual cases of tontine-related murder; stories like Robert Louis Stevenson’s *The Wrong Box [ https://substack.com/redirect/6a1aa1a1-74f5-49ef-94d7-31ab73409a2d?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ]* were precursors of the tale of Abe Simpson, Montgomery Burns, and the Fighting Hellfish.
By the late 1800s, tontines, like the early forms of life insurance, were starting to seem unethical. An 1886 work [ https://substack.com/redirect/2e80d192-465a-45b5-8fb5-9ef2e58c212e?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] on insurance criticized the tontine as unfair and morally questionable:
This form may be fitly characterized as the gambling form, inasmuch as the only hope of profit to a few is that the many will be robbed of their savings, Tontine insurance is profitable to the few in just proportion that misfortune shall overtake those who participate in it. No man would risk large payments with the certainty of losing all if he should fail to make one such payment in a term of years, if he were not ticked by the hope that others would be the unfortunate ones compelled by circumstances to discontinue and lose all, while he would be the exception and profit by their loss.
By the early 1900s, progressive reformers in some states outlawed the tontine. But, even where it was still legal, the practice declined in many parts of the world, replaced by corporate insurance and other investment vehicles that didn’t require investors to root for the deaths of other human beings.
You may have read so far and been appalled by the willingness of our ancestors to wager on matters of life and death for other humans.
Or you may have a Polymarket account, which you can use to bet on anything from the winners at the Oscars to the fall of the Iranian regime. If so, you may feel at home gambling on the deasth of others.
“Prediction markets” like Polymarket and Kalshi allow members to bet on pretty much anything. You can put down money on whether Joe Rogan [ https://substack.com/redirect/d1b00811-8fa9-40d2-90cb-b114c53b9582?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] will say “holy shit” during his next podcast, whether inflation [ https://substack.com/redirect/b8d7fab3-3d48-47e6-ae4b-d9939e0d8db8?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] will top 2.5%, or whether Iran [ https://substack.com/redirect/b2f39bdc-a46c-4da1-b012-37e514d94f69?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] will strike Turkey this month.
Polymarket is aware that non-degenerates might find the fact that it provides betting markets on death and destruction in the Middle East to be, well, gross. It does seem to have some standards — it took down a market [ https://substack.com/redirect/6afaf2f4-7f96-443c-b2a0-7f69ffbc4a1e?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] on whether there would be a nuclear detonation somewhere in the world and appended a disclaimer [ https://substack.com/redirect/b2f39bdc-a46c-4da1-b012-37e514d94f69?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] to some of its other betting propositions:
Note on Middle East Markets: The promise of prediction markets is to harness the wisdom of the crowd to create accurate, unbiased forecasts for the most important events to society. That ability is particularly invaluable in gut-wrenching times like today. After discussing with those directly affected by the attacks, who had dozens of questions, we realized that prediction markets could give them the answers they needed in ways TV news and 𝕏 could not.
Sure, whatever. Prediction markets like these have long been controversial. An early, experimental government effort called the Policy Analysis Market [ https://substack.com/redirect/2cc98ca2-7f9e-4d17-bf69-5a6e41576e4a?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] was disbanded in 2003 after legislators got wind of the fact that it might offer betting on a possible assassination of Yasser Arafat or a North Korean missile strike. Senator Ron Wyden said [ https://substack.com/redirect/91b32b70-e827-45b6-baee-e3787c328937?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] that “The idea of a federal betting parlor on atrocities and terrorism is ridiculous and it’s grotesque.” The project was canceled and its director fired.
Things have changed since 2003. More and more of our society has become commoditized and gamified, and we can now bet on pretty much everything. Sports betting, which paved the way for a lot of this, is bad enough — the ability to make esoteric prop bets is eroding fans’ relationships to their teams, and many gamblers have developed debilitating addictions.
It’s one thing to invite the moral strain of betting on your hometown team to lose. It’s another to wager on whether there will be American “boots on the ground [ https://substack.com/redirect/4dc0a32e-83e0-4796-8b90-f8ccb24ddaf4?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ]” in Iran. These markets are especially problematic when it seems like people are engaging in insider trading. Very suspicious (and profitable) bets were placed on the capture [ https://substack.com/redirect/819dfae6-2c29-4d18-ba81-d3d339f5c530?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] of Nicolas Maduro and American airstrikes [ https://substack.com/redirect/e8c5a8c9-2cf7-4786-93b0-6fc775037cbf?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] on Iran right before they happened.
More than one billion dollars [ https://substack.com/redirect/fde31946-8f66-4b5d-9edb-7aada941b7b2?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] has been wagered on the Iran conflict so far. Even though it is illegal in the United States to participate in a “death market,” much of that money is riding on matters of life and death anyway. People bet on propositions like when Ayatollah Khamenei would be “out” as leader and whether Israel would invade Lebanon.
In *What Money Can’t Buy [ https://substack.com/redirect/480f6021-711b-4615-b6bd-dd6d13c31545?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ]: The Moral Limits of Markets*, Michael Sandel wrote that
[W]hen we decide that certain goods may be bought and sold, we decide, at least implicitly, that it is appropriate to treat them as commodities, as instruments of profit and use. But not all goods are properly valued in this way. The most obvious example is human beings. Slavery was appalling because it treated human beings as commodities, to be bought and sold at auction. Such treatment fails to value human beings in the appropriate way — as persons worthy of dignity and respect, rather than as instruments of gain and objects of use.
While the betting on prediction markets doesn’t have the same moral valence as slavery, it’s closer than its practitioners might want to think. Gambling on the suffering and death of real people certainly has the stink that affixed itself to gambling on the lives of others in the 1600s and 1700s. It’s dehumanizing and ghoulish. The prediction markets and the people who run them should know better.
