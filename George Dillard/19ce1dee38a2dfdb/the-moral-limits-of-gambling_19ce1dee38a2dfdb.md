The blurred distinction between gambling and insurance was especially problematic when it came to what today we’d call life insurance. It was not uncommon for people to buy into schemes that would pay out on a person’s death. But, for centuries, you could take out a life insurance policy on anyone, even if you didn’t know them. There was no effective legal or ethical boundary in those days between betting on a stranger’s death and preparing for the financial burden of losing your family’s breadwinner. This is not to say that all betting on who lived or died was considered morally acceptable. Some people took out policies on the lives of people who owed them money, so that they’d get paid either way. Others simply made a bet on the likelihood that an unhealthy-seeming stranger or acquaintance would keel over soon. Even in England, this left a bad taste in people’s mouths. There were practical problems in this time of imperfect recordkeeping because it was difficult to prove that someone had died.
