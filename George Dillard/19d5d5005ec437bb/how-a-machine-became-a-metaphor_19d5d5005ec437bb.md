Nicolas Jacques Pelletier would almost certainly be lost to history if he had died another way. It seems like he was a common criminal. It’s not even totally clear what his transgressions were, but robbery and assault are a good bet. He was convicted of his crimes on New Year’s Eve, 1791. The sentence was death. But what kind of death? Capital punishment was common in early modern France, and executions were always carried out in public. An execution was considered by many to be an enjoyable afternoon out, as you can see in this 1720 engraving from the Netherlands: But the specific fate of a condemned criminal depended on his or her status. Most often, criminals would be hanged. If the offender was of noble birth, they were entitled to decapitation, which was supposed to involve less suffering as long as the executioner knew what he was doing. The French Revolution had taken hold, and he would be the first man to lose his life to the guillotine. Pelletier was taken to the public scaffold wearing the red shirt of a murderer, in full view of the huge crowd that had gathered to see this new mechanism of death. The whole thing took a few quick, efficient seconds. The blade came down, and the criminal’s head plopped into a basket. The crowd filtered away, The machine that killed Pelletier would go on to kill thousands more, but it was more than just a machine. The guillotine would, rightly or wrongly, come to symbolize the French Revolution — but its exact meaning remains up for grabs to this day. The It was decided that decapitation was the most efficient way to end a life, but executioners complained that beheading someone with a sword was an unpredictable and potentially messy endeavor. The process needed to be systematized, so Guillotin — who was actually opposed to the death penalty but reasoned that, if he couldn’t abolish it, he should work to minimize its cruelty — proposed a beheading machine. There’s a bit more to the history of the device, though, which complicates this Guillotin-centric story. The actual device used in France was designed by a Dr. Antoine Louis; at first people called it, perhaps more accurately, a “louisette.” He tested it on corpses from the morgue and live animals until he was satisfied that the machine would do the job. Louis based his design on beheading machines very similar to the guillotine, which had existed for some time. There was the Halifax Gibbet, for example, which had been used in Britain for centuries: And this machine in a 1512 print of the execution of St. Matthew by Lucas Cranach the Elder: So Guillotin, whose name will forever be associated with machines like this, didn’t invent them — he merely came up with the idea of making them more efficient, which is to say more reliably deadly. He inspired others to introduce a diagonally sloped blade and spend a lot of time figuring out the most effective heights and weights. Then they painted his machine red and sent it out to end Pelletier’s life. What did the guillotine represent?
