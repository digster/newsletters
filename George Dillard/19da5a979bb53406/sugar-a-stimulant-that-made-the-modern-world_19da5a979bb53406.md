I keep my sugar in an old plastic canister in my pantry, part of a mismatched set of containers with various flours and other baking ingredients. I have never once wanted to display my sugar to guests. I don’t think they’d be impressed that I own up to four pounds of the stuff at any given time, purchased at the luxury price of $1.99. But if I lived a few centuries ago, I might do things differently. Sugar, to Europeans in the early modern period, wasn’t just an ingredient in desserts; it was both a luxury good and an important medicine. But maybe it makes more sense if we think of these items as drug paraphernalia. If we classify sugar not as a commodity foodstuff but as a relatively rare, new, and exciting stimulant — one of many mood-altering and often addictive substances that came flooding into Europe during this period — perhaps we can understand what was going on a bit better. Sugar had a similar trajectory to a lot of drugs: at a certain point it crossed a threshold and went from being something that was hard to access or only available in an adulterated form (i.e., fruit) to being quite plentiful and available in a purer form. Though we tend not to think of sugar as being in the same class as cocaine or nicotine, it does share some characteristics. Plantation slavery in the New World was, more than anything else, the development that made sugar so cheap and plentiful in the 1700s. And while most British consumers were happy not to think very hard about the ethical implications of their sweet treats, some people did. As the abolitionist movement grew in Britain, so did criticism of the sugar trade. Some people, calling themselves the free produce movement — or, as wags called them, the anti-saccharites — decided to stop consuming sugar produced with slave labor. They would use their consumer power to bring about social change. That social change would also have to be political.
