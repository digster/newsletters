I keep my sugar in an old plastic canister in my pantry, part of a mismatched set of containers with various flours and other baking ingredients. I have never once wanted to display my sugar to guests. I don’t think they’d be impressed that I own up to four pounds of the stuff at any given time, purchased at the luxury price of $1.99.

But if I lived a few centuries ago, I might do things differently. Sugar, to Europeans in the early modern period, wasn’t just an ingredient in desserts; it was both a luxury good and an important medicine. When it first made its way into Europe from Asia, sugar was far more valuable and important than most other ingredients. People believed that it helped to balance the body’s humors and took it as a healing agent.

But as sugar became more common in Europe — crusaders brought it back with them from the Middle East and planted it on farms around Venice — the wealthy people who could afford it began to flaunt the fact that they owned sugar. In 1457, a nobleman named Gaston of Foix gave an extravagant feast, at which he and his guests ate an array of meats, each brought into the room on elaborate conveyances, like a miniature castle wheeled into the room by 12 attendants. Guests were served swans and geese, fried oranges and tarts. And then at the end of the meal came an entire zoo’s worth of animals molded out of sugar. Each sugar animal held in its hand the crest of the King of Hungary, that night’s honored guest.

The presentation of elaborate sweets that Gaston and his guests enjoyed became a standard part of aristocratic tradition. The word “banquet” originally referred not to a big meal, as it does today, but to a dessert course (or standalone event) in which people would indulge in an endless array of sweet treats. It’s a bit weird to see people spending their precious money on such elaborate accessories for a household ingredient. But maybe it makes more sense if we think of these items as drug paraphernalia.

If we classify sugar not as a commodity foodstuff but as a relatively rare, new, and exciting stimulant — one of many mood-altering and often addictive substances that came flooding into Europe during this period — perhaps we can understand what was going on a bit better. Sugar had a similar trajectory to a lot of drugs: at a certain point it crossed a threshold and went from being something that was hard to access or only available in an adulterated form (i.e., fruit) to being quite plentiful and available in a purer form.

Though we tend not to think of sugar as being in the same class as cocaine or nicotine, it does share some characteristics. It can be habit-forming, even addictive. It does give us a rush (the telling name for which is a “sugar high”). And we consume it even when it’s clearly bad for us. You could make a strong argument that, though it’s at the mild end, sugar belongs on the spectrum of stimulants that swept the world during this period.

By the 1700s, regular people had access to plenty of pure, uncut sugar. James Walvin writes that people’s sugar intake rose rapidly:

In a little more than a century, the sugar consumed by people in England and Wales increased sixty-fold, while the population scarcely doubled in the same period. The per capita consumption of sugar in 1700 was 4lb, rising to 8lb in 1729, 12lb in 1789 and 18lb by 1809.

This was nothing, by the way, in comparison to how much sugar people like you and me eat. Today, British people consume almost 100 pounds of sugar per year. Americans approach 150 pounds, while Australian consumption tops 200 pounds per year.

Here’s another sugar bowl, striking in a different way:

The bowl advertises the fact that, unlike most sugar coming into England at the time, its contents were not produced by enslaved people. Plantation slavery in the New World was, more than anything else, the development that made sugar so cheap and plentiful in the 1700s. And while most British consumers were happy not to think very hard about the ethical implications of their sweet treats, some people did.

As the abolitionist movement grew in Britain, so did criticism of the sugar trade. Some people, calling themselves the free produce movement — or, as wags called them, the anti-saccharites — decided to stop consuming sugar produced with slave labor. They would use their consumer power to bring about social change.

That social change would also have to be political. And vivid drawings of the cruelty that slaves faced in the sugar industry:

The workers themselves rebelled, as well. This flag was flown by people who rose up in Bussa’s Rebellion, a revolt by slaves on the sugar plantations of Barbados in 1816. It was designed to reassure British soldiers that the rebels did not want independence from the British Empire; they only wanted to end the cruel system of slavery on the sugar plantations. You will not be surprised to learn that the British military did not care for such subtleties, and crushed the revolt, killing hundreds.

The free produce movement was only somewhat successful. Its initial push, in the early 1790s, did have an impact. Hundreds of thousands of British people participated, and sugar sales fell sharply — but only for a little while.

A category of “free-labour” sugar did emerge, but it remained a niche product. The free-labour sugar came from India, which wasn’t exactly a land of liberty either, and was considered to be inferior in quality. Moreover, in the absence of labeling laws, consumers could never be quite sure whether their sugar really had been produced by free people. Nevertheless, the movement had an impact on the general culture in England. Cartoonists like James Gillray made fun of the famously frugal King George III by linking him with the “anti-saccharites.” The joke was that his skimping on sugar had nothing to do with ethics; he was just cheap.

Just after Christmas in 1831, huge fires burned at the Kensington Estate in Jamaica. It wasn’t an accident; it was a signal that the slave rebellion later known as the Baptist War had begun. The fires soon swept across many of the sugar plantations on the island. Here, the Roehampton Estate, a sugar plantation owned by an absentee British landlord, burns as triumphant rebels celebrate in the foreground:

This rebellion, like most slave rebellions, did not last. Militias crushed the resistance, and hundreds of rebels were killed. But the Baptist War turned out to be the final straw for popular opinion back in England. It seemed impossible, both practically and morally, to maintain slavery any longer. Parliament abolished slavery in August, 1833. The family that owned Roehampton was paid £5,745 for the human property that it lost; the freed slaves were given nothing.

Though slavery had ended in the British Empire, the stimulant that it had made cheap and accessible continued to rule the world. Sugar from the Caribbean was eventually replaced with sugar from beets and sugar cane subsidized by government largesse. By the 20th century, the world was awash in cheap sugar, much to the delight of consumers and the chagrin of our dentists and doctors. The average American now eats a kilogram of sugar a week. We’re still hooked on the stuff.
