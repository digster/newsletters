In 1604, English King James I warned his subjects of a dangerous and disgusting habit that was harming the kingdom. He wrote,

Have you not reason then to bee ashamed, and to forbeare this filthie noveltie, so basely grounded, so foolishly received and so grossely mistaken in the right use thereof? In your abuse thereof sinning against God, harming your selves both in persons and goods, and raking also thereby the markes and notes of vanitie upon you: by the custome thereof making your selves to be wondered at by all forraine civil Nations, and by all strangers that come among you, to be scorned and contemned. A custome lothsome to the eye, hatefull to the Nose, harmefull to the braine, dangerous to the Lungs, and in the blacke stinking fume thereof, neerest resembling the horrible Stigian smoke of the pit that is bottomelesse.

This was the closing to his wonderfully-named Counterblaste to Tobacco, a rant against smoking and smokers. King James didn’t like the smell of tobacco (users “shall be filthily corrupted with an incurable stinke”), its dirtiness ( he was horrified that people would smoke “at the table, a place of respect, of cleanlinesse, of modestie”), or its effect on women (a smoker would “reduce thereby his delicate, wholesome, and cleane complexioned wife, to that extremitie, that either shee must also corrupt her sweete breath therewith, or else resolve to live in a perpetuall stinking torment”).

On the other hand, about a year later, an army captain named Tobias Hume wrote a song called “Tobacco Is Like Love:”

Here, Hume compares the joys — and pitfalls — of falling for tobacco, comparing it to the way that romance can make life worth living while providing plenty of danger:

Tobacco, Tobacco, Sing sweetly for Tobacco,

Tobacco is like love,

O love it For you see I will prove it.

Love maketh leane the fatte mens tumor, So doth Tobacco,

Love still dries uppe the wanton humor, So doth Tobacco,

Love makes men sayle from shore to shore, So doth Tobacco,

Tis fond love often makes men poor, So doth Tobacco,

Love makes men scorne all Coward feares, So doth Tobacco,

Love often sets men by the eares, So doth Tobacco.

Tobaccoe, Tobaccoe sing sweetely for Tobaccoe,

Tobaccoe is like Love, O love it, For you see I have provde it.

Some writers saw the stuff not as dangerous or enticing but as a magic medicine. Nicolas Monardes, a Spanish doctor, wrote enthusiastically about tobacco in his Ioyfvll newes out of the new-found worlde, published in 1565. According to him, tobacco served as a miracle drug for Native American cultures. It was good for toothaches:

In the Toothache when the griefe commeth of a colde cause, or of colde Rumes, putting to it a little ball made of the leafe of the Tabaco, washing first the tooth with a small cloth wet in the Iuyce, it taketh away the payne, and stay∣eth it, that the putrifaction goe not forwarde.

It was a cure for poison (“In Uenom and venomous wounds our Tabaco hath great commendation”) and for curing ordinary injuries:

In woundes newely hurt, and cuttes, strokes, prickes, or any other manner of wounde, our Tabaco worketh mar∣uellous effectes, for that it doeth heale them and maketh them sound…

Here in this Country, & in this City they know not what other to doe, hauing cut or hurt themselues, but to runne to the Tabaco, as to a most readie remedie. It doth maruellous workes, without any need of other Surge∣ry, but this only hearbe. In restrayning the fluxe of blood of the wounds it procureth most maruellous workes, for that ye Iuyce and the Leaues being stamped, are sufficient to re∣straine any fluxe of blood.

One thing was clear: this plant seemed interesting and important. To burn it and inhale its smoke (sure, you could snort it or chew it, too, but the smoking was what seemed to capture the imagination) was, depending on your point of view, either enticing or repellent, restorative or deadly.

It was, most of all, exotic and new — part of a class of new intoxicants that captured the imagination of Europeans in the early modern era. Along with chocolate, coffee, tea, opium, and coca, tobacco helped usher in what might be considered a golden age of mood-altering substances.

So how did the use of tobacco make its way across the Atlantic, and how did the spread of this drug shape societies around the world?

In many Native American societies, tobacco was much more than a recreational drug. Much, perhaps most, of its use was for medical applications (tobacco was often mixed with other plants and burned) or ceremonial purposes. As historian Iain Gately writes, the stuff was used everywhere, in all sorts of ways:

Tobacco was sniffed, chewed, eaten, drunk, smeared over bodies, used in eye drops and enemas, and smoked. It was blown into warriors’ faces before battle, over fields before planting and over women prior to sex, it was offered to the gods, and accepted as their gift, and not least it served as a simple narcotic for daily use by men and women.

It’s worth noting that these ads less often acknowledged another fact about the New World — the way tobacco was produced in Virginia and other places with enslaved people’s labor:

The tobacco craze turned the plant from a curiosity into a global industry, the first of the colonial cash crops. And tobacco production was exceptionally labor-intensive. The difficult work of planting, tending, harvesting, and processing the crop was done first by indentured servants and then, increasingly, by slaves from Africa.

Though the scale of the tobacco industry — and the slavery that it engendered — was later dwarfed by other cash crops like cotton, tobacco served as a test run of sorts for colonial industries. The success of tobacco not only showed that there was a market for addictive, intoxicating substances, but it also demonstrated that wealth and empire could be built on the labor of enslaved people.

Europeans’ obsession with tobacco started almost immediately upon their contact with the New World. Native people offered tobacco leaves to Spanish sailors when they first made landfall. Spanish priest Bartolome de las Casas reported that Christopher Columbus’s men had been fascinated by

men with half-burned wood in their hands and certain herbs to take their smokes… and having lighted one part of it, by the other they suck, absorb, or receive that smoke inside with the breath, by which they become benumbed and almost drunk, and so it is said they do not feel fatigue.

But the Spanish were also well aware that the “herb” was dangerously addictive. Las Casas wrote that:

I have known Spaniards on the island of Hispaniola, who were accustomed to taking [cigars] and who, being reproved and told that this was a vice, replied that they were not able to stop.

Part of the fascination came from the act of smoking. Europeans were used to drinking their intoxicants, and the idea of breathing in smoke seemed strange — Columbus’s crewmembers who returned to Spain, exhaling tobacco smoke, were regarded as devilish.

But, soon, Europeans started to see tobacco as useful. Here, Jean Nicot (from whom we get the word nicotine) presents the plant to Catherine de Medici in 1560, touting its medicinal qualities:

Though some Europeans used tobacco for its medicinal qualities — as we’ve seen, there was a long and vociferous debate between those who thought it was helpful and those who saw it as harmful — most used it for its intoxicating effects. How did tobacco go from being an alien custom to a widespread, socially accepted habit?

First, there were the influencers. A few prominent men like Nicot and Walter Raleigh, who made smoking fashionable in the court of Elizabeth I, established tobacco as an acceptable and enjoyable drug. Soon, smoking tobacco was a cosmopolitan practice of sophisticated people rather than a devilish foreign habit.

Smoking tobacco also fit nicely into 18th-century consumer and social culture, which saw men gathering in public spaces like pubs, coffeehouses, and cafes to ingest mildly intoxicating substances, most of which served as stimulants that fueled the political and intellectual ferment of the time. Smoking went well with coffee and conversation; what better way to while away an afternoon of shooting the breeze than by puffing on a pipe?

There was also a great deal of money to be made, and companies like those that created the early advertisements above played an important role in the establishment of transatlantic empires. The tobacco trade helped to build the British Empire, and the government eventually realized that it had a strong motive to encourage tobacco consumption so that the crown could tax it.

Even James I — he of the Counterblaste — eventually reconciled himself to taxing tobacco rather than banning it. By the 1660s, David Courtwright tells us, tobacco taxes made up “fully a quarter of England’s customs revenue and perhaps 5 percent of the government’s total income.” Tobacco was one of the building blocks of British global supremacy.

These factors — along with the plainly addictive nature of nicotine — converged to establish smoking in Europe. I’ll leave you with some images of tobacco culture as it had established itself in Europe by the 1700s.

Their nicotine fix was the result of a global network of commerce, oppression, and imperialism that created and maintained this social craze.
