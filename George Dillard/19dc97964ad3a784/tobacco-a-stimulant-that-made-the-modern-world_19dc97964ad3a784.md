A few prominent men like Nicot and Walter Raleigh, who made smoking fashionable in the court of Elizabeth I, established tobacco as an acceptable and enjoyable drug. Soon, smoking tobacco was a cosmopolitan practice of sophisticated people rather than a devilish foreign habit. Smoking tobacco also fit nicely into 18th-century consumer and social culture, which saw men gathering in public spaces like pubs, coffeehouses, and cafes to ingest mildly intoxicating substances, most of which served as stimulants that fueled the political and intellectual ferment of the time. Smoking went well with coffee and conversation; what better way to while away an afternoon of shooting the breeze than by puffing on a pipe? There was also a great deal of money to be made, and companies like those that created the early advertisements above played an important role in the establishment of transatlantic empires. The tobacco trade helped to build the British Empire, and the government eventually realized that it had a strong motive to encourage tobacco consumption so that the crown could tax it. Even James I — he of the Counterblaste — eventually reconciled himself to taxing tobacco rather than banning it. By the 1660s, These factors — along with the plainly addictive nature of nicotine — converged to establish smoking in Europe.
