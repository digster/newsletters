Yokohama: Glimpses of a New Japan

A city on the edge of dramatic change

George Dillard
Oct 25
 
READ IN APP
 

We’ve visited Russia and Texas in our series about places on the verge of big change. Now we’re headed to Japan, which underwent dramatic change in the 1860s.

One late-summer day in 1862 on the Tokaido, the road that connected Kyoto and Edo (now known as Tokyo), a party of four British travelers happened upon the 400-member entourage of a Japanese nobleman, Shimazi Hisamitsu. He was the regent of Satsuma, which made him one of Japan’s most powerful lords.

It was Japanese custom for people to dismount when they passed someone of superior rank. The English sightseers seem not to have done so; they didn’t think they were obligated, based on the treaties their country had recently signed with Japan. Nevertheless, a soldier stepped out from the Satsuma ranks, apparently enraged at the disrespect. He attacked; three of the English riders got away, but Charles Richardson was fatally wounded. Another warrior decided that Richardson would not recover from his wounds, so he finished him off. This print captures the chaos of that moment:

Two years before this, a Japanese interpreter working for a British diplomat had been murdered. Then the French legation in Edo caught fire, a probable case of arson. Soon after, two Dutch sailors were killed; some of their fingers were found littered around the crime scene.

The last of these attacks happened in Yokohama, but the potential for this sort of violence was essentially the reason that Yokohama was founded in the first place.

After American warships arrived in Japan demanding access to the country’s markets, the Japanese government signed treaties opening certain ports to foreign trade. The most important of these was to be Kanagawa, a town on Edo Bay. But the actual town of Kanagawa was on the Tokaido, which meant that foreign traders would be in constant contact with Japanese people.

The Japanese government had ample reason to believe that this contact would not go well. There was widespread hostility to the foreign presence in Japan, expressed by the slogan "revere the emperor, expel the barbarians." This ukiyo-e print of a sumo wrestler throwing a foreigner to the ground sums up the feelings of a lot of people:

So the government pulled off a deft diplomatic move. It interpreted the treaties as saying it could put the port anywhere in the Kanagawa region rather than in Kanagawa town, which was what the Americans expected. Instead, foreigners were instructed to come to what historian John McMaster calls “a small fishing village of twenty or thirty huts. It was built on a mud bank, backed by an odious swamp and commanded by surrounding hills. The Japanese called the place Yokohama.”

Yokohama was created as a place where the Japanese government could contain foreign influence. The town was turned into an artificial island by a newly dug canal, in an attempt to retain control over the movement of the foreigners (this replicated conditions on the island of Dejima, the only place in Japan where Westerners had been allowed before the treaties). The hills around Yokohama gave the Japanese a strategic advantage should things go awry, and the place’s isolation was meant to keep the friction between foreigners and Japanese people to a minimum.

Yokohama became a new kind of place, a test case for the new Japan that would soon emerge. It was constructed to keep foreigners out, but it ended up being the city where Japan met the world.

Yokohama evolved quickly. This 1860 map shows the town, with foreign ships bobbing in the harbor:

An 1866 fire (it was accidental, starting in a restaurant) burned much of the city down. Afterward, a British engineer designed a new city plan, with wide boulevards to break fires. You can see on the 1868 map below the outposts of various countries; the city was segregated, with foreigners on the right-hand side of the map and Japanese residents on the left. You can also see that the swamps have been drained into canals.

As one of the few places where Japanese people could see Westerners and Western goods up close, Yokohama held a lot of fascination for the public. Ukiyo-e artists made prints of the unusual people and events that could be seen in the city; Yokohama-e eventually became a recognizable genre.

Let’s return to the killing of Charles Richardson, the story with which I began this post. The British government demanded compensation and trials for the men who killed him. Satsuma refused. The Royal Navy sailed to Kagoshima, a Satsuma stronghold, and exchanged fire with the city’s defenders. The rulers of Satsuma decided that they needed to learn from the West; they went on to send a group of students to study at European universities.

I’ll end with this photo of a samurai, taken by Italian-British photographer Felice Beato at his Yokohama studio right around the time that the Satsuma students headed off to England:

How well did this man understand that his was a dying breed? That the town in which he posed for this photo — in a studio run by a foreigner who would not have gained admittance to the country a decade before, using a technology that had very recently appeared in Japan — a town that had sprung from nothing almost overnight, was the end of his way of life and the beginning of something new?
