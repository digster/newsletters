The Scythians Are Still Elusive

Steppe nomads through the eyes of others

George Dillard
Jun 28
 
READ IN APP
 

Nobody went on a tangent quite like Herodotus. The “father of history” traveled around the Mediterranean world, wandering from his home in Asia Minor to the Levant, Babylon, Italy, Egypt, and Greece. He did so in search of the truth, or at least a good story.

Herodotus’s narrative is as peripatetic as he was; he’s often less interested in complete accuracy than he is in taking his reader on a ride. In Book 4 of his Histories, he interrupts the narrative of Persian conquest to wander into central Asia, explaining the nomadic people who live there. He calls these people the Scythians, and he spends about half of one of his nine books telling us all about them.

From Herodotus we learn that:

  • “The Scythians blind all their slaves, to use them in preparing their milk. The plan they follow is to thrust tubes made of bone, not unlike our musical pipes, up the vulva of the mare, and then to blow into the tubes with their mouths, some milking while the others blow. They say that they do this because when the veins of the animal are full of air, the udder is forced down.”

  • Some remote Scythians “are said to be all — both men and women — bald from their birth, to have flat noses, and very long chins.”

  • They live in a place that “has winters of exceeding rigour. During eight months the frost is so intense, that water poured upon the ground does not form mud, but if a fire be lighted on it mud is produced.”

  • “The Scythian soldier drinks the blood of the first man he overthrows in battle. Whatever number he slays, he cuts off all their heads, and carries them to the king… The skulls of their enemies, not indeed of all, but of those whom they most detest, they treat as follows. Having sawn off the portion below the eyebrows, and cleaned out the inside, they cover the outside with leather. When a man is poor, this is all that he does; but if he is rich, he also lines the inside with gold: in either case the skull is used as a drinking cup. They do the same with the skulls of their own kith and kin if they have been at feud with them, and have vanquished them in the presence of the king.”

  • “Hemp grows in Scythia… The Scythians, as I said, take some of this hemp seed, and, creeping under the felt coverings, throw it upon the red-hot stones; immediately it smokes, and gives out such a vapour as no Grecian vapour-bath can exceed; the Scyths, delighted, shout for joy, and this vapour serves them instead of a waterbath; for they never by any chance wash their bodies with water.”

Was this all true? Even Herodotus himself isn’t terribly sure. He tells a story about people with the feet of goats who hibernate six months a year and live beyond the Scythians but admits, “it does not seem to me credible.”

The Scythians are, Herodotus wants us to know, out there somewhere beyond the civilized world, and they’re fascinating.

Herodotus wasn’t the only Greek to find the Scythians captivating. The Greeks who settled around the Black Sea surely interacted with Scythians, who roamed the nearby steppes with their herds of horses. The Scythians showed up in Greek art fairly often. So the Scythians were on the minds of a lot of ancient cultures in the Mediterranean and Western Asia. They’re generally portrayed as an exotic enemy or a subjugated foreign group. We’re not terribly sure who the Scythians really were; historians mostly agree that there probably wasn’t a fixed group that called themselves the Scythians; modern scholars prefer to speak of “steppe cultures” or “Scytho-Siberians.” Because they were nomadic, the Scythians didn’t have a fixed territory. They roamed throughout Western and Central Asia between 700 and 300 BCE. As they were in warfare, the Scythians remain elusive.

But what can we learn from the Scythians themselves? They didn’t leave behind writing, so the only stories we have are from foreigners like Herodotus. But they did create striking works of art that demonstrate their skills, their wealth, and their exposure to the cultures they bordered.

It’s difficult to say just how much Scythian art was their own invention and how much they learned or borrowed from other cultures, like the Greeks. Most historians agree that the two cultures shared ideas and copied each other to some extent. What we can tell is that Scythian elites invested a lot of their wealth and time into these small, portable, valuable objects. They wanted these creatures with them at all times.

Historians have decided that some of the finest works of art that have been found in Scythian tombs were possibly made by Greek artists, working with Scythian motifs for Scythian buyers. This comb, for example:

The Scythians seem hard to know, but what they’ve left behind is stunning. They lived in a manner that seemed “uncivilized” to the Greeks — no writing, no cities, little farming — but they seem to have developed a sophisticated culture and excellent artistic abilities.

How correct was Herodotus about them? We may never know for sure. But archaeologists have verified one of his stories. Herodotus tells us of their love for burning hemp, shouting for joy when they inhale the “vapours.” That story seems to be true — archaeologists have found cannabis residue (along with opium) in Scythian ritual sites.
