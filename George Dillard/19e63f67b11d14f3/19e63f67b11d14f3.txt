Sometime around 1473, a Benedictine monk named Filippo de Strata wrote a letter to the most powerful man in Venice, the Doge. His subject was a dangerous new technology: the printing press.
The monk railed [ https://substack.com/redirect/9482feaa-a9ff-47eb-a6aa-5b4b787f50f9?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] against the supposedly immoral content of the new printed books that were being sold in Italy:
They shamelessly print, at a negligible price, material which may, alas, inflame impressionable youths, while a true writer dies of hunger. Cure (if you will) the plague which is doing away with the laws of all decency, and curb the printers. They persist in their sick vices, setting Tibullus in type, while a young girl reads Ovid to learn sinfulness. Through printing, tender boys and gentle girls, chaste without foul stain, take in whatever mars purity of mind or body; they encourage wantonness, and swallow up huge gain from it.
But mostly, it seems, he was angry that scribes like himself — whose skills had once been admired and desired — were suddenly being made irrelevant:
The Italian writer lives like a beast in a stall. The superior art of authors who have never known any other work than producing well-written books is banished. This glory pertains to you, Doge: to lay low the printing-presses. I beg you to do this, lest the wicked should triumph.
“Writing indeed, which brings in gold for us, should be respected and held to be nobler than all goods, unless she has suffered degradation in the brothel of the printing presses. She is a maiden with a pen, a harlot in print.
You can sense the panic in Filippo de Strata’s writing. Here was a man who had devoted his life to a respected profession that paid its practitioners well. He had surely worked hard to master difficult skills and done work that seemed valuable to society. Scribes like him did beautiful work, instilling every text with artistry and humanity. And then a new machine abruptly threatened this established order.
So it’s not surprising that Filippo de Strata begged the Doge to intervene. It’s also not surprising that his overwrought pleas had no effect. The Doge seemed uninterested in saving the scribes’ profession, and he probably couldn’t have done so if he had tried. The printing press was too cheap and efficient.
In the long run, the new invention was good for society, even if it was very bad for a generation of scribes. Younger people in the late 1400s, reading the writing on the wall, simply went into different professions. But people like Filippo de Strata suffered a destabilizing loss as their once-valuable and prestigious profession disappeared.

Now there’s a new technology whose impact — according to its backers, at least — is going to be just as seismic as the printing press. Artificial intelligence will revolutionize work and transform the economy. But how, exactly?
Some people see a future [ https://substack.com/redirect/3e1a2659-4958-4fbb-9210-ecc385fa0a17?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] in which there is mass unemployment and a need for a fundamentally new social contract, perhaps including a universal basic income for the billions made jobless by AI. But that scenario seems a bit like science fiction (or John Maynard Keynes’ wildly inaccurate predictions in “Economic Possibilities for Our Grandchildren [ https://substack.com/redirect/4efc5544-c330-48de-b2ae-3bfc3dac32f1?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ]”).
Observers who are more grounded in history tend to have a more positive set of predictions about the impact of AI. They remind us that previous technological revolutions did not result in widespread unemployment. Instead, they created new, previously unanticipated jobs [ https://substack.com/redirect/a718882f-a82b-4e41-af70-3c5b6f853ef4?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ]. Sure, there wasn’t much need for scribes anymore, but the world needed more printers and booksellers in the long run. AI, we’re told, will be no different.
But the problem is, as Keynes memorably put it, that “in the long run we are all dead [ https://substack.com/redirect/7b2c939a-c3eb-481d-9c20-db159c65c23c?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ].” The people who happily claim that AI will create more jobs may be right, but they often gloss over one important fact: even when new technologies led to job creation over time, they caused immense pain to people like Filippo de Strata, who had their social status and income quickly ripped away from them. Many of these people were never made whole.
In short, new technologies can create more prosperity and jobs in the long run, but they often do great damage to the generation that has to live through the transition.
We see this not just in the advent of the printing press but in other technological transformations, as well. James Feigenbaum and Daniel Gross studied telephone operators [ https://substack.com/redirect/27ea0dde-d8f1-4b11-ba54-ab3a339604b3?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] in the 1920s, when AT&T installed technology that made their jobs redundant. Before automation, being an operator was one of the best and most common jobs available to women. Suddenly, these women found themselves replaced by technology.
Though Feigenbaum and Gross find that “future cohorts” of women did not experience higher unemployment from the disappearance of operator jobs, the “incumbent” women suffered badly. Few of the newly-unemployed operators found other jobs in the telephone industry. Many former operators fell out of the workforce; those who did find new jobs were generally paid less than they had been as operators, and many women had to move to find work. Though automatic switching technology didn’t have a major effect on women’s employment in the long run, it destroyed the careers of a whole generation of women.
The same dynamic took place on a larger scale during the Industrial Revolution. When textile production was mechanized, the new power looms were able to do the work of hand weavers much more quickly and cheaply. The machines destroyed a once respectable and well-paying profession, replacing it with more dangerous, worse-paying jobs. The weavers were devastated by the change, and many never recovered. William Cobbett wrote [ https://substack.com/redirect/ed6e83f5-25cd-45f4-82bb-b41a58874aeb?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] in 1832 that
It is truly lamentable to behold so many thousands of men who formerly earned 20 to 30 shillings per week, now compelled to live upon 5s, 4s, or even less. It is the more sorrowful to behold these men in their state, as they still retain the frank and bold character formed in the days of their independence.
And William Cooke Taylor [ https://substack.com/redirect/ed6e83f5-25cd-45f4-82bb-b41a58874aeb?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] came across a desperate former weaver in Lancashire in 1842:
One of the men particularly struck my attention; he was the living skeleton of a giant. He told me he had been a weaver and in prosperous times had earned from thirty or forty shillings per week; he had a wife and four children and had long maintained them in decency and comfort; work began to grow slack. He drew the fund he had placed in the savings-bank; he was soon exhausted, and work was slacker than ever. He began to sell his furniture. Before last Christmas everything had disappeared, including the Sunday clothes of himself, his wife, and children. Since that time he had been for seventeen weeks without work of any kind.
Friedrich Engels noticed in 1845 that the new technologies of the Industrial Revolution were creating great wealth, but that wealth was not making its way into the hands of the workers who toiled in the factories. He criticized [ https://substack.com/redirect/f71f7f41-a924-4eb2-85c0-4f4e957c056a?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] “the industrialists, who grow rich on the misery of the mass of wage earners.”
Economic historian Robert Allen later built [ https://substack.com/redirect/f71f7f41-a924-4eb2-85c0-4f4e957c056a?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] on Engels’ observations with more sophisticated economic data. He found that, between 1790 and 1840, British GDP skyrocketed but workers’ wages remained low. After that, the Industrial Revolution paid off with rapid improvements in pay, employment, and well-being.
But during “Engels’ Pause,” as Allen calls it, things were bleak: technological change destroyed once-lucrative and stable careers and introduced an element of chaos into the economy. Things really sucked for a while.
If history is any guide, this is where we’re headed with AI. Which professions will be the new scribes or hand weavers? Will we someday be talking about accountants or paralegals the same way we talk about telephone operators?
In the long run, AI may bring humanity untold riches and an abundance of new jobs. But in the long run, you and I will be dead. In the meantime, this new technology is going to create a lot of pain for a lot of people.
