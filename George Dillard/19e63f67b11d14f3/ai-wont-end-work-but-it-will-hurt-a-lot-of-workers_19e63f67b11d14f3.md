You can sense the panic in Filippo de Strata’s writing. Here was a man who had devoted his life to a respected profession that paid its practitioners well. He had surely worked hard to master difficult skills and done work that seemed valuable to society. Scribes like him did beautiful work, instilling every text with artistry and humanity. And then a new machine abruptly threatened this established order. So it’s not surprising that Filippo de Strata begged the Doge to intervene. It’s also not surprising that his overwrought pleas had no effect. The Doge seemed uninterested in saving the scribes’ profession, and he probably couldn’t have done so if he had tried. The printing press was too cheap and efficient. In the long run, the new invention was good for society, even if it was very bad for a generation of scribes. Younger people in the late 1400s, reading the writing on the wall, simply went into different professions. But people like Filippo de Strata suffered a destabilizing loss as their once-valuable and prestigious profession disappeared. Now there’s a new technology whose impact — according to its backers, at least — is going to be just as seismic as the printing press. Artificial intelligence will revolutionize work and transform the economy. But how, exactly? Some people Observers who are more grounded in history tend to have a more positive set of predictions about the impact of AI. They remind us that previous technological revolutions did not result in widespread unemployment. We see this not just in the advent of the printing press but in other technological transformations, as well. James Feigenbaum and Daniel Gross studied Though Feigenbaum and Gross find that “future cohorts” of women did not experience higher unemployment from the disappearance of operator jobs, the “incumbent” women suffered badly. Few of the newly-unemployed operators found other jobs in the telephone industry. Many former operators fell out of the workforce; those who did find new jobs were generally paid less than they had been as operators, and many women had to move to find work. Though automatic switching technology didn’t have a major effect on women’s employment in the long run, it destroyed the careers of a whole generation of women. The same dynamic took place on a larger scale during the Industrial Revolution. When textile production was mechanized, the new power looms were able to do the work of hand weavers much more quickly and cheaply. The machines destroyed a once respectable and well-paying profession, replacing it with more dangerous, worse-paying jobs. The weavers were devastated by the change, and many never recovered. Friedrich Engels noticed in 1845 that the new technologies of the Industrial Revolution were creating great wealth, but that wealth was not making its way into the hands of the workers who toiled in the factories. He Economic historian Robert Allen later But during “Engels’ Pause,” as Allen calls it, things were bleak: technological change destroyed once-lucrative and stable careers and introduced an element of chaos into the economy. Things really sucked for a while. If history is any guide, this is where we’re headed with AI. Which professions will be the new scribes or hand weavers? Will we someday be talking about accountants or paralegals the same way we talk about telephone operators? In the long run, AI may bring humanity untold riches and an abundance of new jobs. But in the long run, you and I will be dead. In the meantime, this new technology is going to create a lot of pain for a lot of people.
