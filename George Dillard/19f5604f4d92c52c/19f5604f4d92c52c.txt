In fact, both of these examples illustrate my larger point: that we have very little idea who the real Goths were and have repeatedly overlaid their real history with more modern fantasies. What we now call Gothic architecture was called this as an insult. The Renaissance Italians who coined the term meant to contrast what they saw as unrefined medieval architecture from northern Europe with the more classical architecture of ancient Rome and the neoclassical stuff that was being built in their age. Essentially, “Gothic” was the meanest thing they could think to say about those medieval buildings, as the word was associated with barbarism. 
But by the 18th century, some people started to be drawn to the dark, mysterious, decaying aesthetic associated with Gothic architecture. There was a whole genre of literature (Dracula, Frankenstein, etc.) that came to represent “Gothic” vibes for generations. Then, of course, some quite excellent musicians started wearing all black and laying the mascara on thick. Their melancholic approach to life recalled Gothic literature, so they started to call themselves goths. 
None of this had anything to do with the actual Goths, the Germanic tribe that popped up on the borders of the Roman Empire — and therefore began to appear in history — in the 100s CE. 
The Romans portrayed the Goths mostly as barbaric others. Ammianus Marcellinus [ https://substack.com/redirect/5e4133a9-476e-4a1d-b3e7-33cede01541a?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ], for example, doesn’t even flesh them out as much as he does the Huns. The Goths are simply tough and troublesome. Their victory over the Romans at Adrianople in 378 and their sack of Rome in 410 said much more about the weakening Roman Empire than it did the Goths themselves. 
Since there’s really no art portraying the Goths from the late antique period, when they were a powerful force in much of Europe, later artists just guessed at what they might have been like. Alaric, the Gothic leader who marched into Rome, was seen as a typical high medieval king in 1493:

But none of these images really tells us anything about the actual people who appeared in Eastern Europe in the 3rd century CE, clashed with the Roman Empire, split into several groups (usually designated the Visigoths — Western Goths — and Ostrogoths — Eastern Goths), and ruled over parts of Europe, especially Spain, until the 700s.

Other than this, there really isn’t much art that depicts the Goths. All they left behind are fragments, mostly decorative elements for clothing and horses. Most of the artifacts come from the Visigoths, who between the late 300s and early 400s made their way through the Balkans, Italy, and France, finally settling in Spain. The artifacts give us a sense of the imagination and craftsmanship that Goths could exercise.

It’s unclear how much of a cohesive group the Goths ever were. It’s possible that, at least at first, the term “Goth” may have meant more to the Romans who were looking for a way to define the groups across their borders than it did to the Goths themselves. After a few centuries, there wasn’t much difference between Romans and Goths. This was codified in law in the 600s, when the Visigothic Code finally promised the same legal treatment to Romans and Goths:
The Ostrogoths were defeated by the Byzantines in the 500s and absorbed into that empire. The Visigoths established a kingdom centered on Toledo in Spain; they ruled there until the Islamic conquests in the 700s.  Then the Gothic language slowly disappeared as dialects of Latin became dominant. The form of Christianity that the Goths had practiced — once unique — merged with Nicene Catholicism. By the early Middle Ages, there seems to have been no separate group that people thought of as “the Goths” anymore. Having appeared in history rather suddenly, the Goths slipped out of it quietly.
