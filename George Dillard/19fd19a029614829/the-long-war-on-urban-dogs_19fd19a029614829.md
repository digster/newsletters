When you think of dogs in the city, what do you think of? Do you think of the urbanite carrying her little toy dog in a bag on the train? I got a different picture of urban dogs when I visited some parts of Mexico a few years ago. In some of the smaller cities and the outskirts of Mexico City, I ran across lots of loose, apparently ownerless dogs lounging in the sun on the side of the street. They were generally well-behaved and seemed happy, as far as I could tell. I wondered why Americans were so worried about stray dogs if this was what a city full of them was like. But this wasn’t the case in many of the bustling cities of Europe and the United States in the 19th century. Here, dogs were a source of fear and an occasion for panic. This reaction to dogs stemmed from several simultaneous changes. First, the idea of pets was a new one. Though people had kept dogs — and felt fondness for them — for millennia, most dogs had been working animals more than companions. Most people thought pets wasteful and pointless; only fussy rich people would keep an animal as a pet. Pasteur’s discoveries also challenged the idea that the heat of summer could turn a dog into a spreader of rabies. Though mad-dog panics still happened into the 20th century, the worry that every dog could be a killer slowly subsided. This change came as cities had fewer animals in them altogether, as pigs and horses were pushed into the countryside. Cities in the United States and Europe became a mostly human environment; animals no longer roamed the streets. By the middle of the 20th century, the biggest nuisance from dogs in many cities was poop on the sidewalks. The dog days were over in many urban areas.
