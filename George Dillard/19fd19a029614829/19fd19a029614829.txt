Every summer in July and August, the “dog days” of summer descend on the Northern Hemisphere. For thousands of years, it was believed that as Sirius, the “dog star,” rose in the sky, people became languid and disordered while nature became unpredictable and dangerous.
Homer wrote [ https://substack.com/redirect/c192dd7c-558f-4509-b5d6-1943ae374060?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] in the Iliad of Sirius’ menacing appearance:
Sirius rises late in the dark, liquid sky
On summer nights, star of stars
Orion’s Dog they call it, brightest
Of all, but an evil portent, bringing heat
And fevers to suffering humanity
The ancients believed that, with the rising of the dog star, dogs themselves changed. These beliefs persisted into the modern age. It was conventional wisdom that dogs struggled in the heat, panting relentlessly and eventually going mad. This caused them to attack people and infect them with a terrifying disease that would impose paranoia, confusion, hallucinations, and, eventually, death. For a long time, people called the sickness “hydrophobia” because many of its victims developed an irrational fear of liquids. Today, we call it rabies.
These beliefs linking summer weather, the behavior of dogs, and rabies persisted [ https://substack.com/redirect/63f2ee4c-c686-4186-b086-31ba6ddcb0bc?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] long after scientists had debunked them. The beliefs hit a fever pitch, if you will, in the 19th century as people’s relationships with dogs changed at the same time that the urban environment transformed radically. Though dogs had long been present [ https://substack.com/redirect/d4a0fd3b-c964-42b4-a982-cb5c233802a3?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] in cities, the growth of cities and their chaotic nature made urban dogs especially controversial. Were dogs beloved pets or menaces? Should governments protect urban dogs or engage in campaigns to rid cities of them?
The presence of dogs in cities was a surprisingly hot-button issue, reflecting drastic changes in the way that people lived with other people and with animals.
When you think of dogs in the city, what do you think of? Do you think of the urbanite carrying her little toy dog in a bag on the train? A bleary-eyed person out on a cold and dark sidewalk begging their dog to just find a place to pee? Hipster bars full of dogs patiently waiting for something to fall from a table?
I got a different picture of urban dogs when I visited some parts of Mexico a few years ago. In some of the smaller cities and the outskirts of Mexico City, I ran across lots of loose, apparently ownerless dogs lounging in the sun on the side of the street. They were generally well-behaved and seemed happy, as far as I could tell. I wondered why Americans were so worried about stray dogs if this was what a city full of them was like. Other cultures around the world [ https://substack.com/redirect/7e95d3c9-0bc4-4062-baea-ee23f760d1f3?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] have also come to embrace urban dogs.
But this wasn’t the case in many of the bustling cities of Europe and the United States in the 19th century. Here, dogs were a source of fear and an occasion for panic.
This reaction to dogs stemmed from several simultaneous changes. First, the idea of pets was a new one. Though people had kept dogs — and felt fondness for them — for millennia, most dogs had been working animals more than companions. Most people thought pets wasteful and pointless; only fussy rich people would keep an animal as a pet.
But during the Victorian period, it became acceptable for middle-class people to keep pets [ https://substack.com/redirect/ce4f8c41-5926-4ef8-bf32-4cff1396f4f5?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ]. People began to think that pets were good for their kids or made a home more cozy. There was a craze around pet dogs, which demonstrated Victorian values like loyalty and, when purebred, showed off the owner’s wealth and taste.
Many of these pets weren’t living in the countryside, where dogs had long been an important part of life. They found themselves in cities that were growing at breakneck speed. London [ https://substack.com/redirect/a1a04c20-6867-4122-9476-c3f19d4a3547?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] sextupled in size during the 19th century; New York [ https://substack.com/redirect/0343f8e4-fa00-423e-ac23-c46a2fbcd40a?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] was something like 63 times more populous in 1900 than it had been in 1800.
These new urban agglomerations, larger than any cities in human history, had to accommodate not only a huge human population but also millions of animals, from the horses that transported everything around the city to the pigs [ https://substack.com/redirect/9d9603f2-5054-4cff-9e8d-0798d5d67065?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] that people kept for food. And dogs, lots of dogs. There were dogs kept as pets under the strict control of their owners, free-roaming dogs that technically had a home base and an owner, and strays. These dogs became the center of heated disputes that reflected the turbulence just under the surface of these rapidly changing societies.
A lot of the arguments about urban dogs centered on rabies. And it should be said that rabies was no joke. Contracting this disease meant almost certain death until Louis Pasteur developed a treatment in the 1880s. Even today, rabies is untreatable [ https://substack.com/redirect/dee0305d-ad65-4b72-847d-6d0d13b42244?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] once victims start to show symptoms.
For most of the 19th century, people thought that dogs were more dangerous [ https://substack.com/redirect/63f2ee4c-c686-4186-b086-31ba6ddcb0bc?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] during the hot days of summer because they thought the weather drove them to misbehave. Essentially, the heat made them rabid. During the dog days, a lovable pooch might turn into a deadly monster.
It did not seem as though all dogs were equally vulnerable to the heat. Stray dogs that lived on the street were more dangerous than household pets, presumably because of a lack of training and a general sense of dirtiness. Some breeds were considered worse than others. In an age of pseudoscientific race theory, people thought that cross-bred dogs were more of a threat than purebreds.
One type of dog came in for special disapproval: the spitz [ https://substack.com/redirect/63f2ee4c-c686-4186-b086-31ba6ddcb0bc?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ], a fluffy husky-like breed (It’s hard for me to imagine these dogs as vicious predators; my grandmother had a little white spitz that was incredibly cute). These dogs, people believed, were from northern climates and therefore unfit for the sultry heat of many cities. Plus, they weren’t purebred. A scientist named William Hammond exemplified the anti-spitz messaging by mixing racism with a misunderstanding of rabies. He said [ https://substack.com/redirect/63f2ee4c-c686-4186-b086-31ba6ddcb0bc?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] in 1877 that “the Spitz is a cross between the Pomeranian hound and the Artic [sic] fox . . . all hybrids are bad. The mulatto is neither as good as the white man nor the negro [sic].”
A New York Times article [ https://substack.com/redirect/3f851cef-fe2b-457e-a03e-a94af2f206db?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] in 1876 combined anti-spitz mania with all sorts of strange views. I’m going to quote it at length because it’s all so unhinged:
There are but four venomous beasts among the fauna of the United States. These are the rattlesnake, the copperhead, the moccasin, and the Spitz dog, and of the four, the latter is by far the most aggressive and deadly in its hostility to man…. The Spitz dog is peculiarly subject to rabies. It was generally known that the Spitz is ill-tempered and treacherous, but the animal’s exceptional proclivity to going mad and distributing hydrophobia throughout the circle of its acquaintance had escaped the notice of naturalists. Since then every case of hydrophobia which had occurred in this city or its vicinity had resulted from the bite of a Spitz dog….
Were any of our citizens to insist on keeping pet rattlesnakes, and permitting them to run at large in the streets, the law would soon convince them of the propriety of weaning their affections from creeping things and setting them upon less dangerous pets….
If we had left the brute in the Arctic regions, either he would not have gone mad or else he would have confined himself to biting the casual Esquimaux, or the infrequent Arctic explorer…
The article goes on to compare the spitz unfavorably with the “Calvinistic” Scotch terrier and declare the breed “worthless” because it did not hunt rats and “no man ever found in him the least true consolation and sympathy. He is misanthropic, malicious, and useless; and… the keeper of a Spitz is deficient in those qualities which secure the respect and love of high-toned and intelligent dogs.”
The article concludes that “this noxious beast… deserve[s] nothing but extermination.”
Extermination may not have been what urban dogs deserved, but it was often what they got.
Cities tried all sorts of things to control their dog populations, often in response to terrifying accounts of attacks on women or children that the press played to the hilt. The laws were usually passed in the summer because of popular beliefs about rabies. Many cities forced owners to muzzle [ https://substack.com/redirect/3d72f811-bfa1-4aeb-871e-c69ab41cabd3?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] their dogs all summer and animal catchers picked up any unmuzzled dogs that roamed the streets (New York had a pound, but it was only open in the summer). A lot of dog owners, for their part, complained about the muzzling laws, considering them unnecessarily hard on well-behaved dogs.
But muzzles were only the beginning. Many cities killed urban dogs en masse, often paying a bounty for dead canines. Mexico City [ https://substack.com/redirect/28ee3ab2-5e03-4953-86eb-dffbb224064f?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] paid its lamppost-lighters “four pesos for every hundred dead dogs” in the late 1700s and early 1800s. Later, it hired contractors to sweep the city and kill all of the dogs they could find. The poisoned meat left out for strays killed so many dogs (including some that weren’t strays) that the city cremated thousands of animals a year.
English cities, too, had held “dog culls [ https://substack.com/redirect/8c21147c-7fc9-4178-91eb-cca853628183?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ]” since the 1700s, drowning or bludgeoning animals. Horace Walpole was one of the few to take the dogs’ side:
The streets are the very picture of the murder of the innocents — One drives over nothing but poor dead dogs! The dear, good-natured, honest, sensible creatures! Christ! How can anybody hurt them?
In British India [ https://substack.com/redirect/62f891c6-c8fc-499a-aeca-1697b2b74ef4?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ], where the colonial authorities wanted to “clean up” the streets of cities, dogs were shot, poisoned, and clubbed to death. In New York, it was sometimes open season on urban dogs, especially in the periods after high-profile dog attacks. After a nine-year-old boy was bitten, vigilantes shot six spitzes in the street. The city offered [ https://substack.com/redirect/b1548b47-7194-45de-b702-96ab6fc40168?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] $1 per dead dog, and gangs of children roamed the area capturing and killing thousands of dogs per year. The New York pound drowned thousands more dogs that it captured each summer.
These efforts met with limited success. When the Young Turks, who ruled the Ottoman Empire, tried to westernize their country, one part of their program was the elimination of Istanbul’s often-beloved 60,000–80,000 street dogs [ https://substack.com/redirect/113178ef-12da-4f5d-b2b8-6c66e8b88262?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ]. They failed, and dogs still roam Istanbul freely, although the current government is trying once again to remove them from the city.
In many cities in the United States and Europe, the war against dogs was relatively successful.
Animal rights organizations, most notably the ASPCA, formed in the late 1800s and pushed against the most egregious anti-dog violence. First, mass killing of dogs was made more “humane” — gas chambers and electrocution [ https://substack.com/redirect/3d72f811-bfa1-4aeb-871e-c69ab41cabd3?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] replaced clubbing and drowning. Then, groups like the ASPCA [ https://substack.com/redirect/8560fdbb-70d0-4f5c-a99e-fc18033016ea?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] took over animal control in some cities and instituted animal shelters rather than pounds where dog-catchers were paid by the number of animals they collected. The killing of dogs continued in many cases, but it was out of public view, at least.
Meanwhile, the fear of urban dogs began to subside after Louis Pasteur’s discovery of a rabies vaccine in 1885. Pasteur’s discoveries also challenged the idea that the heat of summer could turn a dog into a spreader of rabies. Though mad-dog panics still happened into the 20th century, the worry that every dog could be a killer slowly subsided. This change came as cities had fewer animals in them altogether, as pigs and horses were pushed into the countryside. Cities in the United States and Europe became a mostly human environment; animals no longer roamed the streets.
By the middle of the 20th century, the biggest nuisance from dogs in many cities was poop on the sidewalks. The dog days were over in many urban areas.
