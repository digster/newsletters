This map by Antonio Pigafetta, a Venetian passenger on Magellan’s voyage, makes it pretty clear what Europeans like him were after when he created it in 1521. He renders some small islands in what is now Indonesia, but the centerpiece of the image is a clove tree. One of the Europeans sent to the Indian Ocean in search of these spices was a Portuguese apothecary named Tome Pires, who arrived in 1511. He became a “factor of drugs” — which is what Europeans considered many of the spices to be — in the growing Portuguese empire. Pires seems to have bounced around the spice-producing areas of the Indian Ocean. He supervised Portuguese operations in Kerala, India, was sent to Malacca, and then back to India. He eventually became the Portuguese ambassador to China, a country the Portuguese antagonized relentlessly. It seems not to have ended well. He was either killed by the Chinese government or exiled. As we know all too well these days, geopolitical conditions can determine whether straits are flourishing places of commerce or logjams where trade comes to a screeching halt. The Strait of Malacca was no different. For almost 500 years, from the 700s to the 1100s, the kingdom of Srivijaya, centered on Palembang on the south side of the strait, ruled over Sumatra, Java, and the Malay Peninsula. Its rulers prioritized trade, defeating the pirates who had often made travel through the strait hazardous. The ships that made their way through the strait are commemorated on the Borobudur temple in Java, at the eastern end of Srivijaya influence: Srivijaya’s Buddhist rulers hosted pilgrims from all over the region and sponsored beautiful art: Srivijaya was later subsumed by the Majapahit Empire, centered farther east on Java. Its influence stretched from the Spice Islands in the east to Malaya in the west. During this period, a Malay nobleman named Parameswara in the early 1400s made a series of savvy alliances with pirates to gain power in the strait and turned a sleepy fishing village called Malacca into an important trade hub. Known for being a safe port with fair trade practices, Malacca grew quickly. Parameswara did what he could to establish ties between his city and the major centers of trade. He entered into marriage alliances with Muslim rulers to the west. Meanwhile, he traveled to China and gave tribute to the emperor; later, Zheng He’s giant treasure ships docked at Malacca. These voyages allowed Chinese mapmakers to render the straits accurately: Malacca became an important city with perhaps 100,000 residents by the year 1500, about the same size as Venice or Kyoto. By the late 1500s, European knowledge of the region was much more complete, so its maps were much more accurate. They needed to be, because the Strait of Malacca was now at the center of European economic and imperial designs: Portuguese intentions in Malacca were clear: they wanted to govern the strait through military force. As the Portuguese Empire declined and European competition in the area increased, Malacca faded in importance. The Dutch took it from the Portuguese in 1641; by the middle of the 1700s, Malacca was just one of many trade cities in the region. By the 19th century, Dutch influence in the region was fading and the British Empire was nearing its peak. They, unlike the Dutch, did have something to sell to China: opium.
