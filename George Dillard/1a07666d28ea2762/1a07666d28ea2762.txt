This map by Antonio Pigafetta, a Venetian passenger on Magellan’s voyage, makes it pretty clear what Europeans like him were after when he created it in 1521. He renders some small islands in what is now Indonesia, but the centerpiece of the image is a clove tree. 
One of the Europeans sent to the Indian Ocean in search of these spices was a Portuguese apothecary named Tome Pires, who arrived in 1511. He became a “factor of drugs” — which is what Europeans considered many of the spices to be — in the growing Portuguese empire. Pires seems to have bounced around the spice-producing areas of the Indian Ocean. He supervised Portuguese operations in Kerala, India, was sent to Malacca, and then back to India. He eventually became the Portuguese ambassador to China, a country the Portuguese antagonized relentlessly. It seems not to have ended well. He was either killed by the Chinese government or exiled. He drops out of the historical record by 1524.
Pires is most famous for his book, the Suma Oriental [ https://substack.com/redirect/b32d2357-0d97-48ea-88f8-a19b1789b205?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ], which explains in painstaking detail all of the political arrangements and trade networks of the region. Most of the book is simply descriptive, but when Pires gets to Malacca, the city where he was posted for several years, he makes a firm recommendation to his king: hold onto Malacca at all costs. He praises the city again and again:
And should anyone ask what advantage to his exchequer the King our Lord can derive from Malacca, there is no doubt that… Malacca is of such importance and profit that it seems to me it has no equal in the world…
Who can doubt that in Malacca bahars of gold will be made, and that there will be no need of money from India, but it will go from here to there? 
Men cannot estimate the worth of Malacca, on account of its greatness and profit. Malacca is a city that was made for merchandise, fitter than any other in the world…
Why was Malacca so valuable? It was the city’s strategic location, at 
the end of monsoons and the beginning of others. Malacca is surrounded and lies in the middle, and the trade and commerce between the different nations for a thousand leagues on every hand must come to Malacca. Wherefore a thing of such magnitude and of such great wealth, which never in the world could decline, if it were moderately governed and favoured, should be supplied, looked after, praised and favoured, and not neglected…

If we zoom in, we can see Malacca and the strait it commands:
The strait, which Pires describes as a “gullet,” is the logical route for ships leaving the South China Sea on their way to the Indian Ocean. As he mentions, the monsoons make Malacca even more important. In the winter and spring, winds pushed [ https://substack.com/redirect/9efa2238-6791-4da1-b199-bf7317d938ce?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] ships south from India and China; the rest of the year, they pushed them back north. Rather than travel the entire route, ships could stop in Malacca and trade (or wait out the monsoon there).
Pires understood that Portugal’s control of Malacca gave it immense power: 
Whoever is lord of Malacca has his hand on the throat of Venice. As far as from Malacca, and from Malacca to China, and from China to the Moluccas, and from the Moluccas to Java, and from Java to Malacca [and] Sumatra, [all] is in our power. 
As we know all too well these days, geopolitical conditions can determine whether straits are flourishing places of commerce or logjams where trade comes to a screeching halt. The Strait of Malacca was no different. 
For almost 500 years, from the 700s to the 1100s, the kingdom of Srivijaya, centered on Palembang on the south side of the strait, ruled over Sumatra, Java, and the Malay Peninsula. Its rulers prioritized trade, defeating the pirates who had often made travel through the strait hazardous. The ships that made their way through the strait are commemorated on the Borobudur temple in Java, at the eastern end of Srivijaya influence:
Srivijaya’s Buddhist rulers hosted pilgrims from all over the region and sponsored beautiful art:
Srivijaya was later subsumed by the Majapahit Empire, centered farther east on Java. Its influence stretched from the Spice Islands in the east to Malaya in the west. 
During this period, a Malay nobleman named Parameswara in the early 1400s made a series of savvy alliances with pirates to gain power in the strait and turned a sleepy fishing village called Malacca into an important trade hub. Known for being a safe port with fair trade practices, Malacca grew quickly. Parameswara did what he could to establish ties between his city and the major centers of trade. He entered into marriage alliances with Muslim rulers to the west. Meanwhile, he traveled to China and gave tribute to the emperor; later, Zheng He’s giant treasure ships docked at Malacca.

Malacca became an important city with perhaps 100,000 residents by the year 1500, about the same size as Venice or Kyoto. It shows up on the first Portuguese map of the region, from 1502, which demonstrated a pretty sketchy understanding of the geography of the area:
This made Malacca a tempting target for the Portuguese captain Alfonso de Albuquerque, whose ruthless methods [ https://substack.com/redirect/5dd923a5-bd2f-4165-9187-b9a87d80c9dc?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] we’ve already witnessed. He realized that Portugal did not have many goods that the people of Southeast Asia desired, so he resolved to take the riches of the region by force. Albuquerque took Malacca in 1511. The strait was key to his designs. He wrote [ https://substack.com/redirect/604ad467-5768-42b6-8bbb-58317eabc3b1?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] that “I am very sure that if this Malacca trade is taken out of [the hands of Muslim merchants], Cairo and Mecca will be completely lost and no spices will go to the Venetians except those they go to Portugal to buy.”
Tome Pires arrived soon after. By the late 1500s, European knowledge of the region was much more complete, so its maps were much more accurate. They needed to be, because the Strait of Malacca was now at the center of European economic and imperial designs:
Portuguese intentions in Malacca were clear: they wanted to govern the strait through military force. Ironically, this conquest introduced disorder; it probably increased [ https://substack.com/redirect/2009c4ea-8b00-4194-be0a-d0c902057542?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] piracy by disturbing what had been a pretty stable system of trade. And, if we’re being honest, the Portuguese behaved in a way that was indistinguishable from piracy anyway.

As you can see on the map above, there are a lot of places from which a military power could control the straits. As the Portuguese Empire declined and European competition in the area increased, Malacca faded in importance. The Dutch took it from the Portuguese in 1641; by the middle of the 1700s, Malacca was just one of many trade cities in the region. 
By the 19th century, Dutch influence in the region was fading and the British Empire was nearing its peak. They, unlike the Dutch, did have something to sell to China: opium. The British chose Singapore as their main city on the strait, and thus their main node of opium distribution. We’ll tell that story next week.
