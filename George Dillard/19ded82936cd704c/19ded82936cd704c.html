Coffee: a Stimulant That Made the Modern World

"Harmless and healing liquor" or "bitter invention of Satan?"

George Dillard
May 3

Unlike the other great early modern drugs, coffee was a genuinely new phenomenon in the 1500s. Tea, chocolate, tobacco, opium, and sugar had all been consumed in some way for centuries, at least in some parts of the world. But nobody seems to have thought of stripping the fruit away from coffee seeds, drying them, roasting them, grinding them up, and brewing them in hot water until the fifteenth century.

Nobody knows who first figured it out. Some argue that coffee originated in the forests of Kaffa, in Ethiopia. Others associate it with the area around Mocha, a port in Yemen.

The emergence of this new stimulant was especially controversial because it happened in the Muslim world, which was suspicious of other mind-altering substances.

Nizar Hermes writes that some people in the Islamic world came to love coffee, especially Sufi mystics who found the drink a near-miraculous boost to their focus and energy. It helped them stay up all night studying and reciting the Quran. One poet, Abu al-Fath al-Tunisi, wrote in the 1500s about “how beautiful [coffee] is — resembling molten jet.”

But others were suspicious. The word in Arabic for coffee was qahwa, which could also refer to wine. If it sounded like wine and intoxicated people, could it be permissible? Nur al-Din al-Imriti wrote a poem in opposition to coffee:

Praise be to God, who forbade people

to indulge in all forms of intoxicants,

and in all that harms the mind, religion, and body,

and in all that leads to corruption and peril.

Know that the renowned coffee

is repugnant and exceedingly bitter.

The debate went beyond clerics. The ruler of Mecca banned coffee in 1511; so did Cairo’s governor in 1539 (he even seized people’s beans). The Ottoman Sultan Murad IV saw a different threat in the coffeehouses of Istanbul. He feared that they’d lead to disloyal talk and foment resistance to the regime, so he tore them down. As coffee made its way into Europe, it struck a similar note of controversy. In fact, the drink’s association with Islam slowed its adoption in Christian countries. Some early European texts called coffee “the wine of Islam,” “the bitter invention of Satan,” or “Mahometan gruel.” A 1672 “Broad-side Against Coffee” was filled with allusions to the drink’s foreign origins:

COFFEE, a kind of Turkish Renegade,

Has late a match with Christian water made;

At first between them happen'd a Demur,

Yet joyn'd they were, but not without great stir;

For both so cold were, and so faintly met,

The Turkish Hymen in his Turbant swet…

Coffee so brown as berry does appear,

Too swarthy for a Nymph so fair, so clear:

And yet his sails he did for England hoist,

Though cold and dry, to court the cold and moist;

If there be ought we can as love admit;

'Tis a hot love, and lasteth but a fit.

There was also a lot of worry about the stimulant effects of coffee. The earliest English account of coffee, written by Sir Antony Sherley, who had been traveling through Ottoman lands, described it as a “liquor, which they do call Coffe, which is made of seede much like mustard seede, which will soone intoxicate the braine like our Metheglin.” (Metheglin is a kind of mead)

Simon Paulli, writing in 1661, lumped coffee in with some of the other intoxicants circulating at the time. He seemed especially concerned about the sexual side effects of coffee:

It is…the Duty of every European to join in engaging the legislature to put a stop to this epidemical Evil, and prohibit the Abuse, not only of tea, but also of Tobacco, since both of these, and Coffee….so enervate the European men, that they become incapable of propagating their species, like Eunuchs, some of whom are highly salacious, but it is sufficiently known, that they are incapable of Procreation, (though they emit something analogous to semen.)

One of the most famous works on coffee was the 1674 Women’s Petition Against Coffee, which alleged that men who drink coffee become

as Impotent… and as unfruitful as those Desarts whence that unhappy Berry is said to be brought.

For the continual sipping of this pittiful drink is enough to bewitch Men of two and twenty, and tie up the Codpice-point without a Charm. It renders them that use it as Lean as Famine, as Rivvel’d as Envy,or an old meager Hagg over-ridden by an Incubus. They come from it with nothing moist but their snotty Noses, nothing stiffe but their Joints, nor standing but their Ears.

It also highlighted another supposed problem with coffee — that it led to too much talk:

For here like so many Frogs in a puddle, they sup muddy water, and murmur infignificant notes till half a dozen of them out-babble an equal number of us at a Gossipping, talking all at once in Confusion, and running from point to point as insensibly…

This was the concern of British King Charles II, who, like Murad IV, saw London’s coffeehouses as potential breeding grounds for dangerous ideas. He issued his Proclamation for the Suppression of Coffee Houses just after Christmas in 1675.

The ban only lasted 11 days; it seemed that the public, including much of Parliament, wanted coffee badly enough to defy their king.

The British public was more persuaded by pro-coffee literature. There was, soon after the Women’s Petition, a Men’s Response, which, they claimed, was not the cause of impotency:

But why must innocent COFFEE be the object of your Spleen? That harmless and healing Liquor, which Indulgent Providence first sent amongst us, at a time when Brimmers of Rebellion, and Fanatick Zeal had intoxicated the Nation, and we wanted a Drink at once to make us Sober and Merry. Tis not this incomparable settle Brain that shortens Natures Standard, or makes us less Active in the Sports of Venus…

The men also defended their talkativeness at the coffee house:

You may well permit us to talk abroad, for at home we have scarce time to utter a word for the insufferable Din of your ever active Tongues, the Foolish extravagancies of our lives, are infinitly out-done by the wild Frolliques of yours…

Historians now believe that both pamphlets — the Women’s Petition and the Men’s Response — were probably written by the same person, who was trying to stir people up. But the author was on to something: coffee was coded in many European countries as a “male” drink, a counterpart to more “feminine” tea.

Many other tracts came out extolling the health benefits of coffee. Sylvestre Dufour, writing in 1685, concluded that coffee

is not only very healthy, but even nourishing, the which savants attribute to the oleaginous humor naturally in coffee which greatly sweetens the ferment, impeaches the stinging of membranes in the stomach,…and furnishes substance proper to be converted into a nourishing juice which repairs the spirits.

Though coffee won the day, it didn’t convince everyone. One of the later crackdowns on coffee happened in the 1780s, when Frederick the Great tried to extinguish the drinking of coffee through a ban, high tariffs, and, eventually, a government monopoly. He was trying to boost local industries, and, anyway, he didn’t know why beer wasn’t a good-enough beverage for all purposes:

It is disgusting to notice the increase in the quantity of coffee used by my subjects, and the amount of money that goes out of the country in consequence. Everybody is using coffee. If possible, this must be prevented. My people must drink beer. His Majesty was brought up on beer, and so were his ancestors, and his officers. Many battles have been fought and won by soldiers nourished on beer; and the King does not believe that coffee-drinking soldiers can be depended upon to endure hardship or to beat his enemies in case of the occurrence of another war.

So Frederick deployed hundreds of “coffee sniffers” to find people still drinking smuggled coffee in Prussia. The sniffers were disabled war veterans who could tell the scent of roasting coffee from chicory or other substitutes; they were paid a bounty for each illegal coffee stash they found:

Everybody hated the coffee-sniffers. When Frederick died in 1787, the ban quietly disappeared. Coffee had taken over Europe, and, indeed, the world.
