Iran Delenda Est

The American obsession with war against Iran

It was probably the most significant example of fig-based political persuasion in history.

When a Roman politician named Cato visited northern Africa to help settle a dispute between Numidia, which controlled much of what’s now Algeria and Libya, and Rome’s old enemy Carthage, in modern-day Tunisia, he expected to find Carthage weak and poor. After all, the Romans had defeated Carthage in a long war about 50 years before, ending their rivalry for dominance in the Mediterranean. Cato had served in this war as a young man.

But when Cato arrived in Africa, he did not find the poor, defeated city he expected. The historian Plutarch writes that Carthage was “teeming with vigorous fighting men, overflowing with enormous wealth, filled with arms of every sort and with military supplies, and not a little puffed up by all this.”

Cato was disturbed by what he had seen. This thriving city, though far weaker than it had been in its glory days, when the Carthaginian general Hannibal had invaded Italy, might pose a threat to Rome after all. So Cato rushed back to the Senate to warn his colleagues. In the Senate chamber, he dramatically let a plump fig roll out of his toga.

When Cato’s fellow senators remarked on the “size and beauty” of the fig, he let them know that it had been grown in Carthage. His message to the fig lovers in the Senate was clear: if he could bring a fruit that was notorious for spoiling in a couple of days across from Carthage, Carthage could have an army in Italy in the same amount of time.

Cato was so rattled by his trip to Carthage that confronting the African city-state became an obsession. He ended every speech with ”Ceterum censeo Carthaginem esse delendam” (”Furthermore, I think that Carthage must be destroyed”). This has been shortened in modern culture to the snappier “Carthago delenda est” (”Carthage must be destroyed”), a phrase that has appeared on Mark Zuckerberg’s T-shirts, among other places.

Cato’s campaign created momentum for a third Roman war against Carthage, which began in 149 BCE. Cato died as the conflict was beginning, but I suppose, had he lived, he would have been pleased to see what happened next. The Romans dictated impossible terms to their old enemies, ordering them to hand over all their weapons and then abandon their city entirely. When the Carthaginians refused, the Romans undertook a brutal assault on the city.

The Roman historian Appian described the suffering of the Carthaginian people:

All places were filled with groans, shrieks, shouts, and every kind of agony. Some were stabbed, others were hurled alive from the roofs to the pavement, some of them alighting on the heads of spears or other pointed weapons, or swords…

Then came new scenes of horror. As the fire spread and carried everything down, the soldiers did not wait to destroy the buildings little by little, but all in a heap. So the crashing grew louder, and many corpses fell with the stones into the midst. Others were seen still living, especially old men, women, and young children who had hidden in the inmost nooks of the houses, some of them wounded, some more or less burned, and uttering piteous cries. Still others, thrust out and falling from such a height with the stones, timbers, and fire, were torn asunder in all shapes of horror, crushed and mangled.

Trenches were filled with men. Some who were thrown in head foremost, with their legs sticking out of the ground, writhed a long time. Others fell with their feet downward and their heads above ground. Horses ran over them, crushing their faces and skulls, not purposely on the part of the riders, but in their headlong haste…

Six days and nights were consumed in this kind of fighting…

Carthage was plundered and burned to the ground. Appian tells us that the destruction was so complete that the Roman commander, Scipio, wept and “publicly lamented the fortune of the enemy.”

Most modern accounts portray Cato’s obsession with Carthage as misguided. Though the two empires had been enemies in the past, it was unlikely that Carthage, which had lost most of its territory to the Romans in two other wars, posed a real threat to Rome. The Romans’ cruelty was disproportionate to the danger they faced from their weakened rival.

For decades, a legion of American Catos has been as relentless about Iran as Cato was about Carthage.

Ever since the Iranian Revolution in 1979, the country’s government has been antagonistic toward the United States, in part because of the United States’ long history of intervention in Iran and its support of the oppressive regime that preceded the current theocracy. Iran certainly did hold American hostages for over a year and has been a sponsor of terrorism against Americans, especially in the 1980s.

In response, there have been constant calls for war with Iran, especially from the political right. And these calls have gotten stronger as Iran has gotten weaker.

The American desire for the destruction of the Islamic Republic goes back to the early 1980s. When Saddam Hussein made the ill-advised decision to invade Iran, the Americans (especially after Ronald Reagan took office) were eager to support his effort, even though Iraq had been the aggressor and Saddam’s regime was unsavory, to say the least. Even though Iraq’s invasion was unprovoked and led to an eight-year war that killed half a million people, the Reagan Administration provided economic aid, intelligence, and diplomatic support to the Iraqis. Bruce Riedel has called the conflict “America’s first war with Iran,” an “undeclared yet bloody naval and air war.”

The United States was especially aggressive toward the end of the war, after Reagan allowed Kuwait to reflag its oil tankers with the stars and stripes and ordered the U.S. Navy to escort them through the Persian Gulf. After an American ship hit a mine, injuring ten crewmen, the United States launched Operation Praying Mantis, the largest American naval engagement since World War II, and destroyed half of the Iranian fleet.

The end of open hostilities between Iraq and Iran did not diminish the appetite of American politicians for conflict. Newt Gingrich, the Republican leader in the mid-1990s, called Iran “the most dangerous country in the world” and pushed a policy of covert operations designed to overthrow the Iranian regime.

After 9/11, the right’s desire for conflict with Iran went into overdrive. Even though the Iranian government was not involved in the attacks in Washington and New York, the Bush administration wrapped Iran up in its larger “War on Terror.” Bush famously included Iran as a member of the “Axis of Evil,” saying that

States like these, and their terrorist allies, constitute an axis of evil, arming to threaten the peace of the world. By seeking weapons of mass destruction, these regimes pose a grave and growing danger. They could provide these arms to terrorists, giving them the means to match their hatred. They could attack our allies or attempt to blackmail the United States. In any of these cases, the price of indifference would be catastrophic.

Bush expressed these views even though Iran had made efforts to help the United States in the days after 9/11, signaling its openness to dialogue with Washington. The Americans rebuffed these offers and chose hostility instead. Bush’s speechwriter David Frum, when asked about the inclusion of Iran in the “Axis of Evil,” said that Iran was an existential threat to the United States:

The whole State of the Union speech was a very important statement, about as important a statement as the president could give, because he was warning the American people of threats to the security -- not just the security, the very survival -- of the whole nation.

If the kinds of people who crashed those planes into the World Trade Center had weapons of mass destruction, had nuclear bombs, they would have used them. ...

Among these governments are North Korea and Iran and Iraq, and they present a threat to the United States as profound, as terrible, as communism in its day and Nazism in its day; not because these regimes are as powerful as the Soviets and the Nazis were -- they’re not -- but the weapons they are pursuing are more powerful than anything that the Soviets and the Nazis had. And the regimes themselves are that much more reckless, more bent on death and destruction; not [more] than the Nazis, but certainly [more] than the communists....

Even as the Bush-era invasions of Afghanistan and Iraq turned into quagmires, leading conservative pundits piled pressure on the government to attack Iran. Bill Kristol pushed for war with Iran in 2006, asking, “Why wait? Does anyone think a nuclear Iran can be contained? That the current regime will negotiate in good faith? It would be easier to act sooner rather than later.” Norman Podhoretz made “The Case for Bombing Iran” in 2007, around the same time that presidential candidate John McCain sang “Bomb Iran” to the tune of the Beach Boys’ “Barbara Ann.”

When the Obama administration finally made progress against an Iranian nuclear weapon with the JCPOA, an agreement that required Iran to dismantle most of its nuclear program, Republicans seemed to prefer war over negotiation. Senator Tom Cotton called for “several days of air and naval bombing.” Former Bush advisor John Bolton advocated bombing Iran — or letting Israel bomb Iran — as the only solution to an Iranian nuclear program. Cotton organized an “open letter” signed by 47 GOP senators that constituted an attempt to sabotage the deal, threatening that the next president could undo the agreement with “the stroke of a pen.”

In the Republican primary debates of 2016, the candidates tried to top one another with their disdain for the JCPOA and desire for aggression against Iran. Chris Christie worried that the Iranians would take America’s “lunch money” unless the next president got tough. Donald Trump called “the Iran deal… one of the worst deals I have ever seen negotiated in my entire life.” Ted Cruz said that “If you look at the threats facing this country, the single gravest threat, national security threat, is the threat of a nuclear Iran. That’s why I’ve pledged on day one to rip to shreds this Iranian nuclear deal.” Only John Kasich, who never had a chance, said he would try to honor the deal rather than discard it and get aggressive with Iran.

Trump, of course, did tear up the JCPOA after taking office. He promised that he would negotiate a better deal, but never did. The Iranian politicians who had gone out on a limb to negotiate with Americans lost their status, and hardliners got the upper hand. It was now extremely unlikely that the Iranian regime would trust the Americans again; instead, it responded to the demise of the agreement by restarting its nuclear program.

Why were conservatives so fixated on going to war with Iran? They’ll tell you that Iran’s regime is uniquely fanatical and dangerous. Perhaps. The country is also weak and relatively poor; its GDP is roughly equivalent to that of Alabama. Maybe Iran posed a unique danger to the United States; maybe it was a convenient perpetual enemy, easily associated with the boogeymen that the right has used to rally support for a quarter century or more.

Now the “Iran delenda est” crowd has finally gotten what it has wanted for so long. The United States is attacking Iran. Thousands of innocent people will die and billions of dollars will be spent. We’ll see in the coming months and years whether their long-awaited dream of violence against Iran pays off — or whether, like the Roman attack on Carthage, it will be a pointless human tragedy.
