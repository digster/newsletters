The War Before the War

Germany’s secret terror campaign during World War I

George Dillard
Sep 30
 
READ IN APP
 

The day before the Fourth of July, a strange man came calling at the Long Island mansion of J.P. Morgan, Jr.

He pulled a gun on the butler and demanded to be taken inside. The butler tried to lock the intruder in the library and called for Morgan to hide. The man got loose and shot Morgan, who happened to fall onto his would-be assassin; at that moment, Morgan’s wife Jane took the opportunity to take the man’s gun, while the butler knocked the man out with a chunk of coal. Morgan was wounded, but survived.

Just two years before the attack, J.P. Morgan, Jr. (or Jack, as he was called), had taken control of the banking empire his father had built. Now, in 1915, he was taking advantage of the war that had spread across Europe to make even more money. Morgan, who was close friends with the British ambassador to the United States, lent hundreds of millions of dollars to the Allied combatants. After he was arrested, the assailant said that his goal was to stop “the slaughter of thousands of Europeans.”

As police interrogated the man, who identified himself as Frank Holt, they realized that this was more than a simple assassination attempt.

The night before, the U.S. Capitol had been rocked by an explosion in the middle of the night; it knocked a guard off his chair and destroyed a reception room, but caused no other casualties. At first, the explosion was thought to be an accident, perhaps a gas leak. But a letter from the bomber, an R. Pearce, appeared in the Washington Evening Star the next day:

Police, looking at the accounts of the Capitol bombing and the Morgan assassination that ran next to one another on newspaper front pages, soon realized that R. Pearce and Frank Holt had expressed very similar sentiments about the war. Upon interrogation, Holt acknowledged that he had built a bomb, snuck it into the Capitol, detonated it, hopped on a train, and then shot Morgan the next morning.

But there was more. His name wasn’t Holt, nor was it Pearce. He was Erich Muenter, a former Harvard professor who had disappeared after being accused of poisoning his wife. He had spent time in Mexico before remarrying and finding employment at Cornell.

Muenter killed himself in jail soon after being arrested, so the police never got the whole story. But his terror spree was apparently not over. Before he died, he told people to expect an explosion on a ship sailing out of New York on July 7th. Sure enough, dynamite ripped a hole through the decks of the S.S. Minnehaha, which was carrying American-manufactured arms to England.

It may be that Muenter planted that bomb himself. But it’s equally possible that he was, as he hinted to his jailers, part of the larger network of German terrorists in the United States. This group of spies and saboteurs worked tirelessly to damage what they perceived to be American support for the Allies — while risking the possibility that they would bring the United States into the war against Germany.

While it’s unclear how deeply connected Muenter was to German intelligence, there’s no question about Franz von Rintelen. A wealthy Prussian nobleman who claimed to be related to the Kaiser, Rintelen was sent on a secret mission to sabotage American shipping.

As it became clear that the war was going to last years, not months, and that it would be won by the countries that could best feed and supply their troops, the Germans began to worry that the ostensibly neutral Americans were a grave threat. While the United States did not enter the war until 1917, its banks, including J.P. Morgan, and manufacturers were important suppliers of money and weapons to the Allies (many American companies were willing to sell to Germany, too, but a British blockade made that impossible). The German Navy sank some of these vessels, but many of these goods were shipped on boats with passengers. After a German submarine sank the Lusitania, killing more than 1,000 people, the Germans paused unrestricted submarine warfare, but remained determined to stop the Americans from supplying the Allies.

That’s where Rintelen — or Emil Gasche, as he identified himself on the passport he used to enter the United States — came in. He entered the United States with a suitcase filled with $500,000 in cash and went right to work undermining the arms trade between the U.S. and Britain. He and his associates set up fake corporations that bought up American gunpowder and then destroyed it; the idea was that this would shrink the supplies that were available to arms manufacturers and then make American ammunition more expensive. He even made a failed attempt to take over the gunpowder business of the DuPont company and tried to pay workers to strike in key factories.

Rintelen then moved on to more destructive methods. He and a chemist devised small “pencil” or “cigar bombs” which were small enough to slip into ammunition crates on boats. They were equipped with timed fuses that would, once a ship was out to sea, shoot flames into the adjacent ammunition and cause a huge explosion. Best of all for Rintelen, there was no evidence of foul play; newspaper reports at the time described the explosions as the sort of accident that happens when you put a lot of explosives in a ship’s hold. The cigar bombs damaged 36 ships.

Rintelen was also trying to ensnare the United States in conflicts that would distract it from taking a larger role in the European war. He met with Victoriano Huerta, a Mexican politician vying for control of his country in a messy civil war, and promised him German support as long as Huerta promised to invade the United States to reclaim territory in Texas and other southwestern states. German U-boats would bring supplies to Huerta’s troops and allow him to “have his revenge” on his enemies. Though Huerta never invaded, the so-called Plan of San Diego resulted in a number of raids across the border from Mexico to Texas.

Unbeknownst to Rintelen, British intelligence was on to him and was likely able to decode his messages back to Germany. In August of 1915, British agents sent him a false message that appeared to order him to return to Germany. It was a trap; British agents boarded his ship, and he spent the next four years in an Atlanta prison.

Though Rintelen was in jail for the rest of the war, his project continued. Other German agents took up the bombing campaign, with quite a bit of success. One of their attacks caused one of the largest conventional explosions in history.

On “Black Tom” Island, a man-made spit of land in New York Harbor, shipping companies maintained a series of warehouses that contained one of the largest arsenals in the world, almost all of which was destined to end up in Allied hands. On July 30, 1916, all 2,000,000 pounds of explosives detonated between 2:00 and 3:00 a.m. The detonation killed seven people, damaged the Statue of Liberty, and registered at 5.5 on the Richter scale.

As with some of the ship explosions, authorities first believed the event to be an accident. But, after the war, evidence surfaced linking the German government to the explosion. It seems that agents associated with Rintelen had set the fires — possibly with cigar bombs — that caused the huge explosion. The German government later paid reparations to the United States for the damage and loss of life.

The Black Tom bombing was the German terrorist attack that caused the largest loss of human life, but it was nothing compared to what German agents did to American horses.

In addition to supplying the Allies with explosives, the United States also shipped thousands of horses — which were essential to the war effort — across the Atlantic. In 1915, German agents recruited Anton Dilger, a German-American scientist who had traveled to Germany to treat injured soldiers. Dilger’s experience made him determined to do what he could to help the German war effort. He returned to the United States with samples of the glanders bacteria, which is extremely deadly to horses.

With his brother Carl, Anton set up a lab in Maryland where they bred glanders and anthrax. They tested the germs on guinea pigs and then delivered them to agents of the German intelligence bureau. The plot killed thousands of horses before police began to suspect foul play and increased patrols around the horse corrals. Anton Dilger left the country before his plot could be discovered. He eventually made his way to Mexico. His brother Carl remained behind, building cigar bombs until his penchant for getting drunk and talking too much got him drummed out of the service of the Kaiser.

The German terrorism campaign in the United States was well-planned enough to evade detection — most of the strong evidence about the coordinated nature of this activity came out after the war was over. It was clever and audacious, too. But it was also a failure. The German sabotage efforts never put a serious dent in American industry or stopped American shipments to the Allies.

As Americans became more suspicious about German sabotage, the German efforts to keep America from helping the Allies got more reckless. Germany eventually took more drastic measures, resuming submarine attacks against American shipping and making renewed overtures to Mexico in an attempt to incite an invasion of the United States. These decisions would, by April 1917, result in an American declaration of war against Germany. In the end, the men who had been sent to keep the Americans out of the war had spent two years giving the Americans reasons to enter it.
