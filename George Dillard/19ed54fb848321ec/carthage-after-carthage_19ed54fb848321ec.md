In late antiquity, there were a lot of writings about Jesus floating around, but which ones were divinely inspired and which were just, you know, books? Christians spent centuries arguing about the issue and slowly narrowed in on a set of scriptures that they deemed holy. By the 300s, the church had mostly decided on a set of books that belonged in the Bible. Many of the significant moments in this process happened in Africa. An Alexandrian bishop named Carthage? The same city that the Romans burned to the ground? The place where they salted the soil so that nothing would ever grow there again? Yep, that Carthage, although the story about the salt is almost certainly untrue (though often repeated, it Carthage was clearly a major city in imperial Rome — by some estimates, the biggest city in the west next to the capital itself — and an early hotbed of Christianity. Tertullian, a major theologian from Carthage during the days when the religion was illegal, Stories of Carthage usually end with its defeat at the hands of the Romans in 146 BCE, but the truth is that the city had a long and fruitful second act. The Romans built a new city, which thrived through the fall of Rome and hung on as a Byzantine possession well into the 600s CE before finally being destroyed by Arab armies.
I’m not sure why I love them so much, but mosaics are my favorite form of ancient art, and Roman Carthage produced an unbelievable assortment of them. What’s more, these are some of the best Roman mosaics because North Africa yielded a wider variety of colored stones for artists to use.
