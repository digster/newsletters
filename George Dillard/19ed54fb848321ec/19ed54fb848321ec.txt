In late antiquity, there were a lot of writings about Jesus floating around, but which ones were divinely inspired and which were just, you know, books? Christians spent centuries arguing about the issue and slowly narrowed in on a set of scriptures that they deemed holy. 
By the 300s, the church had mostly decided on a set of books that belonged in the Bible. Many of the significant moments in this process happened in Africa. An Alexandrian bishop named Athanasius [ https://substack.com/redirect/3cb33ce1-02ad-4159-a668-f3d2803f600d?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] listed the canonical books in 367, and then important church leaders met in Carthage to ratify the list in 397. 
Carthage? The same city that the Romans burned to the ground? The place where they salted the soil so that nothing would ever grow there again?
Yep, that Carthage, although the story about the salt is almost certainly untrue (though often repeated, it is not supported by any ancient evidence [ https://substack.com/redirect/ec11ea12-6454-4f39-8502-8e68798fcf73?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ]). In fact, Carthage was the site of at least nine major councils of the Church between 251 and 525, some of which were attended by luminaries like Augustine of Hippo. 
Carthage was clearly a major city in imperial Rome — by some estimates, the biggest city in the west next to the capital itself — and an early hotbed of Christianity. Tertullian, a major theologian from Carthage during the days when the religion was illegal, wrote [ https://substack.com/redirect/237706f7-efec-4206-8510-2cb7d5c06b4a?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] in a letter to Roman officials that Christians “have filled every place among you—cities, islands, fortresses, towns, market-places, the very camp, tribes, companies, palace, senate, forum,—we have left nothing to you but the temples of your gods.”
Stories of Carthage usually end with its defeat at the hands of the Romans in 146 BCE, but the truth is that the city had a long and fruitful second act. The Romans built a new city, which thrived through the fall of Rome and hung on as a Byzantine possession well into the 600s CE before finally being destroyed by Arab armies. 
As we saw in Sunday’s newsletter [ https://substack.com/redirect/a926dfeb-a750-43e4-88b4-aa8813b3c8a6?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ], the Romans did a thorough job of destroying the old Carthage. But a lot more remains from Roman Carthage — most notably, an exceptional collection of mosaics that depict lots of aspects of Roman life. 
I’m not sure why I love them so much, but mosaics are my favorite form of ancient art, and Roman Carthage produced an unbelievable assortment of them. What’s more, these are some of the best Roman mosaics because North Africa yielded a wider variety of colored stones for artists to use.
