If I asked you to imagine nuclear warfare, I bet you’d picture something out of the movies. You’d probably imagine missiles streaking across the sky, powerful warheads obliterating entire cities, that sort of thing. And, yes, that was one version of nuclear warfare. But the clever scientists on both sides of the Cold War envisioned many more ways to kill people with fission than your basic global thermonuclear exchange.
There was, for example, “Atomic Annie,” officially known as the M65 atomic cannon, which featured in Dwight Eisenhower’s inaugural parade in 1953. This mobile cannon, pulled by a pair of tractors, was designed to move around a battlefield and launch atomic shells at the enemy. These guns could fire a warhead of about 15 kilotons, which was roughly equivalent to the bomb that destroyed Hiroshima in 1945.
Around the same time, the United States deployed the “Davy Crockett Weapon System,” a launcher that sat on a tripod and could be operated by a small crew of soldiers. It was built to launch the W54 nuclear warhead, the smallest American nuclear weapon, which had a yield equivalent to 20 tons of TNT.
The W54 warhead (also used in air-to-air missiles) was built to be portable, meaning that it could be worn in a backpack if necessary, carried by a soldier or paratrooper, and placed as a “special atomic demolition munition” near an enemy target.
The Soviets themselves were no slouches when it came to atomic ingenuity. They, too, developed mortar systems like the 2S4 Tyulpan to launch nuclear shells at enemy targets. They planned the T-15 nuclear torpedo, which was designed to travel more than a dozen miles toward enemy naval bases and detonate, causing both a nuclear explosion and a tsunami. And, after the USSR fell, insiders said that the country had been working on a “suitcase bomb [ https://substack.com/redirect/20032598-4b0c-4087-9132-c49abc1b7454?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ]” designed to be carried by KGB agents into enemy facilities.
The terrifying ingenuity of American and Soviet weapons designers complicated the idea of nuclear war. But none of these inventions was as controversial as the so-called neutron bomb, which captured the public imagination in the 1970s.
The neutron bomb was, depending on who you listened to, either a more humane weapon or the apotheosis of monstrous Cold War logic. And the debates around its deployment still echo in our conversations about technology and warfare today.
Samuel Cohen [ https://substack.com/redirect/7a92f6b5-b40a-4129-a9f7-27e88af9a754?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] was a classic American meritocratic success story.
The child of Jewish immigrants — his father was a carpenter in Brooklyn — he soon proved to be a bright student. He went off to college at UCLA and then joined the military. The Army recognized his talent and sent him for further schooling at MIT. He was then sent to work on the Manhattan Project, where he helped create the bomb that destroyed Nagasaki. After the war, he worked at the Rand Corporation. There, one day, he pulled out the slide rule he had gotten as a 15th-birthday gift and did the math that led to the neutron bomb.
What Cohen figured out was that, by tweaking the specifications of a bomb and the casing material, designers could create a weapon that emitted a lot of radiation in the form of neutrons without a big explosion. It was the opposite of the traditional nuclear weapon, which aimed to maximize the concussive power of a detonation. The neutron radiation emitted by the bomb would dissipate quickly, making the area safe for human habitation soon after the detonation.
Cohen promoted his new discovery (he called it an “Enhanced Radiation Weapon”), and the press reported on it (U.S. News said [ https://substack.com/redirect/3195b2fb-1b5f-4a95-9526-d3fb89aa2b0b?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] it would “kill man with streams of poisonous radiation, while leaving machines and buildings undamaged”). The U.S. government tested several versions of the device in the early 1960s and flirted with putting them on short-range missiles, but never deployed the weapon. This didn’t stop the Soviets from using it for propaganda purposes. Nikita Khrushchev said the neutron bomb was designed [ https://substack.com/redirect/7a92f6b5-b40a-4129-a9f7-27e88af9a754?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] “to kill a man in such a way that his suit will not be stained with blood, in order to appropriate the suit.”
The neutron bomb might never have gotten much attention if not for Walter Pincus, a Washington Post reporter who wrote a series of attention-getting articles about the bomb in the late 1970s (the first, in June, 1977: “Neutron Killer Warhead Buried in ERDA Budget [ https://substack.com/redirect/8a10cd89-00fe-44b7-85a7-9e66ef43360a?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ]”). He described how these weapons killed people — “’almost immediate incapacitation,’ with convulsions, intermittent stupor and a lack of muscle coordination” — and filled his reports with quotes from experts full of ominous terms like “kill radius.”
When Pincus wrote his article, the government had resumed thinking about deploying the neutron bomb again for several reasons. The old tactical nuclear weapons of the 1950s and 1960s (like “Atomic Annie”) were aging and needed to be replaced. Experts worried that the overwhelming strength of Soviet armor in central Europe would be impossible to stop without the use of tactical nuclear weapons. Even though Soviet tanks had been shielded against blast damage, the soldiers inside them would still be vulnerable to neutron radiation. Finally, war planners envisioned fighting the next world war in West Germany or France; they wanted a way to defeat enemy troops without destroying NATO’s own infrastructure.
Press coverage of the neutron bomb created a firestorm. The Soviet Union issued statements saying that the bomb exposed the hollowness of American ideology — it was “monstrous [ https://substack.com/redirect/c7fc8932-2039-47ed-b8f2-7f0d5b7eb7dc?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ]” and “barbaric,” a dangerous new escalation in the arms race. Soviet messaging said that the bomb was “the perfect capitalist weapon [ https://substack.com/redirect/ffc00dee-ae3e-4ae0-b1fc-1b7e0e4d152f?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ]” because it was designed to spare property but kill people.
Writers [ https://substack.com/redirect/3195b2fb-1b5f-4a95-9526-d3fb89aa2b0b?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] in the United States, too, saw the bomb as a result of Cold War ideology pursued too far, of “the moral idiocy of military technocrats.” A growing anti-nuclear [ https://substack.com/redirect/eb3b32d5-6f4b-4172-a5b9-f8d6953c425a?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] movement marched against the weapon, outraged that the American government would consider such a cruel weapon. Experts feared that, because the neutron bomb would be seen as a “limited” nuclear option, it would prove too tempting for battlefield commanders and make nuclear war more likely (not everyone was opposed to the bomb: Pope Paul VI gave Cohen a medal [ https://substack.com/redirect/7a92f6b5-b40a-4129-a9f7-27e88af9a754?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] in 1978 for his contributions to peace).
The press around the neutron bomb also created a diplomatic mess. Jimmy Carter, who was uncomfortable about the implications of the weapon, was not sure whether to manufacture it. He first asked [ https://substack.com/redirect/ffc00dee-ae3e-4ae0-b1fc-1b7e0e4d152f?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] European leaders to take the political risk of committing to the deployment of American neutron weapons in their country, then decided to indefinitely postpone his decision on producing them. Leaders like Helmut Schmidt, feeling hung out to dry, never forgave Carter.
Ronald Reagan briefly resurrected the neutron bomb controversy when he took office and restarted the arms race. He announced the production of neutron warheads, but never deployed them. They sat, stockpiled in the United States, until the Cold War ended.
Samuel Cohen maintained to the end that the neutron bomb had been maligned. He told [ https://substack.com/redirect/7a92f6b5-b40a-4129-a9f7-27e88af9a754?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] the press that “It’s the most sane and moral weapon ever devised. It’s the only nuclear weapon in history that makes sense in waging war. When the war is over, the world is still intact.”
In his perhaps-too-rational mind, the neutron bomb just made sense. He’d been scarred by the destruction he’d seen as an observer in Seoul during the Korean War. If you were going to fight a nuclear war, he figured, why would you create unnecessary destruction? Better to zap the enemy with neutron radiation and spare yourself the rebuilding process.
The story of the neutron bomb is a good example of what can happen when military logic comes into contact with the real world. What seemed like a perfectly rational outcome to military planners was hideous and inhumane to real people, who didn’t want to think of human life as more expendable than buildings or bridges.
One of the reasons that the United States decided not to deploy the neutron bomb is that it was also developing the Assault Breaker [ https://substack.com/redirect/85461483-e16a-4887-ba85-6cf57debe313?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ], an early version of a precision-guided weapons system that could target enemy tanks much more accurately than ever before. This technology begat others that eventually evolved [ https://substack.com/redirect/85461483-e16a-4887-ba85-6cf57debe313?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] into the semi-autonomous drones that patrolled the skies over the Middle East and the “algorithmic warfare [ https://substack.com/redirect/2bd30487-162c-4a90-9e55-e4af83f9e628?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ]” technologies that militaries are racing to produce today.
One version of the future of warfare may look like AI systems and unmanned drones [ https://substack.com/redirect/be66b0a1-5310-4e84-a159-005210d458ec?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] making decisions for themselves according to “rational” rules of warfare. They will, I suppose, be programmed to keep war “clean” and minimize “collateral damage.” But they, like the neutron bomb, may strip something important away from war — the horror that should come with it.
In the 1970s, people recoiled at the idea of a tidy neutron-bomb war, partially because they worried that this would make war more palatable to leaders. We should be worried today about the same thing. If our leaders — or the robots to whom they outsource their thinking — come to believe that there is such a thing as a “clean” war, will they be even more open to taking human life?
