If I asked you to imagine nuclear warfare, I bet you’d picture something out of the movies. You’d probably imagine missiles streaking across the sky, powerful warheads obliterating entire cities, that sort of thing. And, yes, that was one version of nuclear warfare. But the clever scientists on both sides of the Cold War envisioned many more ways to kill people with fission than your basic global thermonuclear exchange. There was, for example, “Atomic Annie,” officially known as the M65 atomic cannon, which featured in Dwight Eisenhower’s inaugural parade in 1953. This mobile cannon, pulled by a pair of tractors, was designed to move around a battlefield and launch atomic shells at the enemy. These guns could fire a warhead of about 15 kilotons, which was roughly equivalent to the bomb that destroyed Hiroshima in 1945. Around the same time, the United States deployed the “Davy Crockett Weapon System,” a launcher that sat on a tripod and could be operated by a small crew of soldiers. It was built to launch the W54 nuclear warhead, the smallest American nuclear weapon, which had a yield equivalent to 20 tons of TNT. The W54 warhead (also used in air-to-air missiles) was built to be portable, meaning that it could be worn in a backpack if necessary, carried by a soldier or paratrooper, and placed as a “special atomic demolition munition” near an enemy target. The Soviets themselves were no slouches when it came to atomic ingenuity. They, too, developed mortar systems like the 2S4 Tyulpan to launch nuclear shells at enemy targets. They planned the T-15 nuclear torpedo, which was designed to travel more than a dozen miles toward enemy naval bases and detonate, causing both a nuclear explosion and a tsunami. But none of these inventions was as controversial as the so-called neutron bomb, which captured the public imagination in the 1970s. The neutron bomb was, depending on who you listened to, either a more humane weapon or the apotheosis of monstrous Cold War logic. And the debates around its deployment still echo in our conversations about technology and warfare today.

If you were going to fight a nuclear war, he figured, why would you create unnecessary destruction? Better to zap the enemy with neutron radiation and spare yourself the rebuilding process. The story of the neutron bomb is a good example of what can happen when military logic comes into contact with the real world. What seemed like a perfectly rational outcome to military planners was hideous and inhumane to real people, who didn’t want to think of human life as more expendable than buildings or bridges.
