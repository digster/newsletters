That was destroyed by the Romans, along with the rest of Carthage and all of its people’s literature. From this large empire centered on one of the world’s great cities, we have no poetry, no hymns, no myths, no history. It was all destroyed by the Romans. So when I say we have an account of Hanno’s voyages, what I mean is that we have an apparent Greek translation of a Carthaginian original. It’s hard to say how accurate it is (it’s certainly changed a bit, as it talks about Hanno establishing a “Temple of Neptune” in Africa). This is the problem with the Carthaginians. Most of the views we can get of them come through others’ eyes, even though they were a major civilization that rivaled the Romans for control of the Mediterranean world. This is because the Romans did such an effective job of destroying Carthage and literally covering it over with a giant Roman city. So who were the Carthaginians? Let’s try to sift through the few artifacts that remained after the Roman destruction of the city and let them tell us about themselves. Hanno’s adventurous voyage down the coast of Africa makes sense if you understand the origins of the Carthaginians. The city was one of hundreds of colonies established by the Phoenecians, who built an The part of Africa that makes up modern-day Tunisia, jutting out into the Mediterranean, was a perfect place for the Phoenicians to establish a trade city. They first established the city of Utica there, and then, by the 800s BCE, Carthage, which means “New City.” It was a perfect spot for a settlement: on a defensible hill, above a sheltered harbor, at the center of the trade routes that connected Spain, Italy, Sicily, Greece, and the Middle East. The Phoenicians brought their gods with them to Africa, and their ideas of the divine mingled with those of the other civilizations they encountered. Ba’al Hammon, who controlled whether crops grew, was the husband; here’s a later, Romanized image of him from Carthage: His wife or consort was the goddess Tanit, whose worship seems to have been central to Carthaginian religion. Her domain was vast: she was both a fierce warrior goddess and a patron of motherhood and fertility. Images of her seem to have been everywhere in Carthage. She shows up on coinage: And here’s a beautiful statue of her from Carthaginian Spain: Sometimes she had a lion’s head: But more common is her symbol. The so-called sign of Tanit, which looks a little like an Egyptian ankh, seems to have been everywhere in Carthage, demonstrating her centrality to their religion. Why was Tanit everywhere? Some scholars think that her symbol was a way to ward off evil and curses, but like so much else about Carthaginian society, we don’t know much for sure. It’s all refracted through Roman accounts, and since Rome was Carthage’s greatest enemy, it’s hard to say whether the Romans were telling the truth. Perhaps the biggest controversy over Carthaginian religion regards child sacrifice. Several Roman sources say that the Carthaginians killed their children as a part of some religious ritual.
