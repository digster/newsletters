Chocolate: a Stimulant That Made the Modern World

"There is nothing comparable in the world"

George Dillard
May 10
 
READ IN APP
 

This week in our series on the stimulants that made the modern world: my personal favorite, chocolate!

Until 1957, when Pope Pius XII reduced the requirement to three hours, Catholics were expected to fast from midnight on mass days until they received the Eucharist. In fact, it was considered virtuous to refrain from eating in the morning even on days when one did not attend mass, a sign of one’s ability to resist bodily temptations.

This meant that the Spanish women of Chiapas, in Mexico, were pushing their luck when they had their servants deliver them cups of chocolate in the middle of mass, just before communion. They claimed that they suffered from “weakness and squeamishness of stomach” that required a little drink.

The local bishop threatened them with excommunication. The women started attending mass in a smaller church where the priest was less likely to scold them for their chocolate habit. The bishop died a week later, supposedly after drinking — you guessed it — chocolate. Afterward, people in the region quipped, “Beware the chocolate of Chiapas.”

Chiapas wasn’t the only place where there was a theological debate about drinking chocolate. The Catholic Church didn’t quite know how to categorize the stuff. Was it a drink and therefore permissible during fasting periods, or did it provide sustenance and deserve to be banned?

In the end, the Church realized it was fighting a losing battle. People enjoyed their morning chocolate so much that they were willing to risk eternal damnation for it. By 1662, theologians had decreed that chocolate, if drunk, was OK — “Liquidum non frangit jejunum”: “Liquid doesn’t break the fast.”

This little contretemps demonstrated the confusion that bedeviled Europeans in the centuries after they first encountered chocolate. What, exactly, was this stuff? Was it even tasty? Who was it for, and how should it be consumed?

As with some of the other stimulants that Europeans first encountered in the early modern period, like tobacco, coffee, and tea (chocolate contains caffeine and theobromine, another upper), there was a lot of disagreement about whether chocolate was good, or good for you.

An Italian named Girolamo Benzoni was served some chocolate in Nicaragua, and wrote that it “seemed more a drink for pigs, than a drink for humanity. I was in this country for more than one year, and never wanted to taste it.” A Jesuit named Jose de Acosta declared that no one who had grown up without chocolate could learn to enjoy it. He compared it to “feces.” Many Spanish colonists saw chocolate as intrinsically linked to indigenous people’s “idolatry” or witchcraft. Women were said to “bewitch” men with chocolate potions.

But others had more nuanced views. Juan de Cardenas, in his Problems and Marvelous Secrets of the Indies, published in 1591, took an evenhanded approach:

In terms of the harms and beneﬁts, I hear every person with his own opinion: some despise chocolate, considering it the inventor of numerous sicknesses; others say that there is nothing comparable in the world . . . there is no one who does not present his or her judgment to the populace.

Despite people’s reservations, chocolate soon swept across the world. Historian Marcy Norton argues that chocolate was the "first stimulant beverage consumed in significant quantities by Europeans.” It preceded coffee and tea. It was, in some ways, a gateway drug that paved the way for more heavily caffeinated beverages to come.

So what was the American chocolate that Europeans encountered?

First, it was quite important to Mesoamerican society. People put a lot of resources into both depicting and using it. People had special containers for chocolate, like this one found in the Yucatan:

This vessel, which has an inscription identifying it as the “cacao drinking cup of Chakjal Mukuuy,” was clearly for special occasions as practiced by a high-status person:

And this Mayan cup, for drinking chocolate, features a waterbird on one side:

And a series of glyphs, slanted so that they’d be straight when someone was drinking from the cup. They read: “It is his cup for fresh (or pure) cacao.”

There were lots of preparations, many of which would be quite unfamiliar to the chocolate-enjoyer of today. People fermented cacao beans, ground them up, and mixed them with all kinds of things. There was a relatively bitter mixture of chocolate, water, and achiote, a spice that gave it a reddish color. Some people mixed it with chili peppers to make it spicy. Some mixed it with masa or other forms of maize. Others put dried, aromatic flowers in it. The preparation closest to our own heavily-sweetened chocolate consumption contained honey.

Whatever people mixed with chocolate, most of the time they prepared their drinks by pouring them between two containers. This is one of the larger containers that would rest on the ground; people would dip their cups into it to serve themselves when the drinks were ready:

Chocolate seems not to have been an everyday drink for common people. It was reserved for nobles and special occasions. Great warriors might be rewarded with a cup, visiting diplomats would be treated to it, and couples getting married would get a sip on their big night. It was also valuable. In the Codex Mendoza, a 16th-century attempt to capture Aztec culture, loads of cacao and drinking cups are listed as a form of tribute right next to the jaguar skins:

Chocolate got its first foothold outside the Americas in Seville, where thousands of pounds of the stuff entered Europe every year by the 1620s. I really like this 1644 illustration of Poseidon delivering chocolate to Europe from across the sea:

For a while, Europeans copied many of the bitter or spicy preparations that they learned in the Americas. Or they tried to, at least. Eurasian spices like cinnamon and pepper replaced the American ones. By the end of the 1700s, Europeans were drinking chocolate with sugar as a sweetener and a little bit of cinnamon.

As worldly Spaniards took up chocolate drinking, people in other countries did, as well. Works on chocolate were translated into English by the 1640s, and there were several chocolate shops in London by the 1650s. The diarist Samuel Pepys reported using a morning dose of chocolate to settle his stomach after a wild night in 1661. Chocolate seems to have been sold in the same places as coffee, although by the end of the century, vendors tended to specialize in one or the other.

Chocolate was, by the 1700s, a sophisticated way to start the day. This Dutch girl is sitting down to her morning chocolate in a 1756 painting:

Chocolate became associated in some minds with decadent laziness. A 1704 poem described a “Lass of London mould” who “without fail was wak’d at Ten, Drank Chocolate, then slept again.” Maybe it was describing somebody like this fancy Italian lady who takes her chocolate in bed:

Like the Mayans, Europeans had fancy serving vessels for chocolate:

Chocolate showed up in still lifes as part of a luxurious display of food and household items:

By 1668, the first Earl of Sandwich wrote down a recipe:

Prepare the chocolatti . . . and Then Putt the vessell that hath the Chocolatti in it,into a Jaraffa [i.e. a carafe] of snow stirred together with some salt, into tender Curdled Ice & soe eate a with spoons & shaike the snow together sometyme & it will putt the Chocolatti into tender Curdled Ice & soe eate it with spoons.

Food innovation ran in the family, I guess (the fourth Earl of Sandwich is somewhat dubiously credited with inventing, well, the sandwich), because this seems to be an early version of one of the world’s great foods, chocolate ice cream. Chocolate was well on its way to becoming the sweet, energizing treat that we know and love today.
