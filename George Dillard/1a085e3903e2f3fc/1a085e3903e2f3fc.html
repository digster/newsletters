Portraits of Singapore

Snapshots of a growing colonial city

George Dillard
Sep 9
∙
Preview
 
READ IN APP
 

A post is coming on Sunday about Singapore more generally, as a key node in the British Empire, a chokepoint for trade, and a hub of the global opium trade. But today I wanted to spend a little bit of time looking at the people who lived in the city in the 19th century and daydream a little bit about what their lives must have been like.

The growth of British Singapore coincided directly with the advent of photography as an art form and a way of recording aspects of daily life, so it’s not surprising that as the city grew, photography studios popped up. Western photographers in Singapore tended to have a few types of subjects: they captured photos of European colonialists, prominent local people, and what they called “types” — ethnographic photos of people from certain religious or ethnic groups.

The images of Europeans in Singapore were designed to be indistinguishable from the types of photos that they might have taken back in London, in keeping with the idea that they were establishing an outpost of “European civilization” in Southeast Asia. The studio also photographed people from all of the various groups that populated the booming city — groups that the British carefully categorized, ranked, and segregated. Many of these people wear some kind of “national costume” and are identified only by ethnicity — not by occupation or any other aspect of their personality.

I wonder whether getting photographed in this clothing was their idea or the photographer’s. Looking at many of these photos, I can’t help but wonder how these people ended up in Singapore, what these photos show us of their personalities, and what they conceal...
