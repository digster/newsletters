What Types of Leaders Make Sporting Events All About Themselves?

From Nero to Trump

George Dillard
Apr 1
 
READ IN APP
 

The Roman Emperor Nero fancied himself a skilled singer and athlete, and he knew that nobody could tell him otherwise. The historian Suetonius tells us that Nero was so obsessed with Greek arts and athletic festivals that he undertook a tour of Greece to participate in them. The emperor forced the Greeks to hold their festivals in unscheduled years to coincide with his visit and he made himself the center of attention at each and every one.

Nero — who had become emperor as a spoiled teenager and never evolved much beyond that — dominated every competition he entered. When he entered a singing contest, no one was allowed to leave the theater while he was performing. Suetonius tells us that some people faked death so that they could be carried out rather than listen to the ruler’s caterwauling.

Nero had statues of past winners removed so that they didn’t overshadow his glory. He won the Olympic chariot race even though he fell from his chariot and never finished the race.

All in all, Nero was awarded more than 1,800 prizes in various artistic and athletic competitions, many of which he never participated in. Sometimes he bribed competitors to take a dive, sometimes he paid back the city that hosted the games after he won, and sometimes he simply intimidated everyone into giving him what he wanted. Nero ruined these contests for everyone besides himself.

I think we’ll be spared seeing Donald Trump line up at striker for the United States soccer team at this year’s World Cup (golf majors may be in more danger; Trump already has a checkered record of cheating his way to club championships). But that doesn’t mean that he won’t try to make the World Cup all about himself. He understands that this is an event that draws global attention, and Trump is nothing if not attuned to opportunities to get people to pay attention to him. He already pushed his way to center stage at the World Cup draw last year, accepting the “FIFA Peace Prize” as part of the deal.

Just as Trump (along with Kash Patel) put himself in the center of the American men’s hockey win at the Olympics, turning what could have been a rare feel-good moment of national bonding into another tiresome partisan squabble, he will insert himself and his politics into this celebration of the world’s game. As we’ve seen with Nero, Trump wouldn’t be the first leader to use sports as a political weapon. What do leaders who do this have in common?

International sports competitions have always had a political element. When Pierre de Coubertin founded the modern Olympics in the 1890s, he viewed it as a way to harness nationalism toward a higher purpose, promoting democracy and peace through international cooperation. In a 1892 speech, he said about his movement that

It is democratic and international. The first of these characteristics will guarantee its future: anything that is not democratic is no longer viable today. As for the second, it opens unexpected prospect to us. (…). Let us export rowers, runners and fencers; this is the free trade of the future, and the day that it is introduced into the everyday existence of old Europe, the cause of peace will receive new and powerful support.

By 1930, FIFA, the international soccer federation, had decided to hold its first World Cup, inspired by founder Jules Rimet’s “idea of unifying and reconciling nations through sport.” Though the first World Cup, in Uruguay, mirrored Rimet’s lofty ideals, the second one did not.

The 1934 World Cup was held in Italy, and Benito Mussolini was determined to turn the event into a celebration of fascism — and himself. Understanding the significance of sports to the working classes on whose support he relied, Mussolini devotedly promoted the World Cup. He spent heavily to refurbish the country’s stadiums, adorning many of them with signature fascist architecture. The venue in Turin was named the Stadio Municipale Benito Mussolini. He even tried to bolster his populist bona fides by making sure the press caught him in line to buy his own ticket for the opening match against the United States.

Il Duce understood that a victory for Italy would be good for his regime, as well. Luckily, he was helped by the fact that several South American teams, along with the teams from the British Isles, did not attend (this was not a principled stand against fascism; the boycotts were over sporting disputes). To make sure that Italy won, Mussolini intervened in the selection of referees for matches, choosing a young, less experienced ref for Italy matches and then inviting him up into the VIP box before games for a pep talk.

When Italy did win the final, Mussolini handed out the medals himself — along with a custom-made trophy called the Coppa del Duce that he had commissioned for the event. It was much larger than the real World Cup trophy, requiring four men to carry it onto the field.

A few days later, the triumphant Italian dictator met in Venice with the new German leader Adolf Hitler. Mussolini saw Hitler as a rival and refused to use a translator, instead relying on his shaky knowledge of German to communicate. Though the two countries pledged solidarity, the men themselves left the meeting with bad feelings. Hitler did take one thing from Mussolini, though, which was how to use an international sporting contest for political purposes.

Hitler saw the 1936 Berlin Olympics as a showcase for his Nazi project, and was even less shy than Mussolini about putting a personal stamp on the games. He and his propagandists injected pageantry to the opening ceremonies of the games, which, up until then, had been a simple parade of nations and flag raising. The Germans added a torch relay and performance featuring thousands of singers and dancers to promote the idea that “Aryan” culture was the heir of the legacy of ancient Greece.

It was the precursor of the elaborate modern opening ceremonies, and it was also fascist propaganda. Hitler knew to play nice while the games were underway — he decreased the persecution of Jews and other minorities in the hopes of presenting to the world a picture of fascist order and progress. But the Nazi regime was ever-present. When German athletes won, they gave the Nazi salute; spectators chanted “Sieg Heil!” when Hitler entered an arena.

Though Jesse Owens’ victories in track and field damaged Hitler’s claim of racial superiority, the Berlin Olympics were nevertheless a powerful weapon in the Nazis’ propaganda arsenal.

When the 1978 World Cup took place in Argentina, the country was in the midst of a “Dirty War,” in which a junta led by General Jorge Rafael Videla was hunting down and disappearing thousands of suspected dissidents. The country’s economy was in decline, and its population was deeply divided. Even though he wasn’t much of a sports guy, Videla decided to try to turn the World Cup into a moment of national rebirth.

Videla spent heavily on infrastructure and pageantry for the events and made himself a presence at the games. In fact, he was rumored to have gone into the Peruvian locker room right before a match that Argentina had to win by four goals in order to advance. Peru played much worse than expected, and Argentina won 6–0. In return, the Videla government sent thousands of tons of grain to Peru and unfroze its government’s assets.

Though the Argentinians went on to win the World Cup that year, it didn’t do much for Videla. It only superficially obscured the oppression of the regime. People incarcerated in torture prisons recall hearing the crowd’s cheers from the stadiums down the road. Videla’s popularity continued to plummet and the junta was ousted by 1983.

There are other examples of leaders putting themselves at the center of athletic events; none of them flattering to Trump.

Zairian dictator Mobutu Sese Seko made the Muhammad Ali-George Foreman fight in Kinshasa (the so-called “Rumble in the Jungle”) a propaganda event for his regime, placing his face above the boxers’ on the official posters for the event. Vladimir Putin — who has long promoted himself as a manly athlete — made the 2014 Sochi Olympics and the 2018 World Cup into a sales pitch for his regime, spending more on the Olympics than anyone had before. Four days after the Sochi closing ceremonies, he spent some of the goodwill he had built up by invading Ukraine.

By now, you’ve noticed a trend. I looked for democratically-oriented leaders who made themselves the center of attention at an event like the Olympics or World Cup, but couldn’t find any. Sure, they show up at sporting events, but they don’t try to turn them into a one-man show.

It seems that the desire to hijack a sporting event for personal propaganda purposes is an authoritarian impulse. No wonder Donald Trump is so eager to do just that this summer.
