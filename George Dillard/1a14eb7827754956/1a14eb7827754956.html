Galveston, 1900

The port before the storm

George Dillard
Oct 18
 
READ IN APP
 

The second post in this series about places on the brink is about a city that thought it was on the verge of unparalleled prosperity until a natural disaster that they could not foresee (or maybe didn’t want to foresee) destroyed everything.

On March 18, 1900, the Galveston Chamber of Commerce held its seventh annual banquet at the Tremont Hotel. Its program showed a railroad heading off toward the bright future on the horizon:

You can tell it was a super fancy affair because the whole menu was in French (black coffee sounds so much more pedestrian than “café noir”):

A band played music while everybody ate their steak. You’ll note the nods to Polish and Hungarian culture:

And then the city’s dignitaries got up to give boosterish speeches about the business opportunities that lie ahead in the dawning century:

For those who were invited to attend, the evening must have been full of promise. Here were the great and good men of a city on the rise, celebrating their accomplishments and looking forward to an age of prosperity. Six months later, it would all be gone.

The civic leaders of Galveston must have felt on that night that they had won the race to which many of them had devoted their working lives. There was great opportunity for whoever could connect the booming American West to the rest of the country and the world, and the businessmen of Galveston thought they had done it.

Cities all over the Gulf Coast of Texas saw an opportunity to establish themselves as the “Gateway port to the West.” The problem was that most of the candidate cities were unsuited for the task. Texas’s coast was riddled with sandbars and wandering rivers, making it difficult for ships to get all the way to land. They often had to transfer their cargo to shallower-hulled boats and then unload again at the port. The two main contenders for the crown were Indianola, where German immigrants had helped to build busy ports:

And Galveston, which sat on an island that commanded the entrance to Trinity Bay and Houston:

In 1875, Indianola was devastated by a hurricane. It tried to recover, but was hit again in 1886. The town was largely abandoned; today, much of it is underwater.

The destruction of Indianola secured Galveston’s position as the dominant port in Texas. The city boomed in population, nearing 40,000 residents by 1900 (almost double the 1880 figure). It was an entry point for thousands of immigrants, especially people from Central and Eastern Europe. Its ports shipped cotton to the rest of the world:

Banks and other institutions established themselves there, giving the city a reputation as the “Wall Street of the Southwest.” The city was home to many fine buildings, like this bank:

There were lovely parks (the Tremont Hotel, where the Chamber of Commerce banquet was held, is in the background here):

Bustling ports:

And, of course, beaches:

There was just one problem: all this was just 7 feet above sea level in an area prone to hurricanes. But in these situations, where things are going so well, we find ways to ignore looming danger, and we listen to the experts who are willing to help us pretend that nothing’s wrong. And that’s what the people of Galveston did.

In 1891, a meteorologist named Isaac Cline of the brand-new U.S. Weather Bureau wrote in the Galveston Daily News that Texas was “not liable to serious damage” from hurricanes. His article was full of scientific terminology and sophisticated-looking maps and figures. He surveyed the recent hurricanes in the Gulf of Mexico and declared that the Texas coast “is much less subject to severe meteorological disturbances than many other portions of the country” and dismissed fears of a hurricane in Galveston as “an absurd delusion [that] can only have its origin in the imagination and not from reasoning.”

Convinced that disasters would be infrequent and that the long, shallow shelf offshore would break up a storm surge if it did happen, Galveston’s civic leaders ignored calls for a seawall. It would be bad for business if people started to think that Galveston was vulnerable to a natural disaster, wouldn’t it?

It’s hard to imagine in these days when a simple severe thunderstorm watch can lead to beeping phones and overexcited weathermen taking over our TV screens, but there wasn’t much warning of the hurricane that destroyed Galveston on September 8, 1900.

It was rainy and windy, of course, but the story about a storm warning only appeared on page three of the Galveston Daily News. A report the previous day had announced “great damage” in Louisiana, but information was “meagre” because of downed wires. People were used to occasional tide swells, and seemed not to think much of the fact that the beginning of a storm surge was creeping up the beaches of Galveston.

Isaac Cline, who had written in 1891 that fear of hurricanes was irrational, realized that something terrible was on the horizon. He raced in his horsecart around the city, warning people to seek high ground. It was a brave act, but too late. A probable category 4 hurricane brought 120-mile-per-hour winds and 15-foot-high floodwaters, killing at least 5,000 people.

Horrific stories circulated around the country in the aftermath of the hurricane. The worst of them was probably the tale of St. Mary’s Orphanage, where the nuns tried to tie themselves with clotheslines to their charges to keep them from being swept away. All ten nuns died, and only three of the 94 children survived.

There was great national interest in the destruction of Galveston. I was struck to find a collection of stereographs of the destruction; it seems like Americans might have viewed these with morbid curiosity in the same way they would look at images of natural wonders:

Galveston was also the site of some of the earliest video journalism. Thomas Edison’s cameramen snuck onto the island in defiance of martial law to record videos of the wreckage. They panned solemnly over sites like the orphanage:

And one of the fanciest places in town, a swanky hotel suitable for fancy Chamber of Commerce dinners where wealthy men dream about getting even wealthier:

Galveston didn’t disappear into the Gulf like Indianola did; today, it’s a thriving beach town, although it is affected by a hurricane or tropical storm once every 2.5 years, on average. But it never reclaimed its place as Texas’s biggest port (Houston claimed that title), nor did it remain the “Wall Street of the Southwest.”

Its leaders, as they looked out toward their horizon in 1900, could not see what was coming for them.
