I’ve long been fascinated by moments when the world was on the verge of change, but the people living through events didn’t know where they were headed. So for the next few weeks, we’re going to be looking at societies on the precipice of something big. What was it like to live on the edge of historical change? We’ll start with the days before one of the biggest historical changes of the 20th century: Russia’s disastrous entry into World War I and what followed, including revolution and civil war. In 1913, Russians celebrated a big anniversary designed to showcase the country’s success. Little did they know that everything was about to fall apart. He first took the title in 1613, which meant that 1913, the year all of these items were produced, was the 300th anniversary, or tercentenary, of the royal family. The Romanovs had not celebrated the 100th anniversary of Michael’s accession in 1713, because at that moment Peter the Great was busy with a war and feuding with his son and heir. They didn’t do it in 1813, either, because they were embroiled in a terrible war against Napoleon. But 1913 seemed like the moment to celebrate the Russian monarchy. Nicholas wanted to send the message that things were going very well, that Russia was a modern country in the league of the other world powers, ruled by a powerful and popular royal family. That’s part of the reason that he copied other European monarchs’ penchant for putting themselves on coins and souvenirs. But, as we saw with the confusion around the stamps, Nicholas’s efforts often didn’t come off as intended. The celebrations around the tercentenary were complicated by Nicholas’s own struggles and the internal contradictions of Russian politics. It was a glamorous celebration, but it also revealed the weakness of the royal family’s position and foreshadowed the dark days that were right around the corner. First, let’s take a look at the glitz and glamor of the celebration. Beautiful decorations went up in St. Petersburg: There were parades in provinces like Estonia: Many of the celebrations took place in Kostroma, the town where sixteen-year-old Michael Romanov had accepted the crown three centuries before: But centering Kostroma presented another complication to the story the Romanovs were trying to tell. Michael, you see, had accepted the throne from an assembly of nobles and notables in 1613. But Nicholas had not gotten along with similar bodies in his own time, nor had he ever been terribly popular with the Russian people. After losing the Russo-Japanese War in 1905, Nicholas had faced bitter revolts from starving peasants and exploited workers. The revolution forced him to accept a Duma, or parliament, as part of the compromise that ended the war and saved his throne, but he dissolved it after 72 days. A second, more radical Duma lasted a few months before he dissolved that, too. Nicholas illegally altered the voting system to get a more compliant parliament after 1907. So it was awkward to have to acknowledge the fact that his ancestors were given power rather than inheriting it through divine right. The tsarina, Alexandra, suffered from crippling anxiety and illness, and skipped events or left early. Many of the people watching her at these celebrations, however, interpreted her stiffness as aristocratic coldness. Could the tsarina really not be bothered to come to a celebration of her own family? Meanwhile, the eight-year-old tsarevich, Alexei, who got pride of place in the Romanov family poster featured above, was suffering badly from hemophilia. You can see him being carried in some of the pictures of the celebration because he could not walk very far on his own: As the great and powerful Russians gathered at the Cathedral of Our Lady of Kazan to celebrate the tercentenary, another moment demonstrated the tensions that would soon tear Russia apart. The chairman of the Duma noticed a strange man, dressed in silk, sitting in the spots reserved for the Duma. As he approached, he realized that the intruder was Rasputin, the “holy man” who had wormed his way into the Romanovs’ inner circle by promising to heal Alexei. The two men engaged in a tense standoff, and Rasputin eventually consented to leave. After he stormed out of the church, the news of his presence and departure rippled through the crowd. Though the royal family worked hard to create an image of 300 years of Russian monarchy, they never settled on an effective image. The celebrations of 1913 thinly papered over the cracks in Russia, and the onset of war the very next year would force those cracks wider and wider. Even though they may have been aware of the country’s problems, the royal family could not have known that, within five years, they would be dead, along with two million of their countrymen and the nation of Russia itself.
