A narrow waterway between two major bodies of water is the perfect location for a major city, and the Bosporus is no exception. At its southwestern end, the Thracians built a city called Lygos, which was followed by a Greek city called Byzantium, which was renamed Constantinople by the Romans and Istanbul by the Turks. But Mandrocles’s feat was even more impressive than that because of the Bosporus’s unique qualities. The immense force of the water spilling from the Black Sea into the Sea of Marmara (and eventually the Aegean) creates a tricky “Devil’s Current” at the surface. But below, a countercurrent pushes heavier, saltier water from the Sea of Marmara north into the Black Sea. However, for most of recorded history, the Bosporus has been a chokepoint, too. As the Ottomans chipped away at the Byzantine Empire, they were faced with an impossible-seeming task: conquering the city of Constantinople, which was surrounded on three sides by water and on the fourth by nigh-impregnable walls. It must have been a little nerve-wracking to run this gauntlet even in peacetime: And these 18th-century maps make it clear just how tight a squeeze the strait was, and how commanding a position Istanbul had over it: Next to the Ottomans, the Bosporus was probably most important to Russia. The country’s only warm-water ports, which were on the Black Sea, were useful only if Russian ships could get out to the Mediterranean. From Peter the Great onward, Russian leaders schemed to keep the Bosporus open. The Crimean War of the 1850s was partially about Russian ambitions in the Bosporus; the Ottomans allowed British and French ships through the strait to fight against Russia. Eventually, most world powers agreed to keep the Bosporus closed to warships during peacetime unless the Ottomans agreed. After the Ottomans fell and the Cold War began, the Bosporus became less important. The Soviet Union had more ways to get its ships out to sea, and Turkey was not the world power that the Ottoman Empire had been. But one incident in 1946 illustrated the Bosporus’s significance even in the Cold War era. When the Turkish ambassador to the United States, Mehmet Münir Ertegün, died in 1944, his body remained in the United States until the end of the Second World War. When it came time to return his body in 1946, Harry Truman decided to send his casket home in style. The ambassador’s body was sent back to Istanbul on the U.S.S. Missouri — recently back home from hosting the signing of the Japanese surrender on its decks. The Missouri sailed right up the Bosporus and saluted Ertegün with its guns: The message was clear: Americans would sail through the Bosporus and the Soviets could not stop them. The straits still held symbolic power even in the nuclear age. For most of the Cold War period, the Bosporus was calm. The 1936 Montreux Convention had given Turkey control of the strait and contained clear provisions: merchant ships could travel freely but Turkey could deny warships passage. This was largely academic; the region was free of naval conflict for many decades. Starting in 1973, the Turks spanned the Bosporus several times with impressive suspension bridges, some of the largest in the world. It seemed like the strait would, as in Mandrocles’s day, be a connector rather than a chokepoint in a more peaceful world. But the peace did not last. Four days after the Russian invasion of Ukraine in 2022, Turkey invoked the Montreux Convention for the first time since World War II, denying passage to warships moving in and out of the Black Sea. Control of the Bosporus was once again an important strategic asset, as it had been for much of the last 2,500 years.
