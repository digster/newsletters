Is Our Children Learning

The declines in NAEP scores and other tests seem to be concentrated on the weakest students. The bottom of the distribution is falling through the floor and the rest is mostly holding up. This does not match anecdotal reports coming out of schools, which suggest widespread declines, although those are presumably untrustworthy and it’s traditional to think children are in general getting dumber. That doesn’t mean they aren’t but we need systematic evidence.

Standardized Tests Help Disadvantaged Students

Your periodic reminder. See this study, also this one and this one. SAT scores and other objective measures fight income inequality in college admissions.

Holistic admissions make it worse. But then everyone already knew that. A paper from Raj Chetty, David Deming and John Friedman brings the math.

Gerry Tan: If you *only* used SAT to admit to elite colleges, share of admits from top 1% income falls 15.8% → 9.9% and representation from <$200k rises by +8.8%, with no reduction in post-college outcomes.

It’s ‘holistic review’ and ‘ban SAT’ policy that allows the most wealthy and powerful to virtue signal while getting an edge.

Paper Abstract: The high-income admissions advantage at Ivy-Plus colleges is driven by three factors: (1) preferences for children of alumni, (2) weight placed on non-academic credentials, and (3) athletic recruitment.

…

The three factors that give children from high-income families an admissions advantage are uncorrelated or negatively correlated with post-college outcomes, whereas academic credentials such as SAT/ACT scores are highly predictive of post-college success.

Non-academic credentials, including most athletic slots (since most are not the popular and accessible sports like basketball or football), end up largely being manufactured by the wealthy by spending money and performing class. As commentator Naveen says, the main flaw in the SAT/ACT is that they run into big time ceiling effects, and can’t differentiate top 1% from top 0.1% or top 0.01%.

There is a very easy and obvious fix for that, which are academic credentials that do differentiate at that level. What, like it’s hard? Even if we can’t do better than this, and we have to do exactly zero innovation, we can give the students eyeing Ivy+ level colleges the GRE or the LSAT or the AIME. Check their AP scores. And so on.

Do Not Saturate Your Benchmarks

One main problem with SAT and ACT scores is that they max out. If you’re trying to get into Harvard, you have a 2400 or 36, but mostly so does everyone else. There is a simple solution to this, which is to extend the SAT and ACT so the top score is higher, and the toughest questions are a lot tougher, and no one ever gets a perfect score. This only impacts the very top directly, but it also means one can aspire to that elite and make it count, shifting incentives.

There are other standardized tests like AP exams, and in theory one could jump ahead and take a GRE test in 11th grade, but in practice these don’t do the job.

My actual belief matches Paul Graham, that this is all on purpose, as part of the War on Education. We don’t want to give smarter or more knowledgeable students a big advantage, so we make it hard to see.

The Name Game

Having a last name late in the alphabet will cost you 0.6 points on a 100 point scale when your work is graded in school in alphabetical order, which it often will be. This is one of many later-name-gets-punished mechanisms, and they add up, for both first and last names, more than people realize.

Fighting Grade Inflation with GAAP Accounting

Thus, Harvard decided things had gotten rather out of hand, and tried a grade cap.

Grade caps, such as saying Harvard classes can only give 20% As, can solve grade inflation per se, but they mean that students get penalized for taking harder classes.

Alex Tabarrok says this makes them not a good solution, but I would compare this to the situation as it is now. Don’t tougher classes usually give out less As not more? There are some exceptions, but lots of dirt easy courses are mostly As and by default very hard courses are relatively stingy. So forcing this to line up is helpful on the margin.

If you want to solve the problem of true course difficulty, as in controlling for student ability and effort, then you need to control for that explicitly.

There actually is an important modification.

Scott Kominers: Harvard aims to curb grade inflation by capping A grades in each course at (20% of enrollment) + 4 and evaluating honors using average percentile rank. I’ve studied grading through the lens of incentive design.

The verdict: Harvard’s proposal fails the game theory exam.

The modification is that a smaller class can let that +4 go a long way. If you have only five students, then you have zero problems.

My solution is simple, which is that we scrap the letter grades entirely, and only offer percentiles. Except, as per tradition, we call them something else: Strength assessments.

As in, we know everyone at Harvard is super strong, so we assign them all a strength score of 18 in all subjects. Then the lowest percentile is 18(01), so everyone knows it is still impressive, and the best are 18(99) and then 18(00), the top of the human range. That’s grade inflation done right, and then everyone knows to adjust for difficulty.

I am, of course, largely kidding with that.

But also there’s an easy way to not be kidding, which is to offer a clear statistic to compare performance between different classes. Sports fans have done many similar things, and know how to adjust for strength of schedule and even moves between minor and major leagues, so we all know this is not so hard.

Thus, I propose Grade Average Above Par (GAAP) Accounting.

Par performance is defined as how a marginal admitted student would perform, and we use standard sports-style statistical methods to compare high school and college grades to form predictions of that score for every class, based on who took the class and how those students perform elsewhere.

Then you can extend this across schools, since you can compare their matching high school transcripts and standardized test scores, to get a universal U-GAAP score, also known as Real GPA (RGPA), which can then be comparable across schools, and can predict how a Michigan student would have done at Harvard, or on the other end at Ohio State.

You can say that if employers look at GPA rather than U-GAAP, it won’t matter. But as a school you have a lot of control over this, because everyone is lazy, and you can report the GAAP as GPA and RGPA, and only at most report in an obscure location the old raw GPA as NGPA (nominal GPA), or perhaps not at all.

After all, if you can’t calculate your own NGPA, you don’t deserve to benefit from it.

Most College Students Fake Wokeness

What does not stop upon admission is the preferences kayfabe.

It is rather obvious that on college campuses there is a lot of preference falsification going on, and a lot of lying about beliefs, and a lot of lying about your beliefs to other people who actually share your beliefs but are also lying the same way you are.

I still had no idea things were this extreme.

Steve Stewart-Williams: 88% of students report pretending to hold more progressive views than they really do.

Forest Romm and Kevin Waldman: Between 2023 and 2025, we conducted 1,452 confidential interviews with undergraduates at Northwestern University and the University of Michigan. We were not studying politics — we were studying development. Our question was clinical, not political: “What happens to identity formation when belief is replaced by adherence to orthodoxy?”

We asked: Have you ever pretended to hold more progressive views than you truly endorse to succeed socially or academically? An astounding 88 percent said yes…

…

78 percent of students told us they self-censor on their beliefs surrounding gender identity; 72 percent on politics; 68 percent on family values. More than 80 percent said they had submitted classwork that misrepresented their views in order to align with professors. For many, this has become second nature — an instinct for academic and professional self-preservation.

To test the gap between expression and belief, we used gender discourse — a contentious topic both highly visible and ideologically loaded. In public, students echoed expected progressive narratives. In private, however, their views were more complex. Eighty-seven percent identified as exclusively heterosexual and supported a binary model of gender. Nine percent expressed partial openness to gender fluidity. Just seven percent embraced the idea of gender as a broad spectrum, and most of these belonged to activist circles.

Perhaps most telling: 77 percent said they disagreed with the idea that gender identity should override biological sex in such domains as sports, healthcare, or public data — but would never voice that disagreement aloud.

That is some Soviet-level, reign of terror level preference falsification. We have a question where a full 77% of students that:

  1. Believe [~X].

  2. But will if asked in public will refuse to say [~X].

  3. And will admit, to themselves and in a confidential survey, to both of these things.

That only leaves 23% total that either believe in [X], or are willing to say [~X].

That doesn’t mean a preference cascade is coming to campus any time soon. There is no reason this situation cannot be a stable equilibrium. It also seems likely to profoundly screw up the kids involved to be constantly lying in these ways.
