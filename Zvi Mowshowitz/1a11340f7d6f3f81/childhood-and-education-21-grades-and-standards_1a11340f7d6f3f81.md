Fighting Grade Inflation with GAAP AccountingThus, Harvard decided things had gotten rather out of hand, and tried a grade cap.
Alex Tabarrok says this makes them not a good solution, but I would compare this to the situation as it is now. Don’t tougher classes usually give out less As not more? There are some exceptions, but lots of dirt easy courses are mostly As and by default very hard courses are relatively stingy. So forcing this to line up is helpful on the margin. If you want to solve the problem of true course difficulty, as in controlling for student ability and effort, then you need to control for that explicitly. There actually is an important modification.
The modification is that a smaller class can let that +4 go a long way. If you have only five students, then you have zero problems. My solution is simple, which is that we scrap the letter grades entirely, and only offer percentiles. Except, as per tradition, we call them something else: Strength assessments. As in, we know everyone at Harvard is super strong, so we assign them all a strength score of 18 in all subjects. Then the lowest percentile is 18(01), so everyone knows it is still impressive, and the best are 18(99) and then 18(00), the top of the human range. That’s grade inflation done right, and then everyone knows to adjust for difficulty. I am, of course, largely kidding with that. But also there’s an easy way to not be kidding, which is to offer a clear statistic to compare performance between different classes. Sports fans have done many similar things, and know how to adjust for strength of schedule and even moves between minor and major leagues, so we all know this is not so hard. Thus, I propose Grade Average Above Par (GAAP) Accounting. Par performance is defined as how a marginal admitted student would perform, and we use standard sports-style statistical methods to compare high school and college grades to form predictions of that score for every class, based on who took the class and how those students perform elsewhere. Then you can extend this across schools, since you can compare their matching high school transcripts and standardized test scores, to get a universal U-GAAP score, also known as Real GPA (RGPA), which can then be comparable across schools, and can predict how a Michigan student would have done at Harvard, or on the other end at Ohio State. You can say that if employers look at GPA rather than U-GAAP, it won’t matter. But as a school you have a lot of control over this, because everyone is lazy, and you can report the GAAP as GPA and RGPA, and only at most report in an obscure location the old raw GPA as NGPA (nominal GPA), or perhaps not at all.
