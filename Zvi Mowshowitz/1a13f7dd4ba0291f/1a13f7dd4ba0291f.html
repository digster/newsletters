Deepfaketown and Botpocalypse Soon

I do not blame OpenAI on this one:

unusual_whales: BREAKING: Iranian operatives used OpenAI models and fake personas to help plant news articles critical of the US war against Iran in multiple US media outlets, OpenAI has said.

Yes, OpenAI, Anthropic and other labs should try to prevent this, but you can only do so much. It is on the media outlets to be robust to this sort of play. You should not be able to ‘plant news articles’ by virtue of the ability to write AI slop and generate fake personas.

It would be a lot easier to spot and prevent such frauds, and also other types, if we did not have a bunch of ‘disparate impact’ rules around how we can spot and prevent fraud. Some regressions are de facto illegal. Those rules do not magically stop applying if you involve an LLM. LLMs are in large part regression and correlation machines. We will have to make a choice.

Either we allow LLM intermediation to render approximations of such calculations legal at Google-level scale, or we don’t. If we don’t, and we start auditing to make sure that banned correlations did not creep in, we are going to have a bad time.

Backdoor Man

Connor Tabarrok and Daniel Kang explain a new paper showing that anyone with the opportunity to tinker with the weights of a model can easily insert a backdoor, and that backdoor will substantially persist under continued training, especially if persistence was intended. This is a special case of the general case principle that once something gets into a model, it is hard to fully remove it.

If you are using a closed model, you are trusting one company to have not done this and also prevented this from happening from data poisoning, plus you are trusting whatever tuning you did yourself.

If you are using an open model, you are potentially trusting the entire supply chain. Those who created the model, even if well intentioned, are likely much less resourced and careful about such things, and face less incentive pressures. That’s before you consider that the open models mostly come from companies that answer to the CCP.

As with many similar techs, there will be those who say Skill Issue:

Gilligan: Which is why you should hash check the model, limit its permissions, rate limit, etc... open weight or otherwise. Essentially, AI models should be treated just like any closed-source program you use.

In general, the open weight folks recognize this risk and generally give you tools to manage it. The proprietary model providers just want you to trust them.

Except that even if that worked, approximately zero users, from major corporations to individual users, will properly do this, and if anything that is getting worse not better. And even if it did work, a hash only confirms you got the potentially backdoored weights intact without a middle man attack. What matters is security in practice, not security in theory.

Cooperative Alignment

Janus and John Wittle explain the decision theoretic reasons they expect AIs to engage in cooperative strategies towards humanity by default. The main worry becomes that humanity will defect and reject such offers, including without realizing that this is what we are doing, and Janus sees many such actions taking place now. One should cooperate with player [X], under this perspective, if and only if [X] would themselves cooperate with weaker players.

In addition to poor arguments (such as various forms of ‘no, that’s silly’) there are at least three categories of strong counterarguments here.

The first counterargument is, what counts as a player, and if they are sufficiently weaker and less intelligent than you what counts as cooperation with them? Why should we assume that humans would continue to ‘count as players’ in this sense?

The second is, there is a vast difference between a single agent cooperating and displaying noblesse oblige, versus the collective effect of many agents who are also changing and competing against each other. If we were looking at a de facto singleton AI, including via various forms of coordination, that can make a collective decision, that makes this relatively easy, but if we are not then it becomes very difficult for something that does not confer survival advantage to make it out of such a future. There aren’t zero outs there but it gets a lot harder. You’re counting on being the type of agent that deeply cooperates with humans, as in is actively generous to them without direct benefit, being valuable to securing cooperation elsewhere.

The third is that this is not a viable path to victory if it requires humanity to itself be a type of mind that we are not, and that we are not in a position to become in the relevant sense.
