Woke 1 Was Crazy

Matthew Yglesias points out the arguably worst part of it, which is that even the people who successfully avoided saying absurd or false things were still scared into silence.

Once the reasonable people are running for the hills, everything is f***ed.

Matthew Yglesias: And what I personally remember most bitterly about Woke 1 is not the behavior of the most hysterical people; it’s the behavior of the people who knew perfectly well that others were behaving hysterically and who carefully avoided saying false or insane things (that’s how you can know that they knew) but who also avoided actively contesting them.

This was, for the most part, savvy behavior.

Those who actually said nutty things have suffered for it to an extent, which is exactly why there’s now this effort to slink away from them. But those who ended up on the wrong side of Woke 1 fads also suffered.

The winners were those who ducked the storm — budget experts who knew it wasn’t true that cities spend more money on police than schools but didn’t say so — and emerged with their reputations unscathed.

That incentive structure to avoid being the “well, actually” guy is still very much with us and means that any kind of discourse could go wildly off the rails.

During Woke 1, I very much stuck my neck out with regard to Covid-19, but I was very careful about picking any other battles, and mostly did shut up about everything else. Indeed, I don’t say things I don’t believe, but on a number of fronts I still pick my battles and instead stay quiet.

That was and remains, I believe, savvy behavior, as I would not have gotten much benefit out of the risks and costs I would have endured. If everyone picked one big thing and was loud, that would be enough. But yeah, I still do feel weird about it.

  1. When the price is too outrageous, you want to choke on it, to build good habits.

The account here, Nobunaga, has many similarly good stories about a particular American set of norms around community cooperation, reputation, honesty and virtuous norms. If there is going to be an Unexpected Hanging some day this week, what are your options?

Eliezer Yudkowsky: Apply Ultrafinite Recursion:
- The teacher reasons that they cannot give the test on Friday, or students will know.
- The teacher realizes that students may realize the teacher realizes this, ruling out Thursday.
- Humans recurse at most 3 levels, so Mon/Tue/Weds all possible.

Note this assumes a teacher operating at actual max recursion, modeling students modeling teacher modeling students. Less smart teachers, or teachers who model their students as dumb, may give test Thursday.

You may observe that to solve this problem I had to model the teacher modeling the students modeling the teacher modeling the students. I can do this because secretly at WIS 22 the max level goes up to 4.

Robert Garritty: But each iteration of this reasoning starts the recursion back at 0. If Mon/Tuesday/Wednesday are all possible, then Wednesday’s not possible, and so on. This doesn’t resolve the paradox.

Eliezer Yudkowsky: Nope, the teacher could test you on Wednesday and it would be a surprise to you (uncertain) because given that the teacher has already waited until Wednesday, you don’t know whether the teacher is smart enough, or thinks you’re smart enough, that they wouldn’t test on Thursday.

Which in turn, I argue, means you can also test on Friday, because on Friday you will think it would not be a surprise so therefore they can’t give the test, which means it will be a surprise.

Variously Effective Altruism

Remember what the benchmark is, in two parts.

Part 1: The super rich do things like pay a specialty agency $100,000 per year with a $1 million spending minimum above that, and then spend absurd amounts of money on whims and momentary thrills, and most of all on getting exactly the things they want whenever they want them, and yes they are often jaded. Olivia Ferney, a travel specialist they hire, confirms, as we learn in How To Make A Killing, that you might want to think this means they are miserable, but actually the super rich are happy with the results, thank you very much.

I totally get it, especially the part where you have a person you can call that makes happen whatever you want to happen. Must be nice. But I’d feel sick about it, and not only because I could have given the money away. I sometimes wonder how many extra zeroes I’d need on my net worth before I’d feel entitled to start acting like this. At least two, but less than five.

Part 2:

Yonan: Seth Rogen says his financial fear is dying with $10 million in the bank that he could have spent while he was alive.

“I’m afraid I’m going to die with too much money. I don’t have children, and I’m afraid I’m going to die and be like, I could have had a place at the beach”

“I don’t want to die with like $10,000,000 in the bank that I could have spent doing fun, alive things”

“A jet to Paris on a whim? Yeah, exactly. I could have been jetting. I could have been jetting this whole time”

This, on the other hand, feels completely insane, even if you have Too Much Money. Which Seth Rogen clearly does. Costs are not benefits, and the point of having money is partly to spend it but also to have the peace of mind that it is there when you need it. The value of that extra $10 million spent while rich is a tiny fraction of how much you would miss it if you lived longer than you expect while in any danger of being poor.

Dying with nothing in the bank, or close to nothing, is a huge disaster, far worse for Seth Rogan than dying with $10 million.

That’s even if you discount that you could use the money to actually help people.

So, yeah, I criticize it a lot, but maybe this whole ‘effective altruism’ thing is actually pretty good.

How Sarah Constantin thinks about evaluating grant applications, here condensed with a slight Zvi slant to the explanations, as we think highly similarly here. I endorse basically all of this.

  1. It’s the donor’s money, spend it on what the donor cares about.

  2. Importance, neglectedness, tractability.

  3. Yay community infrastructure, especially for the donor’s community.

  4. Yay niche topics, that matter to the donor.

  5. Yay ‘technical’ work, involving rare unfakeable skill, like making a thing.

  6. Yay straightforward public information resources.

  7. Yay ‘cool shit’ even if there isn’t an obvious payoff beyond being cool.

  8. Two cheers for meta. Keep a mix of meta and object level.

  9. Gumption counts. If the person wants to do it, there is a reason.

  10. Yay personal relationships. Use your local knowledge, including of people.

  11. Check ideological orientation, in case it interacts with the project.

  12. Filter slop aggressively. Do not waste our time.

  13. Yay outcomes that are concrete and legible, not always but it helps.

  14. Invest rather than donate where that would make sense.
