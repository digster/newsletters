Previously in series: Table of ContentsYou Still Got ItI used to be like this more often, constantly doubting first if I had it at all, then later if I still got it, then finally if I’ll blow it. Hitting post used to be absolutely terrifying, every time. It’s no longer routine for me to freak out but I’m not zero percent like this now:
What about me? I worry that I’ve had or used all my good ideas, that I’ll be repeating myself. I worry that I won’t recapture the magic that goes into a really great post. I worry that I missed the key windows of time to say the important things. I worry I’m getting too old and my brain is slowly not working right anymore. I worry I’m going to start making big mistakes and look like an idiot. My real anxiety these days comes not from ‘I don’t got it’ but from ‘I could blow it.’ It stems from the opportunity, from the stakes of the games I’m trying to play. Whereas I guess if I ‘don’t have it’ then there’d really be nothing to sweat, you know? The worries mostly apply to me for my ‘effort posts,’ where I’m trying to assemble a clear thesis and create something that will last. For roundups and weeklies, I don’t much worry about it. Those are more like ‘clocking in.’ I notice that most of the time, when I do really have something, I stop worrying. I draw the parallel to being a competitor in Magic: The Gathering. I would worry I didn’t have it. When I was really clocked in I didn’t worry about not having it, I worried more about blowing the chance when I did have it. So it’s a similar pattern. Is the anxiety actually useful? Does it drive you forward? To some extent yes. In order to write well you have to reinvent, to do something you haven’t done before, and you have to struggle and push through bad ideas and false starts and so on, and be intolerant of things that aren’t good enough or could be better. But I don’t think it’s necessary, at least not for everyone. You just need something pushing you forward, some form of dread about not putting in every last bit of effort. You can be driven forward by opportunity or risk, rather than anxiety about ability, and I’m sure there are other similar tricks. Elmore Leonard is all about what stupid things not to do: Never open with weather, avoid prologues, always use “said” for dialogue without adverbs, almost no exclamation points, no ‘suddenly’ or ‘all hell broke loose,’ minimal regional dialect, no detailed character descriptions, avoid long place and thing details, leave out things readers skip. If it sounds like writing, rewrite it. Diana Athill says read it aloud to yourself, have no inessential words, be highly suspicious of your darlings. Margaret Atwood focuses on physical tools like pencils and a thesaurus that I would say you don’t need anymore. She also steps back to earlier points if something went awry, does back exercises and hold the reader’s attention and has others look with fresh eyes. There’s a bunch more. I largely think on the page, as I write. Which means I often start writing before I know what I want to say. It also means I would never write a passage in order to have an early version, or move on from a paragraph that I know is not good enough. If it is not good enough, I am not done thinking. I am not ready to move on. I also think that part of why my process works so quickly is that I don’t train myself that it is okay to write these ‘vomit drafts.’ If you’re willing to do that, you’re committing to rewrites. Then again, I never write fiction. If I wrote fiction, I could totally see doing what famed Simpsons writer Swartzwelder says to do, writing filler dialogue and jokes to have a first draft, so you know what you are looking at, and then fill in from there. The key difference I have is, for my purposes writing nonfiction, I find that trying to revise bad writing is way harder than writing well in the first place. If I realize something is bad I’m inclined to entirely start over. In fiction perhaps it does not work that way? I’m not sure. None of this gets you out of editing. I have a lot less subscribers, largely because I don’t maximize, because I recognize that the impact is on a power law, by bringing key things to the key people who will act on that. I do worry about the ‘silo effect,’ where you do better if you stay in your lane. I want to write about games more, or other fun things, but that gets punished. I pay some price for the tangents I do insist upon. I find the silo problem even worse on Twitter or other social media than Substack. If you’re giving me 50% Tweets I want and 50% Tweets I don’t want, then that’s no good, because the cost of evaluating a Tweet is high relative to the cost of full consumption. Whereas if you give me 50% articles or books I want, and 50% that I don’t, that’s great. You can totally spam my inbox with the other half, because I spend one minute discarding the bad things and 1 hour consuming the good thing. It’s only a problem if the bad things fool you into investing a lot of time. My take for posts that have a thesis, rather than being roundups: You should only ever split an article into two small parts in places where the parts can stand on their own. If no one would ever want to link to or read Part 1 without Part 2, or Part 2 without Part 1, then you want to post it all at once, no matter the length. That assumes you are on the internet, so you face no physical limitations. Except the Gmail clipping at ~102KB, but that ship sailed for me a while ago. The reason The Sequences work so well is that while they form one large construction, you can link to or read each individual post on its own. If you have a full book of content, then you have to split it into chapters, even if the chapters don’t stand on their own, but I would not consider that below 10k words, and would only seriously consider it around 20k, even if your normal length is 2k. For roundups, I think it’s fine to split sooner, especially if that gives the sections better common themes. My new goal is non-AI roundups topping out around 5k.
