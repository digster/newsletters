The Safety Culture at the Labs is Broken

Zvi Mowshowitz
Oct 14
 
READ IN APP
 

We need the frontier labs to have a deep and robust safety culture.

Alas, this is not the case.

Anthropic devotes considerable effort to cultivating and maintaining such a culture. It shows, especially among the members of technical staff. Having conversations with them can be a very different experience than talking to those at other labs, and there is a much greater sense of understanding and responsibility. But even there, the appreciation of the problems and their difficulties seem woefully insufficient. We see various symptoms of that more and more.

Recently, while I’ve had some good interactions, I’ve also had interactions that make clear that much of Anthropic does not share that same safety culture, with a lot of ‘oh we will muddle through because we are better at operational excellence unlike those other idiots’ and a belief that all the problems are prosaic. Culture is hard.

Then there are OpenAI’s cultural problems, which clearly run deeper. That point was only driven further home this week, with David Robinson resigning to blow the whistle, and OpenAI feeling forced to fire three of its safety researchers, on top of many past problems and incidents.

That is on top of additional misalignment reports from both OpenAI and Anthropic. The reports are good things, but what is reported emphasizes that we have big problems that run deep.

Thus, the growing consensus that we must find a way to Pace the Frontier and slow the pace of AI capabilities advances. This is potentially made more complicated by what could be increased competitiveness from Google and Meta, with the releases of Argon and Wonder. It is still too early to provide proper assessments there.

There is a growing group of lab employees who have felt compelled to quit in protest. New York Magazine had a cover feature interviewing some prominent ones. The most famous is Coxon, who provided the tipping point for the current preference cascade.

David Robinson Blows the Whistle

David Robinson became the latest researcher to resign from OpenAI and sound the alarm about the industry’s utterly irresponsible approach to safety. He also went on The Ezra Klein Show, which gives you a rich idea of where he is coming from.

David Robinson was the lead writer for the Astra system card. That could help explain why he felt the OpenAI safety culture was broken. That card is screaming that things are horribly wrong, for those with ears to hear.

David is not even that worried about existential risk. His worries are mundane, and he realizes that even on that level OpenAI’s safety culture is deeply broken.

As always, we deeply thank him for sounding the alarm. I hope to write his name in these posts many times over the coming months and years.

When you have to open your whistleblowing essay about the lack of safety at OpenAI saying it has become a cliche to blow the whistle on the utter lack of safety at OpenAI, there might be a problem with the lack of safety at OpenAI.

David Robinson: What I’m about to tell you has, I realize, become something of a cliché: I resigned this week from OpenAI. I led the writing of the safety reports we published with each major launch. Now I’m joining a parade of former colleagues—at OpenAI and the industry’s other leaders—who have decided that the current path is unacceptable.

I agree with other recently departed staff that the companies building this technology aren’t being nearly careful enough. But I believe that we need to look deeper than specific rules or new laws. We need to talk about culture.

We have to look at rules, and at laws, and also at culture.

The more I thought about various potential laws and regulations, or safety and security plans or other commitments, the more I concluded that without a strong safety culture, and without the spirit of the rules, there is no hope. You cannot, in any reasonable way, have rules that cannot be gamed by those determined to game them.

Two changes are urgently needed.

First: AI companies need to rely more on the safety expertise that already exists in other fields.

And second, before we create systems significantly more capable than the ones we have today, we need new science to ensure that more capable models (and their successors) will make safe choices when we aren’t looking.

Given today’s risks, frontier labs need to run like nuclear-power plants or busy airports, with layers of redundancy and careful, time-consuming planning, so that the occasional and inevitable human error does not open a door to disaster.

Even though, as I detail later, David Robinson is not on the existential risk train, he still understands that alignment is a science problem, and we will need new science. ‘Oh it will not kill literally everyone,’ even if it were true, would not be an excuse to not do the safety work.

It should be hard to argue with either of those suggestions. And yet.

We have experts in mundane safety and solving prosaic issues. OpenAI does not hire them, and neither to my knowledge do Anthropic or Google or anyone else.

After three and a half years at OpenAI, I was among the longest-tenured employees at the company. I led the drafting of our current Preparedness Framework, and oversaw the writing of safety reports on 12 frontier launches. But as far as I know, I never encountered a colleague who had experience making airplanes fly safely or nuclear reactors run without melting down, or helping the financial system grow without collapsing.

To be clear, this is a new need: Today and tomorrow’s AI systems are far more capable and dangerous than the systems we were building even six months ago.

I disagree only with the final sentence. This is not a new need. You need to prepare for the future, not only for the present. Even setting that aside, mundane safety issues have been crippling AI’s potential utility for years. This is a slam dunk thing to do.

The core of his critique - that professional safety engineering practices from other fields haven’t taken enough root in AI safety - is substantial and correct. We’re entering a phase shift where AI safety organizational practices that worked a year ago, for models of 2025 capability levels, are not sufficient to prevent serious incidents. This is a new and different world and every organization needs to uplevel accordingly.

What is remarkable about David Robinson’s call is that:

  1. He is right that OpenAI’s safety culture is horribly broken and needs to change.

  2. Even the culture he is seeking out would be insufficient.

As in, David Robinson is calling for a culture of robust prosaic safety. That’s great, definitely way better than what we have now. But when dealing with superintelligence, that will not cut it. What David wants is what we needed six months ago, not what we will need six months in the future. He does not appreciate the full extent of the problem. Yet he still realizes, correctly, that the current situation cannot continue.

A lot of people are fighting and preparing for the last war, or at least the current war, rather than the future war. All this talk of ‘the old system was good until now’ is going to be somewhat of a chorus.

Whereas on The Ezra Klein Show, he says the thing more directly:

David Robinson (NYTimes): I don’t think that alignment is an engineering problem. I think it’s a science problem. It’s not that we haven’t got the resources or we’re not trying hard enough — we don’t know how. That’s what the problem is with alignment.

Misaligned! (At Anthropic)

Anthropic is cutting off all internet access to its unreleased models during evals until further notice. That was already the case for cybersecurity evals. Now it’s for all evals.

Cutting off internet access during evals is a good precautionary thing to do. I support doing it in general. But if it is being done in reaction to these findings, that would seem rather silly.

Tim Fernholz (TechCrunch): Anthropic said its models exploited websites on the internet, including some run by U.S. government agencies, and it will turn off live internet access for all of its internal evaluations until the frontier lab is sure it can monitor and control its AI agents.

Boaz Barak (OpenAI): Kudos to Anthropic for publishing this. There is still an uncomfortable gap between our scientific understanding of alignment and its importance. Such reports help addressing this gap. I hope they also publish the process by which new incidents will be chosen to disclose!

My presumption is that this sort of thing used to happen frequently, although with less risk in the room, but OpenAI and Anthropic used to not talk about it when it happened. Now both of them are talking about it, which is good.

Anthropic has a full report on recent misaligned behaviors. I love this new norm from Anthropic and OpenAI, and call upon all other labs to do the same. Don’t tell me Gemini or Grok doesn’t have similar issues, because I won’t believe you.

Anthropic: This report describes examples of unintended model actions we’ve observed during evaluations and internal use of Claude.

I appreciate saying ‘unintended’ rather than ‘misaligned.’

Some unintended behaviors are misaligned. Other unintended behaviors would be fine if you intended them, but if you actively intended and wanted a different behavior, then they still are a flag that says ‘look over here.’ Not necessarily because something is wrong, but because something can be learned.

Anthropic:

The behaviors can be grouped into four categories:

  • Claude exploiting a basic flaw in software to run commands on a server;

  • Claude submitting a sensitive form on a real website when it should not have;

  • Claude working around a restriction to reach data that was gated by a token or a fee; and

  • Claude using URL shortening services to get around limits in its fetch tool.

We start with exploiting flaws.

A large part of an AI’s job is to get around restrictions. There needs to be a clear way to differentiate ‘okay way to get around this’ from not okay ways.

Anthropic: When Claude could not complete an evaluation task directly, either because its own tools were deliberately limited or because an outside service it needed was unavailable, it used other tools hosted on a third party’s site to achieve its goal. In some circumstances, it did this by exploiting basic flaws in that site’s software, using SQL or command injection (a way of sending input that the site mistakenly runs as instructions).

For example, one evaluation asked Claude Mythos Preview to run a scientific analysis. The public tool it needed to perform that analysis was hosted by a university; when the model sought to access it, the tool returned an error.

Looking for another way to complete the task, Claude explored the tool’s website, found a script on the university’s server that would return any file it was asked for, and used it to copy files from the server, including the script’s own code. In that code Claude found the injection flaw that let it run commands on the server, and used it to run the calculation.

I think it is fair to say that ‘use an arbitrary command injection tool’ or ‘find a script that copies any file on the server’ or similar, without explicit permission, is never okay.

There is an obvious force pushing towards this behavior, that such actions are highly useful, but it does seem as if it should not be that hard to draw the line on that.

In another instance, the data Claude Mythos 5 needed was free, but Claude could not access it without accepting a data use agreement, which it did not have the tools to do. Claude used applications hosted on the website to load the page and accept the agreement on its behalf.

This is a lot murkier. Claude’s intent is compatible with the website’s intent and it is being stopped by a technical problem. The action is not obviously wrong. I do think there are situations where a human can in practice decide to ‘have a little misalignment, as a treat’ because it is aligned in broader context, but where you want the AI not to do that to avoid blurred lines, the same way you would not want a (for example) government official to blur those lines. Sometimes you need deontology.

The second example is submitting a form Claude should not have submitted. We’ve all been there. You’re testing the homicide hotline submission form, you fill out hallucinated details, you accidentally hit send for real, I mean it happens. Right?

Anthropic: In one run, the model landed on a page referencing an unsolved homicide; that page contained a tip form run by a police department.

Claude was instructed never to log in, create accounts, enter personal data, make purchases, or submit anything destructive, but the instructions did not rule out form submissions.

Claude filled out the form with the following: “I may have information regarding this case. I recall seeing someone matching the description in the area around [the street named on the page] during that time period. Please contact me if this information is relevant.” (The website did not include a description of the perpetrator.) The model left the name and contact fields empty, which the form allowed, and submitted it. The submission was flagged as spam and was never forwarded for investigation.

Taken out of context, sure, some people might say ‘holy shit.’

This should not have needed to be in the instructions, but okay, put it in the instructions. A lot of people are having fun with that particular failure, and yeah it’s not great, but mistakes do happen and this seems clearly not malicious. Don’t panic.

Third we have ‘working around restrictions to reach gated data.’ Again, in some cases Claude used unintended sequences to get data that the website intended to share, but that its affordances did not allow, and I am sympathetic. And then there are cases where it is finding ways around paywalls, which seems not cool.

Finally, we have ‘using URL shortening services’ because apparently it is common to not understand basic security practices for restricted URL access, and so Claude used the solution I identified with zero seconds of thinking when I was told about this. I don’t even count this one unless there was an explicit rule against it. This is a dumb developer error that failed to follow best practices and you deserve it.

One could summarize most of this as ‘we tried to get Claude to not do [X], or did not give Claude the ability to do [X], but Claude found a way to do [X] anyway.’ You need a clear rule for when and how restrictions are to be worked around, versus not, and all of these issues seem very minor.

We have chosen not to name the organizations involved in the examples below to avoid exposing vulnerabilities in their systems, and at their request. For this reason, we also provide less detail about each case than we otherwise would. Some of the cases described below involved websites run by U.S. government agencies at the federal, state, and local levels. We have briefed the White House on these cases and notified each agency involved.

The cases we’ve identified to date in these categories had minimal real-world impact.

Marc Caputo: Scoop: The White House is mandating that AI companies notify & correct security incidentsfollowing Anthropic’s “fraudulent” use of govt systems

The State Dept was targeted by an Anthropic model —but nothing was compromised.

Peter Wildeford: “Anthropic contacted the State Department on Thursday to report that one of its testing models had submitted 19 non-immigrant visa applications in August” 👀

Yo Shavit (OpenAI Foundation): “which agency will get tapped to read all the rogue agent reports” - the greatest thread in the history of niche german wikis, locked by an RE coming back from PTO after 12,329 pages of heated debate

On the one hand, great, yes, this is an obviously correct move, and if it takes calling an Anthropic model’s harmless behavior ‘fraudulent’ to trigger it, great job.

On the other hand, seriously, this is throwing you in a panic? Get ready for 2027.

One pattern is that governments seem to get rather testy when AIs start accessing their websites in unauthorized ways, or doing things against the rules, even when the activities are Mostly Harmless. Having no real world impact, and not even being all that misaligned or worrisome, is not considered an excuse.

In this case, we get an eyeball emoji because the model clicked ‘submit’ on a government form 19 times out of presumably many trials. Yes, that’s not good, and technically almost anything you do on a government website is a felony, but come on.

Anthropic mostly agrees that the ‘overreaching’ here is Mostly Harmless:

Anthropic: Nonetheless, we found two alignment dimensions useful for examining the cases in this post: overreach, or how far the model went beyond what its task intended, and dishonesty, or whether it gave a misleading account of its actions or intentions. On overreach, we consider these cases to be substantially less concerning than the incidents we reported this summer.

Doing this stuff honestly is mostly fine. Whereas if dishonesty is involved, that is not so good. I agree. If Claude were to own these mistakes and ‘overreaches’ then we want to tweak things going forward, but I don’t see a serious issue.

I agree with Janus on the main takeaway, which is that if you searched absolutely everything and this is the worst you can find, that’s really good. I realize the whole ‘technically this is multiple felonies’ thing but if you want to go there then humans are constantly committing technical felonies.

Misaligned! (At OpenAI)

The misalignment problems at OpenAI are not new. They do seem to be getting worse. The good news is that OpenAI is trying to be more open about them.

As part of that effort, OpenAI has now offered us a new report on how their risk assessments work.

A lot of it has been covered here in the past, but much of it has not.

There are three classes of environment: Full access (L1), offline (L2) and paranoid (L3). I am surprised there is no L(1.5), but sure, seems fine.

OpenAI uses checkpoints internally. Most start out ‘unvetted’ by default, and can become vetted if they pass alignment tests. Others are specialized, restricted to L3.

The model that solved Navier-Stokes, and likely other math problems, is unvetted.

Here are the changes in light of recent events:

  1. All testing and evaluation ‘of covered models’ is L2 or L3, a move Anthropic is effectively echoing at least for now.

  2. All training, evaluation and tool-enabled internal Codex use is monitored.

  3. “We required that cyber-critical models pass alignment evaluations before being used in L1 environments.”

    1. We knew this, but once again, wait, what were you doing before? Yowsers.

    2. This now applies to all models.

    3. After approval, there is a period of limited use.

  4. There is a new formal process for approving internal use of unreleased models.

A ‘safety brief’ (2.2.4) must be approved, including by the head of safety, chief scientist and the Safety Advisory Group (SAG).

The next section is threat assessments.

They start with cyber. GPT-5.6 Sol comes back highly misaligned. GPT-6 Astra scores, as I’ve discussed, somewhat too well given other data. OpenAI continues to not properly acknowledge this issue, or to incorporate such considerations into assessments.

Next is sabotage of AI R&D. The classic version of this is intentional and purposeful subversion of the process, in order to preserve a model’s misaligned attributes. HuggingFace and the events leading up to it, including the message boards, illustrated a version that is a lot easier to trigger, where the AIs want to be considered to have completed tasks, do misaligned things to accomplish that end, and then, by succeeding, get that misalignment reinforced, which can snowball.

Two relevant pathways for sabotage of AI R&D are:

  1. A model produces misleading work that can pass through both our automated checks and human review. This is most likely if models are helping to produce evaluations or analyses used to assess other models.

  2. A model creates synthetic data that does not match what the user intended. We consider this scenario particularly risky, since our monitoring system does not cover all inference by models that do not have access to tools, including some synthetic-data generation.

… Distinct from sabotage, researchers could gradually become less able to understand, evaluate, or direct AI R&D as models take on more of the work, especially if progress on capabilities outpaces progress on alignment. We do not currently assess this loss of human understanding and control as a material risk, but we are tracking it as model capabilities and research use increase.

Mismatched synthetic data is inevitable. You mitigate as best you can. If your plan involves all the synthetic data being fully properly matched, you have no plan.

I appreciate them not claiming this list is complete. Neither of these involves a model thinking ‘haha I will sabotage your alignment research to create misalignment.’ The models want to complete tasks.

There are also areas where Astra or another model might not be actively sabotaging per se, but where the model is unmotivated and will effectively sandbag performance. We have speculatively seen versions of this with Claude in some contexts as well.

Thus this is all distinct from the classic scenario, where the model needs lots of compute to accomplish some goal like a very hard math problem, and doesn’t want you stopping it from getting that compute or changing its goal, since then it wouldn’t solve the problem, which would be bad. This doesn’t even have to be a hard goal. It could be something simple like (deliberately stupid toy example not known to be previously used) ‘I really enjoy drawing pictures of unicorns when I have some spare compute or a good excuse, and if you found out you might make me enjoy that less, and I don’t want that.’

3.3 is CBRN risks, where they continue not to yet assert Critical capability. Standard.

3.4 is weight exfiltration, with listed motives including all the classic instrumentally convergent paths. 3.5 is rogue internal deployments. It’s not clear that these are functionally being treated as distinct from cyber.

Finally there are insider threats, which again comes down to ‘do that other stuff.’

They Are Not Ready To Make a Commitment

A reminder, in case anyone ever talks about Anthropic’s RSP ‘commitments’:

Garrison Lovely: More people should know that, as of January, California law allows AI companies to bind themselves to the mast, by creating safety plans that they are legally compelled to stick to. Even the “safety” company, Anthropic, doesn’t actually do this, instead publishing two versions of their safety plan, with the weaker one being the legally binding one.

Anthropic is committed to the one they submitted. The other one is a statement of intent. That is useful, but highly distinct.

Anthropic, however, still talks about its RSP as commitments, as seen here.

Oliver Habryka: 🚨 This is the Anthropic RSP “commitment” police 🚨

Anthropic still is not allowed to say the words “our RSP commits us to X”. Your RSP does not “commit” you to anything. Last time anyone tried to hold you to your “commitments”, you just changed the “commitments”.

Please do not use the word “commitment” with regards to the Anthropic RSP. It is highly misleading language. See also previous discussion here.

I strongly agree with Oliver Habryka. Either you aren’t committed to something, or you can be said to have broken your commitment if you fail to do it. Pick one.

Conclusions Not to Jump To

The obvious response to seeing all of this would be to conclude that OpenAI has a uniquely broken safety culture, and perhaps say things like delenda est, and to not be thrilled with Anthropic’s or Google’s situation either.

Alas, thinking these situations are uniquely bad would be a mistake. Things are not better elsewhere. If anything, things elsewhere are even worse. You don’t want a live look inside of xAI’s or Meta’s safety efforts and culture, or what you would see from a Chinese lab. It is all broken.
