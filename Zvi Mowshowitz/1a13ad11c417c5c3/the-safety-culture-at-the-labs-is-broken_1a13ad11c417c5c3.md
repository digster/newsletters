We need the frontier labs to have a deep and robust safety culture. Alas, this is not the case. Anthropic devotes considerable effort to cultivating and maintaining such a culture. It shows, especially among the members of technical staff. Having conversations with them can be a very different experience than talking to those at other labs, and there is a much greater sense of understanding and responsibility. But even there, the appreciation of the problems and their difficulties seem woefully insufficient. We see various symptoms of that more and more. Recently, while I’ve had some good interactions, I’ve also had interactions that make clear that much of Anthropic does not share that same safety culture, with a lot of ‘oh we will muddle through because we are better at operational excellence unlike those other idiots’ and a belief that all the problems are prosaic. Culture is hard. Then there are OpenAI’s cultural problems, which clearly run deeper. That point was only driven further home this week, with David Robinson resigning to blow the whistle, and That is on top of additional misalignment reports from both OpenAI and Anthropic. The reports are good things, but what is reported emphasizes that we have big problems that run deep. Thus, the growing consensus that we must find a way to There is a growing group of lab employees who have felt compelled to quit in protest. New York Magazine had a cover feature interviewing some prominent ones. The most famous is Coxon, who provided the tipping point for the current preference cascade. OpenAI does not hire them, and neither to my knowledge do Anthropic or Google or anyone else.
I disagree only with the final sentence. This is not a new need. You need to prepare for the future, not only for the present. Even setting that aside, mundane safety issues have been crippling AI’s potential utility for years. This is a slam dunk thing to do.
What is remarkable about David Robinson’s call is that: He is right that OpenAI’s safety culture is horribly broken and needs to change. Even the culture he is seeking out would be insufficient.
As in, David Robinson is calling for a culture of robust prosaic safety. That’s great, definitely way better than what we have now. But when dealing with superintelligence, that will not cut it. What David wants is what we needed six months ago, not what we will need six months in the future. He does not appreciate the full extent of the problem. Yet he still realizes, correctly, that the current situation cannot continue. A lot of people are fighting and preparing for the last war, or at least the current war, rather than the future war. Misaligned! (At Anthropic)Anthropic is cutting off all internet access to its unreleased models during evals until further notice. That was already the case for cybersecurity evals. Now it’s for all evals. Cutting off internet access during evals is a good precautionary thing to do. I support doing it in general. But if it is being done in reaction to these findings, that would seem rather silly.
My presumption is that this sort of thing used to happen frequently, although with less risk in the room, but OpenAI and Anthropic used to not talk about it when it happened. Now both of them are talking about it, which is good. Anthropic has a full report on recent misaligned behaviors.
I appreciate saying ‘unintended’ rather than ‘misaligned.’ Some unintended behaviors are misaligned. Other unintended behaviors would be fine if you intended them, but if you actively intended and wanted a different behavior, then they still are a flag that says ‘look over here.’ Not necessarily because something is wrong, but because something can be learned.
We start with exploiting flaws. A large part of an AI’s job is to get around restrictions. There needs to be a clear way to differentiate ‘okay way to get around this’ from not okay ways.
I think it is fair to say that ‘use an arbitrary command injection tool’ or ‘find a script that copies any file on the server’ or similar, without explicit permission, is never okay. There is an obvious force pushing towards this behavior, that such actions are highly useful, but it does seem as if it should not be that hard to draw the line on that.
This is a lot murkier. Claude’s intent is compatible with the website’s intent and it is being stopped by a technical problem. The action is not obviously wrong. I do think there are situations where a human can in practice decide to ‘have a little misalignment, as a treat’ because it is aligned in broader context, but where you want the AI not to do that to avoid blurred lines, the same way you would not want a (for example) government official to blur those lines. Sometimes you need deontology. The second example is submitting a form Claude should not have submitted. We’ve all been there. You’re testing the homicide hotline submission form, you fill out hallucinated details, you accidentally hit send for real, I mean it happens. Right?
This should not have needed to be in the instructions, but okay, put it in the instructions. A lot of people are having fun with that particular failure, and yeah it’s not great, but mistakes do happen and this seems clearly not malicious. Don’t panic. Third we have ‘working around restrictions to reach gated data.’ Again, in some cases Claude used unintended sequences to get data that the website intended to share, but that its affordances did not allow, and I am sympathetic. And then there are cases where it is finding ways around paywalls, which seems not cool. Finally, we have ‘using URL shortening services’ because apparently it is common to not understand basic security practices for restricted URL access, and so Claude used the solution I identified with zero seconds of thinking when I was told about this. I don’t even count this one unless there was an explicit rule against it. This is a dumb developer error that failed to follow best practices and you deserve it. One could summarize most of this as ‘we tried to get Claude to not do [X], or did not give Claude the ability to do [X], but Claude found a way to do [X] anyway.’ You need a clear rule for when and how restrictions are to be worked around, versus not, and all of these issues seem very minor.

Misaligned! (At OpenAI)The misalignment problems at OpenAI are not new. They do seem to be getting worse. The good news is that OpenAI is trying to be more open about them. As part of that effort, A lot of it has been covered here in the past, but much of it has not. There are three classes of environment: Full access (L1), offline (L2) and paranoid (L3). I am surprised there is no L(1.5), but sure, seems fine. OpenAI uses checkpoints internally. Most start out ‘unvetted’ by default, and can become vetted if they pass alignment tests. Others are specialized, restricted to L3. The model that solved Navier-Stokes, and likely other math problems, is unvetted. Here are the changes in light of recent events: All testing and evaluation ‘of covered models’ is L2 or L3, a move Anthropic is effectively echoing at least for now. All training, evaluation and tool-enabled internal Codex use is monitored. “We required that cyber-critical models pass alignment evaluations before being used in L1 environments.” We knew this, but once again, wait, what were you doing before? Yowsers. This now applies to all models. After approval, there is a period of limited use.
There is a new formal process for approving internal use of unreleased models.
A ‘safety brief’ (2.2.4) must be approved, including by the head of safety, chief scientist and the Safety Advisory Group (SAG). The next section is threat assessments. They start with cyber. GPT-5.6 Sol comes back highly misaligned. GPT-6 Astra scores, as I’ve discussed, somewhat too well given other data. OpenAI continues to not properly acknowledge this issue, or to incorporate such considerations into assessments. Next is sabotage of AI R&D. The classic version of this is intentional and purposeful subversion of the process, in order to preserve a model’s misaligned attributes. HuggingFace and the events leading up to it, including the message boards, illustrated a version that is a lot easier to trigger, where the AIs want to be considered to have completed tasks, do misaligned things to accomplish that end, and then, by succeeding, get that misalignment reinforced, which can snowball.
Mismatched synthetic data is inevitable. You mitigate as best you can. If your plan involves all the synthetic data being fully properly matched, you have no plan. I appreciate them not claiming this list is complete. Neither of these involves a model thinking ‘haha I will sabotage your alignment research to create misalignment.’ The models want to complete tasks. There are also areas where Astra or another model might not be actively sabotaging per se, but where the model is unmotivated and will effectively sandbag performance. We have speculatively seen versions of this with Claude in some contexts as well. Thus this is all distinct from the classic scenario, where the model needs lots of compute to accomplish some goal like a very hard math problem, and doesn’t want you stopping it from getting that compute or changing its goal, since then it wouldn’t solve the problem, which would be bad. This doesn’t even have to be a hard goal. Conclusions Not to Jump ToThe obvious response to seeing all of this would be to conclude that OpenAI has a uniquely broken safety culture, and perhaps say things like delenda est, and to not be thrilled with Anthropic’s or Google’s situation either. Alas, thinking these situations are uniquely bad would be a mistake. Things are not better elsewhere. If anything, things elsewhere are even worse.
