Modesty arguments often say that you should mostly or entirely bow to ‘expert consensus’ or the views of particular others, and who are you to disagree. It has been a few years since I’ve properly addressed this so: My answer is that you are you. Other people are saying things for a wide variety of reasons, many of which are not about them paying attention and focusing on seeking this particular truth. Those people make mistakes all the time, and often have other motives and influences at work, especially social pressures and information cascades. Them being as smart as you, or smarter than you, does not exempt them from this, and them being higher status or credentialed or cooler definitely does not exempt them. A smart informed person sincerely thinking [X] can easily cease to be evidence for [X], once you have thought sufficiently about both [X] and why that person thinks [X]. Or, as I once put it: This post covers Eliezer Yudkowsky making a narrower claim than mine, about not conflating status with smarts and epistemic authority, and my documenting related discussion that happened on Twitter. This concerns a particular historical interaction, but what matters is the general point. I then briefly extend this to my broader claims. I affirm Modesty’s Motte. I am heavily against Modesty’s Bailey. Epistemic PeerageNote the bolded passage (bold is mine) in the exchange below:
It doesn’t mean you should mostly or fully defer to such a person. That would imply they are an ‘epistemic superior,’ someone whose opinion carries greater weight than your own, or even an ‘expert’ or ‘guru’ that you should defer to entirely except under special circumstances. Epistemic peer means that, if you both have access to the same information, and they have not yet explained to you their reasoning, you should not presume one of you is better at this than the other, so you should probably take the two unadjusted opinions and split the difference. Once they explain their reasoning, you should instead evaluate their reasoning. Sometimes they will have better reasoning than you, sometimes it will be worse. Remember the law of Conservation of Expected Evidence. If you could move towards their belief but not away from it, you should adjust your beliefs now. ‘Blows up a hedge fund’ goes too far in describing what happened to Leopold’s hedge fund, Situational Awareness. It declined from $45 billion in assets to $10 billion, after it used too much leverage and got caught in margin calls, forcing a sale of its public book, but it did not per se blow up. Leopold’s entire approach to his hedge fund did represent an extremely not-modest view of the world, to not only bet on his own ‘situational awareness’ and analysis of the market, but to do so at high leverage and for amounts where he was likely impacting prices, and thus vulnerable to additional pressures. Wrong, Stupid and Low Status Are Three Distinct ThingsMost people only partially make the relevant mistake. Each correlates with the others, but mostly people know they are distinct. It is most common to equate calling someone wrong and calling someone stupid. Only a stupid person would be so wrong. You would only dare think they were wrong if you thought they were stupid. You would only dare call them wrong if you thought they were stupid. You know that calling them wrong will be heard as calling them stupid.
Whereas, of course, smart people are wrong about things all the time, and stupid people are right about things all the time. Sometimes the smart person is wrong, and the stupid person is right, or the smart group is wrong and the stupid group is right, either because or in spite of superior information. The other links are usually seen as more tenuous. Of course people constantly use accusations of stupidity or wrongness to try and lower someone’s status, or smartness and rightness to raise it. Most people in most places still push back hard against attempts to too systematically equate smart or right with high status, or especially stupid and wrong with low status. We realize they are not the same, and also the monkey brain knows that there be dragons. The danger comes in places that do systematically equate intelligence and rightness with status. Academia is the most famous version of this. LessWrong-Yudkowsky style rationality has this as an obvious failure mode. You have to constantly push back against where the high status person says ‘think for yourself’ and everyone says ‘yes, as the guru says we will think for ourselves exactly as he told us to.’ There is no amount of hammering ‘no I am not a guru, at most I am an epistemic superior, I really am saying to think for yourselves’ or ‘we are not a cult’ that will consistently fully stick. In my experience rationalist circles manage, after much effort, to do a good overall job at this, but not a great one. Effective Altruism is set up to have a maximally bad version of this mistake. There is tons of pressure to quantify all the things, and to maximize quantified results, and to bow to the legible, and a lot of demand to be told how to figure out how to think and do in order to do the most good, and thus be good, and so on. Results are often prioritized over epistemics. This all puts extra value on consensus, and various forms of high status often end up equated with correctness or intelligence or both. (As usual, to avoid related implications, my caveat that while EA has lots of issues, we criticize them so much exactly because they are worth taking the effort to criticize, and if you don’t believe that you should see the other guy.) A Quick Survey Of Some Reasons To Not Be Epistemically ModestThe essence of my broader claim is that, in practice, modesty arguments get screened off by sufficient attention and understanding. Modesty is a method for when you cannot budget sufficient attention and understanding, which is most of the time.
The post was initially motivated as a response to Robin Hanson’s review of Eliezer’s highly related book Actual modesty, in practice, happens for a mix of complicated reasons, as I broke down in Once you have invested enough to understand the relevant questions and form an opinion, and have considered the arguments and other causes behind people’s opinions, you can and should substitute your judgment for theirs. Often you should do this despite the ‘experts’ or other epistemic would-be authorities having more relevant knowledge than you, and having devoted more time to related questions than you, outnumbering you, being higher status and more respected and credentialed than you, and perhaps being smarter than you. Your investment of sufficient attention and understanding can be and often is fractal. As illustrated in This, ideally along with a track record of testable predictions and the logic behind them, gives you many tools by which to evaluate whether someone, or some group, is functionally an epistemic peer or superior in a given context. The obvious mistake is ‘I disagree with them, therefore they are not an epistemic peer,’ which would defeat the whole purpose. What you can and should do is adjust your evaluation of sources based on the quality of their arguments. This blog has always been about not being epistemically modest, most famously:

Against Modesty’s BaileyTo close, here is a quick list of the top 10 reasons to decide to disagree with an expert or general consensus, either locally or globally, including a market price, or with others who might otherwise be presumed to know better. You know something they don’t, or know unique reasons you can trust key info. You have devoted more attention to something relevant than they have. They have not priced in or understood your reasons or reasoning. You checked, and their reasoning is bad and they should feel bad. No, seriously, often the reasoning is really dumb, or obviously motivated. Social desirability and other biases impacting the consensus. Information cascades and games of telephone distorting consensus. You can explain their statements in other ways that do not track truth, and your model predicts they would say that even if it was false. They seem to be lying to you, including ‘for your own good’ or the ‘common good.’ The demands for modesty and adherence to a particular epistemic authority are selective and asymmetrical. Why are you supposed to adhere to this particular person or group, rather than others?
On top of the reasons to not be epistemically modest, there are strong reasons to state your immodest beliefs: It is often more helpful to present your own views and your own reasons, rather than to mirror everyone else, to combat the reasons above.
