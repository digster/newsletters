Nikole Hannah-Jones wrote a brave self-critical piece in The New York Times [ https://substack.com/redirect/7a9e645a-65e0-46cb-b651-10733a72e686?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ], centrally about how she intentionally sent her daughter to a well-funded but failing school, PS 307, in the hopes of using voice to improve it, and how that utterly failed her daughter and failed to impact the school. 
Ultimately her daughter recovers, but we see a completely broken system that is unresponsive to internal voice, and until now she made no attempt to blow the whistle and utilize her professional position to do so.
The whole incident illustrates not only how messed up so many of our schools are despite abundant funding, it also raises the question of what the schools are even for. If her daughter can fully recover from severe deficits and be getting As and Bs at a challenging school within a year, what are the first eight years of school for? How much of the schools, grades and tests are fake? Can we somehow do better?

New York City PS 307 
Nikole sent her daughter Najya to PS 307 entirely by her own choice. 
She felt, by her own description, an obligation to her sense of the common good at the expense of her daughter. 
Her daughter began struggling. Tutors were brought in. Najya’s academics kept struggling. Then after many years her daughter looked up the school’s test scores, and oh boy was she pissed off. And this was when her daughter thought all the better schools would cost money.
Nikole Hannah-Jones [ https://substack.com/redirect/ef9774b3-7bb4-49a3-a068-56dd59a67fdb?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] (NYTimes): Even if her schools were not academically the strongest, I explained, her father and I knew that we could provide for her tutoring and any other assistance to make up for what her school may have lacked.
But my child just shook her head, her face a mask of disbelief, and something else: betrayal.
“So you had money to send me to one of the best schools, and you chose not to?” she said, hurling her words. “Because other kids are disadvantaged, you didn’t want to put me in a great school?”
Najya was asking what I’d accomplished by not choosing the “best” school for her. And the honest answer is, I don’t know. When it comes to the choices my husband and I have made about public education, these days I feel that I failed both my values and my child.
Yeah, pretty much. As many others said, it was a very brave and praiseworthy decision to write this post. It does not make up for the events that gave her the opportunity to write it. I won’t get into the minefield of trying to understand her logic, except that she put various ideological abstractions ahead of her daughter’s education.

This is where she fully loses me. This happens, and then she sends her daughter back for second grade. That’s all the information you need. You have a neighborhood public school that isn’t this one. School Spending
Via MR, with the tagline ‘I am happy to provoke my readers’ [ https://substack.com/redirect/2129951e-cdd4-4526-b7e1-fe25c85d52bb?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ]:
Patrick Bayer, Peter Blair and Kenneth Whaley: [ https://substack.com/redirect/2a9b15e6-f281-48a8-a04f-ebdcdb5b9bf4?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] The United States ranks low among peer countries on the ratio of teacher spending to per capita GDP. Is this (in)efficient? 
Using a spatial equilibrium model we show that spending on schools is efficient if an increase in school spending funded through local taxes would leave house prices unchanged. 
By exploiting plausibly exogenous shocks to both school spending and taxes, paired with 25 years of national data on local house prices, we find that an exogenous tax-funded increase in school spending would significantly raise house prices. 
These findings provide causal evidence that teacher spending in the U.S. is inefficiently low.
Claude confirms that all the taxes involved are property taxes. If raising taxes on houses causes the houses to rise in value, that is strong evidence that people value the services more than they cost. If a 1% tax-funded increase in current school spending would increase house prices by about 0.33%, as they claim, that’s huge. 
That shows that people are willing to pay for more school spending. That doesn’t mean that the extra spending results in better educational outcomes, or sufficiently better outcomes to justify the spending. 
It certainly doesn’t mean we are spending efficiently. What exactly could be costing places like NYC in the range of $36k per student? Do you think that if only they had $46k then that would fix things? 
It can still be correct, from the standpoint of parents, to overpay for the ability to overpay for marginal school quality, even when that spending is largely wasted, or the school system is imposing a huge overhead tax. 
The parents are over a barrel. By choosing expensive areas with expensive schools they are paying for reputational and status effects, positional goods and peer groups, in ways that potentially lack social value even if locally efficient.
