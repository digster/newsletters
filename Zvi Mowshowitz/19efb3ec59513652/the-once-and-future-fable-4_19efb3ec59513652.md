What’s The Worst That Could Happen?
I have never understood the theory that if everyone had the same sufficiently advanced superhuman AI hacker, that this would favor defense over offense, given the ability to concentrate firepower on a given target, even ignoring the ‘most code will still not be superhuman for a while’ issue. Teortaxes and others pointed to formal verification. I realize this exists, but so do
A fun aspect of chess is that people mostly do about as many ‘dumb things’ no matter what level of play, from the perspective of that level of play, because as you improve your play more things count as dumb. In theory perfect chess is possible, but we keep seeing the AIs get better beyond where I thought perfect play might be. My model of security is similar. My understanding is that if your code wants to do very well-defined simple things, meaningful formal verification is within reach. But many things are not so simple. You do distinctly have to avoid there being ransomware in every MRI scanner and iPhone. That sounds quite bad.
I do not think you could pull this off, but even if you did it would be a horrendous pain in the ass, and would rule out use of Fable on a wide variety of the most valuable software projects.
The strongest argument here is data retention, some amount of KYC plus classifier-driven observability as the real defense. As in, if Anthropic wants to figure out who is using a lot of Fable tokens for major cybersecurity related operations, that is something they can definitely do, and they can associate it with your reputation. This is not foolproof, it does not fully ‘fix’ the ‘jailbreak,’ but it is part of a defense-in-depth series of obstacles that greatly raises the de facto difficulty, risks and costs of offensive operations. Do enough of that, as I expect Anthropic did, and the balance of impact of Fable shifts so that you’re safer with it released than not released.
The name of the game is effective exploitation. That requires a chain of exploitation. Mythos was special, as I understand it, in large part because it could on its own find and complete such chains, often in unexpected ways.
This is where Arthur loses me. The value of cybersecurity is not ‘less than a basis point of all IT value.’ The value of cybersecurity is all of the value of all IT, period, in the sense that if your cybersecurity fully fails then you have no IT, or might be even worse off than that. Tail risk management does indeed have to take priority sometimes. That does not need to involve ‘AI might get everyone killed.’ This is the same as saying ‘pandemic defense is a small percentage of the national budget’ as a reason not to worry about bioweapons. No matter the right level of worry about bioweapons, that argument is a very bad reason to not worry about bioweapons. The question is not how much typically goes wrong in cybersecurity. It is what goes wrong in a world where cybersecurity collapses. What happens if they breach our national security systems? If hackers penetrate almost any corporate network? If our hospitals and other critical infrastructure can’t stay online? If you can’t do a variety of things online anymore because they are no longer secure?
The fire department considers basically everything subordinate to fire risk and to their own convenience. I think this is quite bad, because the fire risks being prevented are often absurdly small, a vestige of a time when a fire could burn down a city. However, if things were different, and a repeat of the Chicago Fire or similar was a real possibility, then you can see why saying ‘oh the fire department is small expense why are they bossing us around on every little detail of our streets and houses’ does not make so much sense anymore. When we say ‘cybersecurity risk is a tax that we must pay’ the correct question is to talk price. What is the size of this tax? Are you so sure it is worth paying? You could also challenge that the alternative involves paying that tax soon anyway, as part of the calculation. That matters, too, and must be factored in.
Yeah, that’s not how catastrophic risks and existential risks work. People need to accept this. You do not get to wait until you know exactly who is going to do what, where, how and when before you act. If you want for that, and the moment does arrive, you will be too late. You don’t get to say ‘oh, now I see the one particular system they’re trying to hack that we can’t let them hack’ because the way you find out is that they already hacked it. The Data Retention Policy Is About Defense In DepthIf you want to stop abuses of a powerful AI model like Fable, you have to do some amount of data retention, so you can figure out, including retroactively, when someone is up to no good, and respond. This is part of any sane defense-in-depth strategy, and rather costly given many enterprises cannot accept it. To those who say versions of ‘they will train on the data’ or ‘ Pick Up The PhoneIf you make a rule limiting AI frontier model development, as we may have to at some point, you need an agreement with robust monitoring and enforcement mechanisms. There will be great temptation to cheat. If you make a rule limiting AI frontier model deployment, against making it available to the public, you do not do a great job protecting against existential risk, but you do not need such aggressive monitoring, as it is very easy to see what is or is not available to the public, it is and relatively easy to enforce by taking down providers.
The obvious hole is that if someone decides to yolo an open model release, you cannot put the weights back in the bottle. You can absolutely prevent most use and punish them for the release, but you cannot undo the release. If that release was truly existentially risky, we’re all cooked. But all the top releases so far have come from entities that could easily be required to seek prior approval.
