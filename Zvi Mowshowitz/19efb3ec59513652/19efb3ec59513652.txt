This is where Arthur loses me. The value of cybersecurity is not ‘less than a basis point of all IT value.’
The value of cybersecurity is all of the value of all IT, period, in the sense that if your cybersecurity fully fails then you have no IT, or might be even worse off than that. 
Tail risk management does indeed have to take priority sometimes. That does not need to involve ‘AI might get everyone killed.’ This is the same as saying ‘pandemic defense is a small percentage of the national budget’ as a reason not to worry about bioweapons. No matter the right level of worry about bioweapons, that argument is a very bad reason to not worry about bioweapons.  
The question is not how much typically goes wrong in cybersecurity. It is what goes wrong in a world where cybersecurity collapses. What happens if they breach our national security systems? If hackers penetrate almost any corporate network? If our hospitals and other critical infrastructure can’t stay online? If you can’t do a variety of things online anymore because they are no longer secure? 
Policymakers need to recalibrate. If AI is the transformative technology of our age, it is because its value in scientific production, software development, and labor augmentation; this value absolutely swamps the risk presented by patch reverse engineering, which is itself a malady swamped by the benefits of more effective patching. AI is machine intelligence: literally general-purpose capability embodied in complex software. To deprive the world of intelligence because of the risk of patch reengineering is to surrender to extreme risk intolerance
I fear that we've built up a cybersecurity complex -- including political, bureaucratic, think tanks, and media actors -- that is extremely risk-obsessed and views the IT economy as subordinate to the cyber economy. This is an error, however: cybersecurity risk is a tax that we must pay to benefit from the collective intelligence generated via the IT economy. We cannot allow it to be an anchor, depriving us of progressively advanced intelligence.
The fire department considers basically everything subordinate to fire risk and to their own convenience. I think this is quite bad, because the fire risks being prevented are often absurdly small, a vestige of a time when a fire could burn down a city.

When we say ‘cybersecurity risk is a tax that we must pay’ the correct question is to talk price. What is the size of this tax? Are you so sure it is worth paying?
You could also challenge that the alternative involves paying that tax soon anyway, as part of the calculation. That matters, too, and must be factored in.

The Data Retention Policy Is About Defense In Depth

Pick Up The Phone
If you make a rule limiting AI frontier model development, as we may have to at some point, you need an agreement with robust monitoring and enforcement mechanisms. There will be great temptation to cheat.
If you make a rule limiting AI frontier model deployment, against making it available to the public, you do not do a great job protecting against existential risk, but you do not need such aggressive monitoring, as it is very easy to see what is or is not available to the public, it is and relatively easy to enforce by taking down providers.
davidad [ https://x.com/davidad/status/2052329322720153642 ]: Since I have spoken about the infeasibility of an international agreement that would halt or slow the development of superintelligence (game-theoretically unstable now, at best), I should clarify there is no such obstacle to agreements restricting public access to dangerous AIs.
This is because making an AI which meets some criteria publicly accessible is:
 (a) a trivially easy condition to monitor, and
 (b) trivially easy to immediately renege on, if the counterparty reneges.
Together these make a “we won’t if you don’t” agreement potentially stable.
The obvious hole is that if someone decides to yolo an open model release, you cannot put the weights back in the bottle. You can absolutely prevent most use and punish them for the release, but you cannot undo the release. If that release was truly existentially risky, we’re all cooked. But all the top releases so far have come from entities that could easily be required to seek prior approval.
