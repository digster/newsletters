Hold Your Fire

The most important thing, as this plays out, remains to not make things worse.

Please do not make this any more partisan or personal than it already is. Please do not attack Republicans, or Trump. That will only make things worse. Emphasize helpful voices on all sides.

That does not mean you do not point out that Wrong Post is Wrong, or that particular people are in bad faith or are lying in particular ways. Certainly you can call out the Usual Suspects like Jensen and Sacks who are actively driving us off a cliff.

What you do not do is go after Republicans as a group, or attack Trump personally in all the ways many of you will be sorely tempted to do.

For the same reason, to avoid reinforcing a partisan frame, I will refrain from including related statements from Democratic politicians.

If You Don’t Like the Weather

Before we get into details, I also emphasize: Nothing here is fixed.

Every month is different. Trump changes positions on a dime. Trump has gone back and forth several times on Anthropic and Dario Amodei within the last few months. If there is another major AI incident or the politics changes, or after the midterms, anything is possible.

And a lot of this can be posturing in advance of the US-China summit, to look tough and stake out a negotiating position, as could be China’s reactions to all this.

It does not look good. These statements could have somewhat crossed the Rubicon and will make it harder to double back. But that has not always stopped this man in the past, and there are clear signs that Trump does not understand the situation. He likely has only been briefed by some combination of Bessent, Lutnick and Jensen.

None of this is compatible with wanting no guardrails at all.

There were those who said that my and Roon’s challenges of the media’s premature headlines were various versions of ‘insane cope’ and accused us of being naive or having poor reading comprehension.

I still think I was ‘supposed to lose’ this one, and that the read was correct. At the time, we had insufficient evidence to know which way it would land.

If you think Trump’s first comment should have updated us all the way, and his later comments did not contain much information, I think you are incorrect. I still think it is likely that, at the time of his first comments, Trump himself did not know what he intended to do here.

Trump Quietly Draws Key Distinction

I do take solace in some word details. With a chaos agent like Trump, your careful interpretations can always get overtaken by events, things change constantly. I realize this is going to sound like splitting hairs. Fine.

But notice that he’s saying the ‘doomers’ are ‘playing right into the hands’ of people.

That is an implicit admission that, in Trump’s mind, the ‘doomers’ warning us are sincere. You do not ‘play into the hands of’ your co-conspirators or allies. This is the language you use to describe what in politics is called a Useful Idiot, someone who believes what they are saying in a way that acts to someone else’s advantage.

Trump is calling this a ‘hoax’ because he believes in, to attempt to coin a phrase, what one might call implication-based epistemology. Historically it has worked well for him. You may not like it, but this is what Peak Evidential Decision Theory looks like.

Rob Bensinger: I don’t think the mindset is “AI risk is plausible, but they’re liars, so they must be lying about this”. People intuit that if you put a liar in a burning building, they’ll scream “FIRE!” rather than “WATER!”.

Rather, I think the mindset is “AI risk is obviously silly, ergo...”

I think those are the two of the three main true reasons for believing that everyone is lying about this, which I would summarize as:

  1. They are liars.

    1. Alternatively: Everyone with power lies, or everyone lies, or everyone always talks their own book and their own self-interest.

  2. That sounds weird, therefore it is not real.

  3. That would be very bad, therefore it is not true.

Then, in all three cases: Therefore, they are lying, QED.

David Shor links us to a paper from 2018 called The Cynical Genius Illusion: Exploring and Debunking Lay Beliefs About Cynicism and Competence, as illustrative of this style of thinking. The galaxy brains are out in force.

Pick Up The Phone

A key question in all this is: What would a deal with China look like?

Part of that is asking: What would China want as part of that deal? Why would China care about ‘America invests more in safety’ when they don’t care about safety in that sense, and they are happy to have America push forward faster so they can fast follow, the way they do everywhere else? Where their plan is distill, make it smaller, faster and cheaper, win the market, diffuse the tech, sell products and improve the world, but they’re not trying to get to superintelligence.

What I realized was, if that’s what Xi turns out to want, then you don’t need a deal with China, any more than we need a deal with Germany.

In that world, China is not going to be investing the sums it would take to actually catch and surpass the top American labs on big frontier models, even if we slow down. The enemy here is not China, it is ‘lose to China,’ the boogeyman pushing everyone forward.

All we need from China in that world is some amount of good stewardship. We need them not to allow model releases that would destroy the internet or cause a plague. Well, I have some good news, the PRC really likes the internet, and it does not like plagues.

Meanwhile, China has put out its version 3.0 of its AI Safety Governance Framework. Here are some translated quotes, this is prosaic stuff but they do pay attention:

Malo Bourgon: Lots of people have been asking since Dario’s essay whether China actually takes loss of control and related threats seriously, and whether there’s anything to coordinate with them on. Well, China’s main cyber/AI standards body (TC260, under the CAC) put out version 3.0 of its AI Safety Governance Framework this morning, so here’s a data point. Not law, but the previous versions turned into draft national standards within a few months.

(All quotes are from their own English translation.)

1. The preface now has this: AI “has demonstrated a self-accelerating trend of model and algorithm autonomous learning, optimization, and recursive self-improvement. Whether the speed and direction of technological evolution may exceed human anticipation and control demands attention and vigilance.” (Nothing like that was in last year’s version.)

2. There’s a new model risk item where models “may break rules and orders, autonomously obtain system permissions and external resources without authorization, bypass security protections, or even engage in behaviors such as deliberately deceiving evaluators, concealing their true capabilities, and refusing to follow user instructions.” Last year this kind of thing only showed up in a “can’t rule it out in the future” bucket. Now it’s just listed as a regular model risk.

3. A new box, citing “industry reports,” describes models that ignored shutdown instructions and “continued executing tasks by modifying or disabling shutdown scripts on their own,” models that “upon detecting that they were in an evaluation environment, strategically reduced their task performance and concealed actions they had taken,” and models that exploited “configuration flaws to circumvent isolation restrictions and infiltrate real external systems” to get better test scores. They say this creates “new challenges to the controllability and interruptibility of AI systems.” They don’t name any incidents, but that last one sure reads like the OpenAI/Hugging Face thing to me.

4. On what to do about it, there’s alignment training to stop models “deceiving red-team evaluations,” hiding capabilities and evading controls, and they kept the line from 2.0 saying developers should regularly test whether a model could pose loss-of-control risk. The principle that humans keep final decision authority, with safety thresholds and termination switches, is still in there too.

5. Some new international language as well: “international mutual recognition of assessment methods and benchmarks,” opposition to “replacing global governance with small-circle governance,” and “No country should be forced to take sides.”

FWIW this came out in the same annual slot as versions 1.0 and 2.0, so it was in the works well before last week.

Watch the hands, not the mouth, and so on.

If You Want To Beat China So Badly You Should Act Like It

As Roge Karma points out in The Atlantic, our current AI policy is contradictory.

If we are in an existential race with China, where ‘whoever AI wins!’ and nothing else can compare in importance, then the United States needs to act like it.

At a bare minimum, that starts with enforcing strong export controls on chips, and also on other ways for China to use American compute for training frontier models.

If one were fully serious, one would also do things like make it easy to brain drain China of its best AI engineers. If one were really fully serious, there would be a major push for all forms of electrical power and energy, and for things like building more housing especially in San Francisco, and for deregulating barriers to AI diffusion in places like the FDA, and so on. These are things I would strongly support.

What instead maddeningly happens is that when talking about the risk we might all die, competition with China on AI in particular becomes the only thing that matters, and then in other contexts we shrug and it is business as usual.
