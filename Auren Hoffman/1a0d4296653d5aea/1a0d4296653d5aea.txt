moats are dead.
every software company has a slide that explains why its customers cannot leave. switching costs. proprietary data. network effects. the annual contract. none of it holds anymore. customers are leaving, and they are leaving FASTER every quarter.
a friend of mine runs a startup doing about $1B in revenue. we are small investors.
he mentioned he has taken one vacation in eight years. one week. in eight years.
i told him that was crazy and he was going to burn out.
he said he did not know what else to do. they have no moat.
which made no sense to me. the business is growing fast and they lead their category.
he explained it like an operator. they sell AI products, so they sell to the smartest buyers on earth. if they do not have the best product that week, those buyers do not buy. if they do not have the best product for two weeks, the customers they already have start to leave.
that’s the whole business. week to week.
so i started thinking about who actually does have a moat. and the answer is pretty much nobody.
a moat is for defending a castle
moats are for defending territory. mostly for defending very brittle castles.
back in the day you would dig a moat around the castle. then you would make it deeper. then deeper again. you would add a drawbridge. crocodiles in the water if you could get them. and if someone was dumb enough to try to swim across anyway, you would pour boiling oil on them from the wall.
and it worked. for a few hundred years the castle was the dominant piece of military technology in Europe.
but look at what it did to the people inside. everything they did was defense. every resource went into a bigger wall, a deeper ditch, a heavier gate. they were not going anywhere. they were sitting in one spot waiting to get attacked, and the only plan was to make the attack more expensive.
a moat does not just keep people out. it keeps you in.
the moat was never the product. it was the migration
a software moat was never the product. nobody stayed on a mediocre CRM because they loved the CRM. they stayed because leaving meant migrating the data, rebuilding the integrations, retraining the team, getting procurement and legal and security involved, and finding 18 months of engineering time nobody had.
we all ran mediocre software systems, mediocre cloud providers, mediocre data providers, mediocre APIs. for YEARS. leaving was harder than putting up with it, so the bad product survived and its revenue was incredible.
the moat WAS the migration. it was never anything else.
that was the software castle. the moat was the migration. the drawbridge was the annual contract. the boiling oil was the enterprise sales team. and every dollar of engineering went into making the walls thicker instead of making the product better.
then the cannon showed up
castles did not slowly decline. they got obsoleted in about fifty years.
once gunpowder artillery got good, a wall that took a generation to build could be knocked down in a week. constantinople had the best walls in the world for a thousand years. the ottomans brought cannons in 1453 and walls stopped mattering.
by 1550 nobody was building tall stone castles anymore. every one of them was still standing. every one of them was worthless.
the cannon for software is AI.
the first is that AI does migrations REALLY well. moving data between schemas, rewriting integrations against a new API, regenerating the docs. this is exactly the work the models got good at in the last eighteen months. a migration that took a year plus a services contract now takes a 3 weeks with gpt-6 and /goal. the migration that now takes three weeks will soon take a weekend.
second -- every competing vendor will do the migration for you. it is now part of the sales motion. and your own AI tools will help you leave. the same model writing your code will rewrite your integrations against whoever is cheaper.
AND buyers stopped signing long contracts. this one matters the most.
we have over 200 companies at Flex Capital and we looked at how they actually buy software and AI services right now. almost EVERY vendor relationship is month-to-month or usage-based. this is not because they got better at negotiating but because they have ZERO clue what they will be using in two months. the tool that is obviously right today might be obviously wrong before the next quarter.
nobody signs a year for something they might not need in two weeks.
once nobody signs a year, there is no renewal cycle to hide behind. the walls are still standing but they are worthless.
gross retention is the moat metric and it just fell four points
net revenue retention is the quotable number AND the number that lets you hide churn behind upsell.
gross revenue retention asks a simpler question: of the revenue you had, how much stayed. that is the closest thing to a moat you can put in a number.
from the 2026 Aleph / Benchmarkit survey of private SaaS companies median GRR fell from 88% to 84%. the 75th percentile fell from 95% to 91%. four points, in one year, in every quartile. that is the wall coming down.
the companies that stopped pretending are doing better. usage-based companies are at 108% net retention. seat-based companies are at 98%. (Aleph [ https://substack.com/redirect/1c573155-91cc-45fc-a688-e3bc70b1d225?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ]) the ones who gave up on lock-in are beating the ones still trying to hold it.
the only moat left is this week’s product
if it is not the contract, the data, the brand, or the integrations…
what is it?
the best product this week. nothing else.
not the best product. the best product THIS WEEK. and then again next week. there is never a point where you get to stop.
the buyer changed too. the enterprise buyer of 2014 was a committee running an 18 month RFP. that process was super slow and political and easy to game, which is exactly why relationships and a good sales team could beat a better product.
the buyer in 2026 is an engineer with an eval harness. they will run your product against your competitor’s on their own data, on a tuesday. and post the numbers in slack. someone on their team reads your competitor’s release notes within an hour of publication.
you cannot out-market or out-sell a worse product to that person.
you can only out-ship it.
and that means you need to always be on offense. you need to be attacking your competitors by continually creating a better product. you are Genghis Khan, not some two-bit lord in a damp and dirty castle.
private equity bought the castles
enter private equity. PE bought moats, with leverage. the entire PE software model is buy a mature sticky product, cut the R&D, raise prices every year, harvest the lock-in for a decade. every step depends on the customer being unable to leave.
it is the purest castle strategy in all of business. do not build anything new. just make the walls thicker and charge the people inside more rent.
in february, Thoma Bravo and Vista both went out to reassure their LPs that AI was not going to eat their portfolios. (Bloomberg [ https://substack.com/redirect/636b9f08-9a01-42ca-b044-8e6ba99582e4?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ]) Vista’s CEO emailed clients and scheduled a webinar. Thoma Bravo circulated a letter. lenders are demanding higher yields to refinance software debt. one Thoma Bravo company added two years to its maturity and paid an extra three quarters of a point for the privilege.
it might work.  but all those “safe” software companies are going to need a refounding. they will need a much better product.  they have the first crack at their own customers, but they cannot take them for granted.  the old PE playbook will not work.  the new playbook looks like at venture-backed company might look: ABS: Always Be Shipping.
of course, when the largest owners of software moats have to send a letter explaining that the moats are fine, the moats are NOT fine. platform buyouts in software are at a decade low too.
none of these executives are lazy. most of them are grinding very hard. they are just grinding on a moat in a market that no longer pays for moats.
the market is not going to change its mind.
be Genghis Khan, not a castle
the mongols never built a castle.
they were all cavalry. every rider had three to five horses and they could cover 100 miles in a day. they did not hold land or defend anything. they moved and attacked, and when they ran into a walled city they did not dig a moat around it. they brought the siege engineers they had captured from the last city and took it.
in about 25 years they conquered more territory than Rome did in 400. the largest contiguous land empire in history, built by people who had no interest in walls. that is the model now.
the best defense in the world of AI is attack, attack, attack. do not worry about holding land. constantly be on the offense. ship this week. ship again next week. the only moat left is being the best product this week, and the only way to be the best product this week is to have shipped something better than everyone else since last week.
what this does to the people inside
the castle had one advantage nobody talks about. the people inside could sleep.
the walls did the work. the moat did the work. the annual contract renewed itself while the CEO was on vacation. that is what a moat actually bought you. not revenue. it let you rest.
my friend has not taken a vacation in eight years because he understood that if the only defense is attack, the only thing standing between him and churn is whether the product shipped this week. he is not a workaholic. he is a mongol. there is no castle to sleep in.
if there is no moat, you need to work insane hours all the time because the only thing you have is your product.
second-order effects
if “best product this week” replaces “locked in for three years” as the thing that keeps a customer, here is what changes.
software multiples do not come back. the recurring revenue multiple was always a durability multiple. it priced the friction. the friction is gone. we spent a decade paying 18x for revenue that turned out to be month-to-month and much more susceptible to churn.
the PE software playbook is over. without switching costs, “cut R&D and raise prices” just means customers leave faster. a lot of capital is currently marked against moats that no longer exist, and the refinancing is where it shows up first.
sales shrinks, product grows. you cannot sell your way out of a worse product into a month-to-month contract. the eighteen-month RFP is dead and the sales org built for it goes with it.
venture underwriting moves earlier. you cannot model ten years of cash flow off a two-week contract. capital moves toward underwriting a team’s ability to keep shipping rather than a franchise’s ability to keep collecting. the team becomes the whole thesis at every stage, not just seed.
incumbency becomes a liability. legacy customers expecting old behavior, an architecture built for the last paradigm, a sales org for a buying process that no longer exists. all the stuff that used to protect them now slows them down.
founder tenure gets shorter. if the job requires the intensity my friend described, fewer people do it for ten years. expect earlier founder-to-CEO handoffs, and expect the ones who stay to be a smaller and weirder group.
M&A inverts. you stop buying the locked-in revenue book, because the book walks. you buy the team and the current product -- the two things most likely to leave after the earn-out.
the bottom line
companies are buying more software than ever. they just will not sign for it, and they leave the moment something better ships.
the migration is free. the contract is monthly. the buyer runs evals. every vendor and every AI tool on the market is standing by to help your customer leave.
the only thing you have is your product, and you have to be the best one this week. then do it again next week.
goodbye moat, goodbye predictable revenues, goodbye 2010s era.
you are not a lord in a castle anymore. you are genghis khan. always be attacking.
