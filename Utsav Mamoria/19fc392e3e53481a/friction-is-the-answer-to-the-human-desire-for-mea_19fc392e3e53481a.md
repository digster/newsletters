If you found these elements lying around in a pile of dirt, you’d walk past it. If you assembled all scientists in the world (Did I say ALL?), and if you could raise the ones who are dead, and put all of them in a room, along with all the books ever written, you would still not be able to do this.
Do what?
Construct the human body.
In the time you read the paragraph above, your body has made a million red blood cells. They are already speeding around you, coursing through your veins, bouncing around 1,50,000 times, to deliver oxygen to your cells, and then, they perish. Only for a million red blood cells will be made again.
It takes seven billion billion billion atoms to make you. No one can say why they have an urgent desire to be you. They are all mindless particles, without a single thought or notion between them. Yet somehow, for the length of your existence, they will build and maintain all the countless systems necessary to let you enjoy the unique condition called life. You are so large, that if you were to unpack yourself, the airways between your lungs would stretch from London to Moscow. If you formed all your DNA into a single fine strand it would stretch ten billion miles, beyond Pluto.¹
There is enough of you to leave the solar system. You are in the most literal sense - Cosmic.
Sit with that thought for a moment, and then ask yourself, as the peerless Mary Oliver, did
Tell me, what is it you plan to do with your one wild and precious life?²
You exercise minimally and eat maximally. You spend significant amount of time in front of a glowing screen, in a near vegetative state. You buy things you don’t really want, but want them because everybody else does. Everybody wants them because a tiny clique of people who now overwhelmingly decide what popular culture is, said so. A tinier set decides how much we will pay for them. And we often consume what others can see. The point of conspicuous consumption is public visibility and signaling: at one end, we buy luxury goods because others can see and interpret them as indicators of wealth or class. For those of us who believe we are more refined than the rich, we buy niche brands, limited edition products, hard to find or out of print books to signal our taste.
You and I, are engaged in what philosopher Alasdair MacIntyre calls the pursuit of External Goods. Allow me to explain
Internal goods are the goods that belong to a practice itself – things like mastery, excellence, understanding, or the shared benefits that come from doing an activity well (for example, really learning chess, doing good science, or being a genuinely good teacher)
External goods are rewards that are attached to the practice from outside – money, power, prestige, prizes, promotions, rankings – which you could in principle get by many different routes.
So what happens in a world when choice is abundant, and we only have an illusion of control? We can get groceries delivered to us in 10 minutes (For my non-Indian readers, this is a reality in metropolitan India, thanks to exploitatively cheap labour). Our food is instant, high calorie comfort food, and delivery apps are more than happy to suggest ‘What you ordered last time’. We switch between pictures of a friends wedding by the lake, the latest progressive metal concert in town, a beaver devouring cabbage and images of a genocide in full flow in a matter of seconds. From the most important to the most mundane aspects of life are served to us in an inexhaustible buffet. The moment we don’t like something, we can swipe it away. The best storytellers in the world are available to us with a monthly subscription. And we dismiss their work with an infinite scroll. Or worse, we doze off in front of screens. Even if we don’t, seamless transitions remove a natural pause to reconsider, so “one episode” becomes three hours without a conscious decision. Content tuned to our preferences and beliefs reduces cognitive friction (no discomfort, no challenge), but also reduces exposure to diverse viewpoints and deep thinking.
Worse is how we treat each other. We like or react in an instant, registering superficial approval without real engagement. We send reels to our friends, but weeks could go by before we have a real conversation with them. On dating apps, we judge people in a few seconds, and take only slightly longer to ghost them.
AI, probably the most polarizing invention of our times, has ‘democratized’ writing, making videos, creating music and art. It can summarize and draft documents, build apps and website, reducing both busy work AND cognitive work. Silicon valley, which is obsessed with the capture of your time and attention, is in love with creating ‘frictionless’ experiences. We have meal subscriptions, one click checkouts and BNPL (Buy Now Pay Later). We have free trials and “set and forget” subscriptions, so we keep paying for things we don’t use, because cancelling requires effort.
And where does that leave us?
The ‘frictionless’ experience is designed to minimize our choice and agency. This the bot-ification of human beings - they want us to be people who just click, scroll, pay, eat, consume endlessly, in just the way that profits them, consistently and enormously.
In the pursuit of this convenience and frictionless life, we lose a critical element of the human experience: the ability to have productive struggle. When everything comes easy, we learn fewer things, and engage in minimal practice. Practice that produces the greatest pieces of art, the most pathbreaking of scientific discoveries and the most superhuman feats of athletes. At the heart of practice lies the idea of trying. And trying is something a high school girl, living in the tiny town of Kisújszállás in Communist Hungary in the 1960’s, did a lot of.
The woman who saved the world
“What do you think, Karikokati?”
Her high school biology teacher, a man always dressed impeccably enough to carry off a colorful pullover, asked her. He had just read out a quote
“In studying life, you keep diving from higher level to lower ones until somewhere along the way life fades out, leaving you empty handed”
These were the words of Albert Szent-Györgyi, a Hungarian biochemist who first isolated Vitamin C, and whose breakthrough research into cellular respiration laid the groundwork for the identification of the Krebs cycle, and went on to win the Nobel Prize in physiology / medicine in 1937.
The girl who was asked to weigh in on the quote was Katalin Karikó, and by the end of the school year, her class decided to send a letter to Györgyi who now lived in America. And in a way only childlike innocence can imagine, they addressed the letter to
Albert Szent-Györgyi
USA
Months later, they receive a letter back. Personally written by Györgyi, and enclosed with it was a copy of his book The Living State. It was inscribed
To the enthusiastic cultivators of science in Kisújszállás
Little could he have imagined, how maddeningly, beautifully and thoroughly accurate he would be.
Born to a bookkeeper mother and a butcher father, Katalin lived in a modest house without running water, refrigerator, or television; they heated a single room in winter and fetched water from the street pump. Jobs and things were scarce in communist Hungary, and her mother had said
“If you know how to provide people with meat, you will never starve”
This reasoning was neither hyperbole or paranoia. This was the aftermath of World War 1, when famine swept across the entire European continent, and Hungary being on the losing side, had devastated the country - Starvation nipped at its heels, and having any kind of work that paid was a luxury.
Years later, Katalin’s mother would describe to her a memory from that time. Her mother was fifteen years old, heading to work at the pharmacy, as she did every day. She passed a broken Russian tank that had been abandoned on the street. Lying next to it was a dead soldier. Her mother looked at him. He was her age, maybe even younger.
A boy, a dead soldier of war, representing an army that had caused so much devastation. He was just a boy.
History, of course, happens to children, too.
In 1940, shortly before World War 2 broke out, her father had to enlist in the Royal Hungarian Army. Post the war, the Communist Party openly assumed power. They nationalized manufacturing, education, banking and transportation. They collectivized land, and private businesses became public property. Her father’s butcher shop was seized too. He was subsequently arrested, accused of agitating against the party. He was ‘terminated’ by the party, making him a political pariah. He could no longer work any official job, and he begged them, saying that he had Katalin and her sister to feed. Nothing came of it.
To say that the odds were stacked against Katalin would be an understatement. But Katalin had other plans.
Katalin excelled in science during primary school, earning third place in a national biology competition by eighth grade, out of all students in Hungary. When she was 13, she traveled alone from Kisújszállás to Budapest—around 150 km—for a selective science summer camp and later for a week‑long national competition, where she again placed third in the country. A cultivator of science was beginning to take shape.
In the fall of 1973, as the daughter of a political paraiah, as someone whose life could have been completely unremarkable, Katalin Karikó, known to her friends as Kati, started in the biology program at the University of Szeged, a class of just eighteen people.
Kati worked hard, and it paid off. By the third year, she was recommended for the Study Award of the Hungarian People’s Republic, the most prestigious merit award a student could get. It allowed her to live for free in the dormitory and receive a small stipend. But when the final list came back from the ministry, her name was missing. There was no error, it had been actively removed. She was a daughter of someone who had been accused of crimes against the Communist Party.
But the party had underestimated the will of a young Hungarian woman.
Despite the setback, she went onto work with a lipid research group at the Biological Research Centre (BRC) of the Hungarian Academy of Sciences, bridging her early interest in biology with formal biochemical research. When her father learnt that her boss, an outstanding researcher named Tibor Farkas, once wanted to be a butcher, he was ecstatic! So when she graduated, he made smoked sausages, and the lipid research team cooked it in the lab using Bunsen burners. The air filled with thick, fragrant swirls. As they bit into the sausages, the juice squirted everywhere, staining their lab coats and dripping onto the floor. It tasted perfect. It tasted like home. In that moment, her two disparate worlds had come together.
But then, for the next seven years working at the BRC, even after earning her PhD, she was never promoted from a research associate position. She did not know why. She was eventually terminated owing to a lack of funds.
But Kati was Kati.
She sought jobs abroad and accepted a postdoctoral role at Temple University in Philadelphia with Robert J. Suhadolnik. But by then, she had a family. A husband and a two year old daughter, Susan.
She sold their car for $1200. At the time, Hungarians were not allowed to make more than $50 in foreign currency out of the country, as this was a sign a person might be defecting. Yet in America, they would need every dollar they could get their hands on. She and her husband sewed the money into Susan’s teddy bear. It was everything they had, protected only by plush fabric and a few inches of stuffing. And the little Susan, unaware of the gravity of what her mother was doing, left with them for America.
Susan grew up to become a two-time Olympic gold medal winner in rowing.
History, of course, happens to children, too.
At Temple, between 1985 and 1988, Katalin worked on double‑stranded RNA (dsRNA) and participated in early clinical trials exploring RNA‑based treatments for conditions such as AIDS.
In 1988, Karikó accepted a job at Johns Hopkins University without first informing her lab advisor Suhadolnik of her intention to leave Temple. Suhadolnik told her that if she went to Johns Hopkins, he would have her deported, and subsequently reported her to U.S. immigration authorities, claiming that she was "illegally" in the United States. In the time it took her to successfully challenge the resulting extradition order, Johns Hopkins withdrew the job offer. Suhadolnik continued bad-mouthing Karikó, making it impossible for her to get a new position at other institutions.
But Kati was Kati.
In 1989 she joined the University of Pennsylvania (Penn) to work with cardiologist Elliot Barnathan, shifting to messenger RNA as a therapeutic tool. At Penn, she and colleagues showed that introducing mRNA into cells could make them produce new proteins, convincing her that mRNA could underpin a new form of gene therapy.
However, throughout the 1990s mRNA fell out of favor; her grants were repeatedly rejected and in 1995 she was demoted, losing salary and status and never receiving tenure. She was being demoted multiple times and working off of soft money, yet she chose to keep pursuing mRNA despite colleagues and funders dismissing it as impractical, and even laughed at it.
The history of science, it turns out, is filled with stories of very smart people laughing at good ideas.
Kati was looking for a partnership that would help her continue her work in mRNA.
Nature is filled with lock and key partnerships, in which two very different molecules - an enzyme and the substrate it acts upon, for example - fit together exactly right. When the two find each other, their complementary parts snap securely into place, and a remarkable chain of events unfolds.
Again and again in biology, lock meets key and big things happen.
Human digestion. Antibiotics. Insulin and Type 2 diabetes. Painkillers. Cancer treatments.
By 1997, she began collaborating with immunologist Drew Weissman at Penn on why synthetic mRNA triggered strong immune reactions. Together they discovered that modifying nucleosides in mRNA could make mRNA far less inflammatory while preserving its protein‑coding function, a key step for therapeutic use. In 2005 they published seminal papers and in 2006, they co‑founded RNARx to commercialize non‑immunogenic, nucleoside‑modified mRNA, later licensing this IP to companies.
Despite the importance of their work, funding and institutional support at Penn remained weak; she stayed in non‑tenure track roles and was described as “not faculty quality” by some administrators. In 2013 she was effectively pushed out or “forced to retire” from Penn and not reinstated, even as interest in mRNA was starting to revive.
But Penn had underestimated the will of a young not so young Hungarian woman.
All her life, she had been able to count on one hand the number of people she’d known in the “mRNA Believers Club”. She found a couple of them in a relatively small quiet company out of Mainz, Germany. The cofounders, were physician scientists - a Turkish immigrant couple, Uğur Şahin and Özlem Türeci. The company they started was called BioNTech. A company that did not have a website even after five years in existence. Okay, they did have one. One that said ‘Under Construction’.
Katalin joined them as a Vice President. She had colleagues from sixty five different countries. Not all of them spoke German, but all of them did speak science. Kati was at home again. There were however, no more smoked sausages to cook over Bunsen burners. She had lost her father to a heart attack.
And then in 2016, she was diagnosed with the cancer of the parotid gland, the gland that makes saliva. By the end of 2018, her mother passed away from kidney failure.
But Kati was Kati.
She got BioNTech to fund immunologist Drew Weissman’s research at Penn. And that allowed him to make concrete progress on yet another lock and key mechanism.
Vaccines.
And then one day in January, six years after she had joined BioNTech, Uğur read an article in The Lancet about something unfolding, thousands of miles away from the quaint town of Mainz, Germany.
A new respiratory virus circulating through Wuhan, China.
I don’t need to tell you what happened next. Anyone alive in early 2020 has their own memories.
BioNTech entered into an agreement with Pfizer to make the Covid vaccine. Katalin and thousands of people in all kinds of roles: From scientists to employees to contractors worked at breathtaking speed. The vaccine went into a clinical trial with 43,448 participants at 153 sites around the world. And then everyone waited.
On the evening of November 8th, 2020, the telephone rang at Katalin’s home. It was Uğur. The results were in. Unequivocally, it worked.
The modified mRNA vaccine had 95% efficacy against the virus strain. The vaccine was approved for emergency use. Billions of us received it. As the needle filled with modified mRNA went into her skin, she began to weep³. She felt humbled and honored. She was awarded the Nobel Prize in Medicine in 2023.
History, of course, happens to children, too.
In 2021, Katalin visited the University of Segzed and stood beside the bronze statue of fellow Nobel laureate Albert Szent-Györgyi.⁴ Two cultivators of science, decades apart, who changed the world.⁵
Welcome to the Katalin Karikó fan club. So tell me, dear fellow club member, what is it you plan to do with your one wild and precious life?
Because each time Katalin hit a roadblock, she found a way. Each time the ground was pulled from under her feet, she broke new ground. Instead of finding an easier path, she chose friction. The kind that grates against your existence, bruises your ego, batters your soul, burns through your motivation and scorches up your willpower. In her memoir, Katalin says
Do not believe that hard work and happiness are in opposition. Do not believe that one must embrace leisure to know joy.
As I read through her memoir, it dawned upon me
Productive struggle is a deep human desire. Look back at your own life, and think of the events that have defined you and shaped you as a person. Many of them will be stories of overcoming, challenges within and outside, making steady progress until one day you became the person you are.
And this struggle, this friction is not just internal, it shapes everything at a geological scale.
Our planet’s most extreme experiment you didn’t know about
In 2005, Dreamworks animation released a film that follows the adventures of four anthropomorphic Central Park Zoo animals who have spent their lives in blissful captivity and are unexpectedly shipped back to Kenya initially but are shipwrecked on an uninhabited Madagascar.
Yes, that Madagascar, which went onto become a successful franchise, spinning off into television specials, plays, musicals and video games, grossing nearly $2Bn in revenues, over a 10 year period.
But that number is far less impressive compared to what actually happened on Madagascar, or shall I say, still happening in Madagascar, just at a different time scale.
170 Million years.
A story of how friction created an island of extreme endemism - 82% of its native vascular plants and 90% of native terrestrial vertebrates are endemic - Found nowhere else in the world, except on this island of 600,000 sq kms.
Sit back and buckle up as we go through this 170 million year history in just 10 mins.
Around 182 Mn years ago, the massive supercontinent, Gondwana (remnants of which make up around two-thirds of today's continental area, including South America, Africa, Antarctica, Australia, Arabia, and the Indian subcontinent) started to fracture.
When a continent splits into two, geologists refer to it as rifting. Heat from deep inside the Earth pushes up on a piece of crust, the crust cracks along weak lines, and the two halves drift apart. The Atlantic Ocean was born this way; the Red Sea is rifting open right now.
When two pieces of crust pull apart, the edges crack and one side gets lifted while the other sinks. The lifted edge becomes a cliff — an escarpment.
With this process in place, Madagascar started peeling away from Africa, and the western edge of Madagascar was a sharp, fresh cliff facing the new ocean (the Mozambique Channel).⁶
Then, 80 million years ago after Madagascar broke from Africa, things started to break again. This time though, India broke off and drifted northeast. The eastern edge of Madagascar became a brand-new sharp cliff facing the Indian Ocean. This is the eastern escarpment. ⁷
And naturally, each time two pieces of crust fall apart, one side gets lifted, and this time the other edge of Madagascar lifted.
Two extreme events of friction over millions of years led to something dramatic.
When Madagascar first broke off, it tilted east ( and the rivers flowed east). After India broke off, it tilted west — and rivers had to find new paths.
This dual rifting of the Madagascar changed the landscape of the place.
The black solid line on the east is the modern escarpment, steep in nature, and created by the tilting when India broke off. The dashed line in the west is the older escarpment, which first tilted up when Madagascar broke away, but then eventually tilted down. It was once a sharp cliff, but it’s been faded by 140+ million years of erosion, and are now rolling hills.
The eastern escarpment intercepted moist trade winds. Air rose up the cliff, cooled, and dumped rain, which led to the creation of humid rainforest. The Central Highland sat in the rain shadow of the eastern escarpment, and by the time the air reached inland, it had dried out, thereby delivering less rain, making the it a mostly grassland mixed with woodland. Moving eastward, we see the dry deciduous forests, because the plateau blocked moisture coming from the east. The south ended up being a spiny thicket, receiving the least amount of rainfall.
And all of this arises owing to three kinds of friction
Geological friction (rock against rock): Along the eastern escarpment, the faults are still grinding against each other, and the cliff is still retreating inland today.
Hydrological friction (water against rock): As the escarpment retreats, the rivers flowing down its face cut into the plateau. Imagine a river slowly sawing a notch into a cliff, and the notch creeping uphill year after year. When two rivers' notches meet, one river captures the other. And then, a river that used to flow west suddenly gets rerouted east, or vice versa. The Mananara River did exactly this within the last few million years. Every river capture is a violent, sudden change in which way the water flows.
Ecological friction (populations against barriers): A river is a wall to a frog, a leaf chameleon, or a small lemur. When a river flows east instead of west, or splits in two, or dries up, the animals on each bank are suddenly isolated from each other. Over hundreds of thousands of years, the two isolated groups evolve differently and become separate species.
The evolution of Madagascar is a masterclass in friction, three of them that feed off each other: tectonic grinding creates the cliff, the cliff drives water erosion, water erosion moves rivers, and moving rivers split animal populations.
To see this in action, consider three species most commonly found in the Madagascar - Lemurs, Chameleons and Baobabs
Lemurs cannot swim across big rivers. Madagascar's biggest rivers act like fences between lemur populations. When the second rift flipped the drainage and rivers shifted their courses, lemur populations got cut into smaller and smaller groups, and each group evolved into its own species. There are now over 100 lemur species, including 26 species in the genus Lepilemur alone — many of them separated by single rivers.
The eastern escarpment is a collection of hundreds of microclimates stacked on top of each other. The base is hot and humid, the middle is cool and misty, the top is cold and dry. Tiny Brookesia leaf chameleons (some smaller than a fingernail) live in narrow elevation bands on the rainforest floor. A patch of forest 200 meters higher up the cliff might host a different Brookesia species.
Of the world's eight baobab species, six are endemic to Madagascar's western dry forests. The western lowlands are dry because the eastern escarpment steals all the rain. So the baobabs are, in a sense, a direct biological consequence of the eastern escarpment's rain shadow. Each baobab specializes in a slightly different patch of dry forest — Adansonia grandidieri in the Menabe sands, A. perrieri in the far north, A. suarezensis on coastal limestone.
This stunning biodiversity did not arrive altogether. In the story of geological evolution of Madagascar, we have not yet mapped out the flow of a critical element, even though we have mentioned it all throughout.
Time.
Or more accurately, the time between events when nothing significant seems to happen, periods of quiescence.
At 88 Mn yrs ago, India detached from Madagascar, and for the next 22 Mn yrs, Madagascar had subtropical arid climate - the hot and dry weather was not very conducive to the growth of plants and animals.
To make matters worse, this period ended with the K-Pg extinction.
Imagine a 10–15 km asteroid slamming into Mexico⁸, and that impact released energy equivalent to ~100 million megatons of TNT — billions of times what was released when the US dropped the atomic bomb in Hiroshima. This created tsunamis hundreds of metres high across the Gulf of Mexico, and the shocks were felt globally. The debris created by the impact re-entered the atmosphere and across large parts of the world, and the forests caught fire. The sulfate aerosols and soot from wildfires blocked sunlight, causing an "impact winter" where surface temperatures dropped by ~15–25 °C and photosynthesis collapsed globally.
About 75% of all species disappeared including all land dinosaurs, all ammonites, most plankton and many plants across North America. Every animal more than 25kgs was wiped out. Only the small and ecologically flexible species survived.⁹
Essentially, in a play of three acts, the K-Pg extinction event was Act 1 - The Reset.
Madagascar was nearly devoid of species, isolated by oceans, and most importantly, it was still in hot arid latitudes (It had still not drifted to its current position.)
And then began the second act. Act 2 - A long arid quiet where nothing happened.
At this point Madagascar was at roughly 20–25° S latitude, drifting slowly north with the African plate but still parked in the subtropical arid belt. In the modern world, you will find the hot and dry desert landscapes of the Kalahari, Namib, and Australian Outback in these latitudes
31 million years, nothing much of significance happened.
But slowly, rafts of vegetation, storms, and rare oceanic dispersal events carried the species that would become the bedrock of Madagascar’s biodiversity.
Remember the lemurs? They arrived somewhere between 50 - 60 Mn years ago
Remember the chameleons? They arrived in two wave, first 55 - 60 Mn years ago, and then around 25-30 Mn years ago.
Remember the baobabs? They arrived somewhere between 40 - 50 Mn years ago
And then for a long time, they simply survived. It was a period of incubation, when just being around was progress. Think of it like the middle game of chess - there may only be tactical plays, minor threats and attacks, but they are aimed at moving key pieces and gaining more control of the board.
And then, between 30 - 35 Mn years ago, came an event which defined our current state of the planet - the Eocene–Oligocene transition.
The earth flipped from a warm, largely ice-free "greenhouse" world to a cooler world. The Antarctic ice sheets appeared for the first time in tens of millions of years, and temperatures dropped globally by ~4–5 °C. By then the island had shifted into the trade winds belt, and that gave it sufficient rainfall, which became the starting point of the explosion in speciation in the Madagascar.¹⁰ Much like how in the third act of a play, where the tension and the conflict rises to a crescendo, Madagascar climaxed and became what it is today.
90% of what is Madagascar today is what happened in 10% of its existence.¹¹
What if we imagined everything we aim for, every goal and every skill we wish to learn as a three act play?
Let Act 1 be The Reset, where we throw away stifling identities, limiting beliefs, unfounded fears into the inferno of our transformation. And to reset, we must first remove internal friction, that consists of self-criticism and anxiety. Few things are more damaging to us than our inability to believe in ourselves. We must find what drives us, and what are our enduring values.
Let Act 2 be Long Arid Quiet, where we feel the most amount of external friction, where the environment is not conducive, where just sitting down and doing things feels like a burden we are unwilling to bear, where failure is a way of being, and all progress feels incremental. Instead of finding the handy distraction, instead of taking the easy way out and surrendering to the dopamine hit, what if we just went with flow? What if we instead of using self love as an excuse to avoid the hard things, we chose self discipline?
Because for far too long, self discipline has been seen as punishment. In the race to optimize the self, we see self discipline as the pain we need to endure to become a better version of ourselves. We need to see self discipline as a form of self respect. A gentler form, where softness is not at odds with strength. It is a refined, steady and sustainable form of discipline.
And to make this possible, we must return to the common idea, birthed by two of the worlds greatest civilizations - The Chinese and the Indus Valley Civilization.
In the Taoist tradition emerging from China, comes the idea of Wu Wei (無為) - Acting without unnecessary friction. It is almost paradoxical that by doing nothing unnatural, nothing is left undone. Now this may just sound like a philosophical way to say - just relax or worse, become lazy. Instead, it means being at peace while engaged in the most frenetic tasks so that one can carry these out with maximum skill and efficiency. In Taoism, they describe it as the way water flows effortlessly around the barriers it encounters, and how it ends up taking its natural path.
In the Bhakti tradition emerging from India, popularized by the philosopher poet Kabir comes the idea of Sahaj (सहज) - innate, natural, effortless — and denotes a state of inner balance and spontaneous being. It departs from the idea of being still, and describes this state as a form of an intoxication, an inner music to which the self sways and dances.
At the core of both Wu Wei (無為) and Sahaj (सहज), is the idea that the self cannot be the self, if we keep fighting it.
And if you think this is a monastic approach to life, allow me to suggest a few ideas that may help you get closer to such a self. Ask yourself these two questions
Where do I constantly feel friction?
Instead of trying to fix something in your life, find that one recurring moment where you feel that you are forcing yourself. Notice how you are feeling, observe what you are thinking and note it down.
Keep noting it down and you will see patterns emerge.
What is that one thing I can do with my full attention?
What can I do where I am only doing that thing - not thinking and not analyzing? What is that thing where my self-concious self withers away?
The answers to these questions will eventually help us move into Act 3 - The Ignition. But ignition not in the way of unbridled lust for acquiring things and experiences and over optimizing the self, but the ignition as a way to understand what is it that you are willing to be at peace with, even with friction. What are those ways of living and being, despite their initial friction, you find yourself getting lost in? Where the friction in fact becomes the productive struggle that shapes you? Where the friction creates impressions on your self that remain with you much longer than any dopamine riddled activity can? Where it can become part of your story and internal narrative?
So indulge me, dear reader, in an experiment, in the simple act of creating a piece of Origami.
What you see here is a Kusudama. The word kusudama comes from two Japanese words: kusu (medicine) and dama (ball). While traditional origami tends to emphasize single-sheet folds without cuts or glue, kusudama embraces modular design. Each petal is folded from its own sheet, then combined with others into a flower, and finally into a sphere.
But the earliest kusudama weren’t paper at all. During the Heian period (794–1185), people sewed small cloth pouches filled with herbs or incense into round bundles. As paper folding grew in popularity, the practice evolved. Instead of cloth, people began crafting flower-like units from folded paper. These were then joined - by glue, thread, or, in some cases, intricate interlocking folds, often into spherical forms.¹²
But why make them?
Kusudama’s are often hung in a recovering patient’s room. These are received as gifts, with each part of the kusudama being created by several people and then put together. For a person who fell ill, it is perceived as a symbol of a wish for a speedy recovery - a sign of general attention and care.
What if you thought of Act 3: The ignition as a kusudama? Because if we do, we’d understand the following
Friction creates form: Every fold relies on friction between paper layers to hold its shape. Without it, creased paper would spring back to flat. A flat square has infinite possible shapes, but each time we fold, we eliminate possibilities and thus give it specific form.
Friction creates memory: An origami once folded remembers its folds, and will remain creased. The actions, experiences, feelings and emotions from sustained periods of friction create a memory. It imprint on us and give us a personal memory and narrative that we go back to, acting as a glue for our identity.
We need to stop seeing friction as a restrictive force, and start seeing it as a generative one. This understanding will help us realize that friction is foundational to three aspects of the human experience: form, meaning and hope.
Form: If we are to define ourselves as the collection of our thoughts, ideas, principles and knowledge, we must recognize that they cannot exist in the absence of epistemic friction - our ideas and theories must face resistance from reality. We often mistake frictionless / fluid ideas and experiences as freedom, but freedom in itself is not divorced from friction. Without freedom, inquiry cannot happen, without friction, ideas are empty.
Friction shapes our ideas of the self, because the self only exists in its relationship with the world. The nature of the human condition is to be at odds with the world, and only if we have the freedom of inquiry, can we use friction to shape ourselves.
Meaning: In Viktor Frankl’s timeless book, [Man’s search for meaning](https://substack.com/redirect/cc4b5f28-1387-4580-8081-d578cc13596d?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o), he identifies three things from which we derive meaning
Work or creation: Doing a deed, completing a task, or creating a work of art - In this very piece, I used charcoal as a medium for the first time, and immediately felt frustrated. There is extreme messiness, difficulty in erasing dark marks, and it has a tendency to smudge. But only by engaging with it, by embracing the discomfort will I ever be able to get better at it. I went through the same journey with fountain pens and watercolours, and I now find great meaning and joy in engaging with these mediums. I could have always stuck to writing, since that came easily to me, but only by having the freedom to find newer mediums to express myself, did I have the ability to deal with the friction of learning it.
Experiencing or love: Experiencing something fully or caring for another human being - Our ability to love another human being is foundational to the continuation of civilization. But all love comes with suffering, with friction, as our desire for self care clashes with our object of love - Each parent who loves their child has had moments of frustration, even a continued sense of exhaustion in the journey that bringing up another human being is. Their love gets tested. The same goes for people in committed relationships - I know for a fact that I test my partners patience when I procrastinate on simple tasks, or when we are in disagreement about something we both feel strongly about. Her love for me gets tested.
But its our love and our desire to care for each other, despite these frictions, is the reason why these relationships have meaning.
Attitude towards suffering: Finding dignity and purpose in how one bears unavoidable hardship - And I return to the idea of productive struggle. Earlier this year, with a friend, I kicked off the first cohort of
[Unstuck](https://substack.com/redirect/fdec27f4-9331-4dc9-b898-5cd64b71a402?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o), a program created to help millennials design and action a life on their own terms. Over a period of three months, we worked with an intimate cohort of only nineteen people, and help them discover their idea of a good life, and how to action it. These people were all in their 30s and 40s, are considered accomplished by every metric of success, and yet felt that their lives were misaligned. As we went step by step, helping them understand their disconnections, enduring values and natural interests, each one of them found a path they could walk on, but one filled with months and maybe years of struggle. But the program was their reset. This was their Act 1. Each time they took a small step, that generated a small win, they felt themselves being enveloped in the warm embrace of purpose.But along the way, and even into the future, many of these small steps did / will end up being dead ends, or simply failures. With their values as their guiding compass, they are treating their Act 2 with the dignity and kindness it needs. They now have a narrative about themselves that is true to them, unencumbered by their past identities and the burden of societal expectations. This narrative only gets stronger each time they encounter the unavoidable friction that comes with pivoting your life. And it generates the most important fuel that will propel them into Act 3.
Hope: With all the world continues to throw at us, and the problems we are create for ourselves, we can endure long periods of hardship, of being stuck, only if we can reliably tell ourselves that we can overcome it. Tell me, what is it you plan to do with your one wild and precious life?
