Peer reviewers, by and large, do not spend time actually doing quality control on the papers they read: does the code run? Do the analyses match the preregistration? Are the main results invalidated by information buried in the supplement? It seems helpful to have AI flag these issues, since otherwise they don’t get flagged at all. In AI judges always prefer AI reviews, and human judges almost always prefer AI reviews. And these human reviews are about as good as they get. They come from a service at Harvard that pays experts to review faculty members’ papers before submission and suggest areas for improvement, so this isn’t an overworked volunteer skimming the manuscripts and writing a single sentence back to the journal editor (“paper sux do not publessh”). If these results both hold up and generalize, it might seem like the conclusion is obvious: we should replace human reviewers with AI. Unfortunately, that wouldn’t solve most of the fundamental problems with universal pre-publication peer review, like how it prevents interesting research from being done in the first place, or how it fools people into thinking that someone else can do their homework for them. Still, it would have a few undeniable advantages, like saving humans a lot of time, and making it clear to everyone involved that this system is so silly that no one should take it that seriously in the first place.
