RAT RACEIf You Give a Mouse a Cookie is an allegory about a nameless boy who unwittingly enslaves himself to the rapacious whims of a rodent. Once he gives the titular mouse the titular cookie, the mouse demands milk to wash the cookie down, then a straw to drink the milk, then a napkin to wipe its milk mustache, and so on. After several pages of these hijinks, the mouse becomes thirsty, drinks a second glass of milk, and then becomes desirous for another cookie, beginning the cycle anew and making it clear that the boy has damned himself to eternal torment.² The genius of the book is that each of the mouse’s desires are impossible to predict in advance, because they are conditional on the previous desire being satisfied. The mouse only wants the straw once it has the milk; after all, a straw is useless to a milk-less mouse. Thus, the boy can be lulled into believing that each demand will be the last, when in fact the mouse’s appetites are inexhaustible. As it is with Numeroff’s mouse, so it is with humans. We think there is a finite list of jobs to be done because we cannot imagine the new desires we’ll discover once our current desires are sated. A hundred years ago, a particularly skilled futurist might have predicted that we would one day have air traffic controllers, computer programmers, and radio DJs. But who could have predicted that, after we invented all those jobs, we would go on to invent jobs like We chuckle at these professions because they seem to indicate that our economy has finally reached it decadence, as if we’ve filled all the necessary jobs, and now all that’s left are goofy, made-up jobs. But the fact is all jobs are unnecessary. In the strictest sense, we don’t need financial advisors, IT guys, kindergarten teachers, or any other professions. Humans survived just fine for several hundred thousand years doing only two jobs, namely, “hunter” and “gatherer”. The fact that our modern luxuries feel like bare necessities is proof of our limitless capacity to upgrade our nice-to-haves into must-haves. This is why the labor-lump doesn’t shrink, and why the lump of labor fallacy would perhaps be better called the finite desires fallacy. We have a hard time understanding the infinitude of our appetites because our lives depend on us not understanding it. The best way to get someone to do something is to exaggerate the reward for doing it, which is why our motivational systems lie to us all the time: “All you need is [THAT 3% RAISE/TO LOSE 15 POUNDS/THE ADORATION OF ALL YOUR PEERS] and then you’ll be happy for good!” If our imaginations admitted to us that there’s no such thing as “happy for good”, then we might not ever get off our butts at all.³ This is the phenomenon that psychologists call the “ BLOB THE BUILDERThe fear with AI, of course, is that our infinite desires will be no match for infinite intelligence. The standard explanation is that humans are hopeless optimists, and this trait somehow gave us an evolutionary advantage. But we are perfectly able to imagine unprecedented disasters; just check the WHAT’S UP DOG-MANMost human apparatuses are easy to explain from an evolutionary perspective. Skin makes sense. Eyes make sense. Stomachs, teeth, kidneys, language, memory—no brainers, all. But why did natural selection endow us with a mind’s eye that can picture, say, a pig in flight? If our brains are going to have a speculator module, shouldn’t it try to stick to the facts, or at least to the laws of physics? I suspect that it’s a boon to be able to imagine impossible things, because we don’t yet know which things are possible. The future is not just a rerun or a remix of the past—sometimes crazy things really do happen, and if we can’t picture them, we can’t prepare for them. The downside of having a built-in fantasy machine is that it makes all sorts of nonsense seem plausible: witches, poltergeists, chupacabras, being friends with benefits without anyone getting hurt, etc. The mere fact that we’re able to conjure up these things in our mental holodeck makes us feel like they could be real, even if they have no chance of happening. The more we zoom out, both in time and space, the less our imaginations are subject to the limits of reality. For example, Pliny the Elder’s Natural History—the go-to reference work in the Western world for 1,500 years— We do have one advantage over Pliny and his contemporaries: we’ve reached the point in our game of Civilization where we’ve cleared all the fog from the board, and so we can be certain that there is no race of dog-men out there waiting to be discovered. But there is no clearing the fog from the future. If you go far enough forward in time, who knows what you might find! What radical transformations might be around the corner, what fantasies are poised to become realities? Not right now, of course. But soon. These headlines exist side-by-side with reports of events that have actually occurred, as if there is no difference between the near future and the recent past. To a human, apparently, there isn’t. We are especially lost in the future when it comes to AI. I am grateful to the professionals who try to anticipate and prevent all the ways that AI could harm us. The the rest of us, however, don’t even experience the events anymore. We merely use them to fret about the next events, as if, with enough next-ing, we can prepare ourselves for the future before it arrives. But look around—the future has already arrived.
