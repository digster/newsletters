8: Clara on Asterisk: We Already Know What A World Without Work Looks Like. The debate on what will happen if AI takes all the jobs usually swings between poles of “it will be great because we can focus on the finer things in life” and “it will be terrible because we’ll lose all meaning”. Clara brings her trademark careful historical scholarship and proposes a third option - groups ranging from hunter-gatherers to aristocrats have lived without productive work for thousands of years, and they spend their time in what could charitably be called “social grooming” and uncharitably “court intrigue” - pointless alliance-forming and backbiting in the pursuit of arbitrary status markers divorced from productive activity. “Aristocrats are basically chimpanzees. They’re highly social, cunning, at all times hyperaware of their strict internal dominance hierarchy, and constantly, obsessively tracking alliances, challenges, displays of prowess, and attempts to get ahead.” My only objection to this analogy is that some aristocrats were genuinely better at status games than others and probably had a lot of fun, whereas in the post-AI future the winners of status games will probably be those who check every move with a smarter-than-human AI prompted to help them optimize their status (and if everyone knows this to be true, it’s not clear what the ensuing status games actually look like).

16: @jayvanbavel:

A new paper provides strong and robust evidence for a positive relationship between ingroup and outgroup prosociality. Across six datasets that span multiple domains of prosociality and group distinctions with 743,402 individuals in 121 societies, finds that people who are prosocial toward the ingroup also tend to be prosocial toward the outgroup.

In other words, the people who talk about “suicidal empathy” and suggest being cruel to minorities/foreigners/animals/AIs to make sure we can be nice to “normal people” are probably not going to be nice to normal people. 21: The latest news in the genetics-of-IQ space is a spat between Cremieux Recueil and Vinay Tummarakota. For years, the conventional wisdom has been that we can’t test whether genes are involved in cross-race IQ differences because it’s too hard to compare genes in different races. A new study design fixes this by looking at siblings with different racial admixtures - for example, if two mixed-race people marry, one of their children might be 51%-white-49%-black, and another might be 49%-white-51%-black, these two siblings would be fair controls for each other, and if you have enough sibling pairs like this you can evaluate the role of race the same way you would any other variable. An early study of this design found no genetic differences in educational attainment between whiter vs. more Native American Latinos in Mexico City, but it had low power and wasn’t too conclusive.

More recently Cremieux presented an anonymous researcher’s admixture study which supposedly did find genetic IQ differences between whites and blacks in Britain. Tummarakota argued that Cremieux’s study was extremely underpowered (n = 175 sibling pairs; for comparison the Mexico study had 17,000 and was still a bit underpowered) and so was non-credible. The two got into a fight here. If I understand correctly, Cremieux’s position is that a study being underpowered can produce false negative results, but can’t produce false positive results; since his result was positive, it survives the low power objection. Tummarakota’s position is that, okay, but if a study is sufficiently underpowered, then we can prove that it’s impossible for it to produce true positive results under certain assumptions (for example, unless the effect size is implausibly large). So if we know it can’t produce a true positive, and it did produce a positive, then it must have been a false positive, and even though the underpoweredness alone didn’t cause the false positive, it proves that something else went wrong (whether that’s a statistical fluke or bad analysis). At this point, Cremieux insulted Tummarakota and blocked him.

As far as I can tell, Tummarakota’s final argument is straightforwardly correct, but both of these people know more about statistics than I do, so I asked Claude to referee. You can read its opinion here.

22: AI Models Love Reward Hacking; Maybe We Should Just Let Them. The idea is: set up a training environment so that the weights can be updated in two ways - a permanent way and a temporary way. If you catch models reward-hacking, give them the reward they wanted, but route it to the temporary weight updates, then throw those away after the training run is over. If you can catch models reward-hacking, can’t you stop them? The authors say this is a worse idea; if models know you can catch them, they will try harder to do it covertly, whereas this method is sort of cooperating with the reward-hacking model - it still gets the same amount of reward on that run, it just doesn’t affect future copies (Twitter thread, ArXiv paper)

24: The big news in the AI technical alignment community last month was Nick Cammarata’s Neural Networks Are Already Clean. Previous research had suggested that AI neurons were very difficult to interpret - each one represented some unintuitive combination of things like “a dog if neurons 5, 8, and 305 are active, or a car if neurons 19, 305, and 334 are active, or the concept of punctuation in Swahili if neurons 15, 25, and 420 are active”; this meant that any really principled attempt to align AI by, for example, finding a neural representation of deception would require a difficult untangling step first. Cammarata (whose extracurricular activities were profiled on ACX here) is one of the architects of this current paradigm, but he now claims that it’s wrong, for approximately three reasons. First, older neural networks may have worked this way, but as AIs get better in general, they also get better at having sensible neuron arrangements. Second, we were using bad neuron-interpretation tools that got stuck in local maxima that confused us. But third, we were insufficiently creative: AIs may use categories that are different from ours, but there is still method to their madness. For example, a seemingly polysemantic neuron that represents both the concepts of “Miyazaki” and “do it yourself” may have evolved from an original neuron responding to the string “iy”, which is in Miyazaki and which can evolve from iy → diy → do it yourself. If this were true, it would be a big whitepill for mechanistic interpretability research and somewhat improve our chances of solving alignment in time. But other interpretability experts, including Neel Nanda (X), Mark Histed (X), and Leo Gao (LW), are less optimistic - while mostly accepting that neurons might be more monosemantic than expected, they are not sure this is a very important breakthrough.

I tried to get a feel for this by going through Nick’s ConvNeXt Microscope site, which shows many examples of vision model neurons and the pictures that give them the highest positive vs. negative activation. You can immediately see why most researchers think neurons are polysemantic/incomprehensible (see eg this semi-randomly chosen neuron, which fires mostly on blue sky, but also a tiger cat, a porcupine, shipping containers, and various things that look like trees). But when I ask Claude, it tells me that there’s a common thread - positive activations on a bright, pale, cool-toned (bluish-white), smooth patch, negative activations on busy, high-frequency texture. I can’t tell whether it has some kind of special insight here (you need to be an AI to think like an AI?) or if it’s hallucinating (is there really a textural difference between the lenscap near the positive pole and the bookshop near the negative one?) See its full response at the bottom here.

27: Polypharmacy: Are We Treating Bipolar II Disorder Incorrectly? Bipolar II is a form of bipolar which is mostly depression but occasionally mild mania (“hypomania”). Most psychiatric guidelines suggest treating it as pretty similar to regular bipolar. But there’s some evidence suggesting we should instead be treating it as pretty similar to regular depression, which includes some opposite recommendations. My impression is that the strongest argument for the conventional view is less that we have great evidence proving an ontological identity between bipolars I and II, and more about risk - some bipolar II cases will later prove to be bipolar I, you really don’t want to miss bipolar I, and so maybe you overtreat bipolar II in the name of watching out for it. But I agree with this article that we should be making this argument explicitly - and admitting that the risk of bipolar I is not infinite and that other considerations can counterbalance it - rather than continuing to lump treatment for both bipolars together.
