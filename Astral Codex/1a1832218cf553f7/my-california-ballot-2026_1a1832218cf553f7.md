Proposition 5: Changes the California recall election processA recall election is like an impeachment by the voters. If enough voters sign a petition, there’s a special election where we vote to get rid of an official (most often the governor). This special election also includes a list of candidates for who replaces him if he does get recalled. Proposition 5 would keep the recall vote, but instead of a simultaneous vote to replace the official, the official would be replaced according to normal procedure: a recalled governor would be replaced by the lieutenant governor, other major officials would be picked by the governor, and legislators would be replaced in a special election. The current recall system tends toward the following annoying equilibrium: every election, California picks a mildly corrupt pro-dysfunctional-establishment Democrat; a year later, the Republicans object that the candidate is mildly corrupt and in bed with the dysfunctional establishment, and gather enough signatures to hold a recall election; a year after that, the election happens, everyone spends millions of dollars sending billions of spam email messages, and then it fails, because the electorate just got done electing the governor and hasn’t magically changed its mind. This proposition might break the cycle: if the Republicans know with certainty that the mildly-corrupt-pro-establishment Democratic governor will just be replaced by the mildly-corrupt-pro-establishment Democratic lieutenant governor, maybe they’ll stop trying this. And it preserves the useful part of the recall system: if the governor ever crosses the line into being so crooked that people would genuinely prefer the mildly-corrupt-pro-establishment Democratic lieutenant governor, we can still recall him. Proposition 41: Bans state taxes that ignore the Gann limitVoters like new taxes on billionaires, but hate new taxes on themselves. So a group of billionaires unhappy about the Proposition 40 billionaire wealth tax above figured they could write a proposition that sounds like it’s banning taxes in general - maybe even some especially dangerous and irresponsible form of taxes - but is mostly just banning Proposition 40. You don’t need to know what the Gann limit is except that Proposition 40 ignores it, and ignoring it sounds bad. According to California law, if two mutually contradictory propositions pass during the same election cycle, whichever gets the most votes wins out. So if the voters who think that “taxes which ignore the Gann limit” sounds sinister outnumber the voters who think that “let’s seize the billionaires’ money” sounds fun, the wealth tax fails. Hooray for democracy! I’m against the wealth tax, but this is kind of scummy. I weakly vote NO. Local Ballot MeasuresMeasure RTM: Raises sales taxes to fund BARTThis imposes an additional 0.5% sales tax to fund transit - especially Bay Area Rapid Transit, the local commuter rail system. During the pandemic, people stopped using mass transit (maybe because they switched to work-from-home) and ridership numbers have never bounced back. The federal government gave BART some emergency pandemic funding for a while, but now that’s running out, and they say they either need to raise taxes or drastically reduce services. When investigating this, I learned that fares cover only 10 - 25% of BART operating costs (depending on how you define it); the remaining 75 - 90% are government subsidies. I’m not against the government helping some poor people afford commuter rail, or even subsidizing tickets a few percent to help close a small funding gap. But if the government has to pay 75% of the cost, this suggests commuter rail just isn’t a competitive form of transportation, and we should stop throwing more money at it. On the other hand, supporters argue that if we let the BART fail, then traffic on local roads would get much worse - for example, a trip across the Bay Bridge, already unpleasant, might take twice as long. I asked the AI superforecaster to check this, and it guessed about 50% longer - from 23 to 34 minutes. You could build this into a reasonable case for BART subsidies. For systemic reasons, the Bay Area doesn’t have enough roads for all the people who want to drive. Therefore, we should get some people to ride commuter rail instead, as this produces a positive externality for everyone else. But people don’t want to do this at market price, because it’s a terrible experience. Therefore, we should keep increasing the subsidies until enough people agree to take one for the team and ride the train that the roads are clear for the rest of us. Our first priority should be addressing the reasons that the BART is a terrible experience. These include people playing loud music on the train, people stealing stuff on the train, the stations being full of trash/crime/loud music/the smell of drugs, and a lack of parking near the stations. The government has been taking some halting steps toward improving some of these things recently, especially with the new fare gates. More to the point, I don’t expect that voting down this measure will make any of these improvements happen faster, so we’ll just get a collapse of the BART system and longer commutes. I weakly vote YES. Oakland Measure EE: Switches the city to a “strong mayor” systemUS city governments can be divided into two types. The first type, “council-manager” systems, are like a corporation - the elected city council, playing the role of the board of directors, hires an unelected CEO-like city manager to make most day-to-day decisions. The other, “strong mayor” systems, are like a traditional democratic government; the elected mayor is the chief executive and makes most day-to-day decisions. Oakland used to be a council-manager system. In 1998, voters approved a measure that shifted it halfway to a strong mayor system, leaving it in an awkward limbo that satisfied nobody. This measure would move it all the way to a strong mayor system. In favor of this, a recent 750-person group which was convened to fix the city’s charter recommended this measure unanimously. On the other hand, that group was convened by the mayor, so maybe there was a conflict of interest; on the other-other hand, some nonpartisan groups assisted with the group and concur. My city council member, Zac Unger, who I like, opposes this measure, but, again, conflict of interest. Supporters say that it makes it clear who is “accountable” for the city’s failures. Opponents say that Oakland mayors are often terrible (for example, the last elected mayor was raided by the FBI, recalled by voters, and is currently on trial for bribery), and making them all-powerful just makes things worse. There’s some research on the advantages of strong mayor vs. council-manager cities, but none of it applies to Oakland, which is currently an unusual hybrid. I asked the AI superforecaster to estimate the size of Oakland’s budget deficit (as a proxy for good government) if this measure passed vs. failed; it said the deficit would be 3% lower if it passed. I don’t have much to go on, and will work off a prior that keeping things in limbo is bad, a hope that the 750-person group knows more than I do, and the AI superforecaster results. I weakly vote YES. Oakland Measure FF: Removes a tax exemption for some foreclosuresOakland has a big budget deficit, but voters hate taxes, so the city is constantly searching for weird taxes that will raise money without affecting any normal person or legible special interest. Measure FF taxes property transfers via foreclosure the same as every other property transfer. Originally, this was taxed less, because the theory was that a foreclosure was just a bank taking back a house which it technically owned anyway, after its plan to loan that house to a mortgage-holder had failed. Supporters of this policy say that nowadays, banks usually sell the right to foreclose on houses to big institutional investors, so this is more like a normal property transfer. If a small bank is really foreclosing on a house it really owns, then the exemption will stay in effect. I feel like my city officials are really fulfilling the mandate that I and fellow voters have given them of instituting taxes which are so arcane that I can’t understand them well enough to object.
