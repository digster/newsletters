Liam previously led post-training at OpenAI, where he was one of the creators of ChatGPT. Dogus spent years at Google DeepMind leading a large team of chemists and materials scientists. Together, they’ve brought those two trajectories to bear on a problem that has stymied science for decades: not just discovering new materials, but making them reliably and at scale. In this conversation, we explore the limits of language models, the commercialization bottleneck in materials science, why Periodic chose high-temperature superconductors as its first target, and what Liam learned from watching ChatGPT go from an internal product to one of the most successful products of all time. From my notebookWe’re trying a new thing where I share a few of my thoughts from after the episodes. The moments that struck me, the tidbits that reframed my thinking, or something I just really enjoyed. Let me know if you like reading these! To do science you have to do science. This is Periodic’s conviction in a nutshell. Science is the discovery of new things; you cannot prove it in a corpus of existing information. It has to involve trial and error in the real world. Sci-fi remains an endless source of alpha. Why did Periodic set an initial goal to discover a new high-temperature superconductor? In part because ~every sci-fi future features it. (It also doesn’t hurt that it’s stayed top of mind in condensed matter physics.) I underestimated the gap between discovery and utility. It can take 10-20 years for a new material to get to industrial scale. This feels like an equally important place for AI. We don’t want a century of discoveries stuck in twenty year manufacturing bottleneck. Virality is ~impossible to predict. With the benefit of hindsight, it’s insane to hear Liam say that people inside OpenAI expected ChatGPT to attract tens of thousands of users. Perhaps the closer you are to something, the harder to know how useful it will be. Capability ≠ virality. Another interesting wrinkle on the ChatGPT story. Liam said that they had a stronger internal model that hadn’t gone viral internally. ChatGPT used a weaker one! Towards fuller observability of science. When humans conduct experiments, they write up the parts they believe matter. The ‘useless’ data you generate at every other step is discarded. This feels like one of the coolest parts of Periodic’s approach. In theory you can capture the full experimental trace. Little things are big things. A bunch of samples get mislabeled in a lab. Usually, that would take a bunch of irritating human time to unwind. At Periodic’s lab, AI found it and fixed it. “Contamination-free” RL is sort of an experimental veil of ignorance? Liam talked through one of Periodic’s advantages, which is constructing “snapshots” of experimental states. They can then ask: “What should we have done next?” This is possible because they have a corpus of fresh, untainted data. As a result, the model hasn’t inherited the right answer.
