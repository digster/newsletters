I’ve long said that good augmented-reality glasses could be very cool. I actually think a lot of the horrors of the modern world are caused by the one-app-per-screen experience of the smartphone and bringing multiple windows — and context — to mobile computing would be a net positive. I would happily buy a pair of glasses that both extended and opened up my smartphone’s screen and meant I no longer had to use it as a computer in public. Open a bunch of windows while I’m sitting on a train, use built-in cameras to orient the augmented reality features, make all of it end-to-end encrypted, and maybe even block the ability to use those cameras as actual cameras. |
And if a user were to use those cameras as cameras, there’s certainly no reason to not have a big unavoidable flashing light letting people know. Meta Ray-Bans have an LED that flashes when the camera is in use, but there are all kinds of complaints that it’s not immediately visible to those not wearing the glasses. Meta Ray-Bans are not trying to solve real problems with mobile computing. They want you to think they are, of course, but the rotten core of the whole project means they can’t actually admit what they’re doing. They need the glasses because Meta’s attempts at making phones and operating systems have been a flop. They dumped their AI into the glasses because they need a way to sell their AI.
