“I could’ve killed you,” I say, but he doesn’t seem to notice. 
He is eating protein chips. I told him to get a snack at the gas station — I waited ten minutes and watched squirrels grappling with the trash. His fingers are yellow. He has dribbled crumbs all over my floor. Outside, wildfires have unfolded a neat hand of smoke over the hills. I can barely see his gravestones. 
I try again. “Last year, I could’ve killed you. Remember? Not even could’ve. I should’ve, I nearly did. Right there.” I point toward a space between two gas pumps. “I put you there and you weren’t talking, just like you’re not talking now. Then I got back in the car, and I was ready to run you over. It was that time of year. But you’d never been so quiet.” 
He crumples the bag and stuffs it into a cupholder. The noise is unreasonably annoying; I smack his upper arm.
“Are you listening? You were too quiet, so I dragged you along for another full ride around the sun. And now it’s been a year.” I hit him again. “And you’re just sitting there. It’s been a whole year and you still haven’t said a goddamn thing.” 
He rubs his arm. As far as I’ve seen, he doesn’t mind the beatings. 
This time, though, he looks at me and smiles without showing his teeth. Then he opens the door and starts strolling toward the hills. 
I roll down the window. “What the fuck!” I yell. “I wasn’t done!”
He keeps going. He crosses the street, jumps over the guardrail, and disappears into the haze. I hop out and jog after him. I don’t know what happens if I let him get away. 
When I catch up, he’s looking at the most recent gravestone, the one I put up for him last year. It isn’t much to see. I used to try a little harder. The back rows of rocks are adorned with little painted creatures and flowers. That was a long time ago; I just stuck his rock in the ground. 
He coughs into his hands and rubs them together. “I don’t understand,” I say. “This isn’t how it goes.” He ignores me. Crouching down on all fours, he shoves his fingers into the dirt and starts to dig. 
The smoke is torturing my throat. I wonder if he’d follow me back to the gas station, eventually, if I just started walking. 
Instead, I kneel next to him and drag handfuls out of the plot. If I open the grave first, rather than waiting until he’s dead, maybe it won’t be so hard to kill him. 
By the time we finish, it’s dark. I can’t see the car. Two raccoons rush through the grass — they skirt the graveyard completely. 
He stands up and extracts dirt from his fingernails. “Now what?” I ask. 
With his right hand, he gestures toward the open grave, points at me and then himself, and crosses his hands over his chest.
“I’m not going in there,” I say. 
He shrugs and lowers himself into the pit. He scoots to his left and pats the space beside him. 
“No,” I say again. “I’ll just bury you. You did it yourself this time. I’ll just shove the dirt right back where it belongs.” 
He laces his hands over his stomach and smiles.
“I — okay,” I say. “I don’t know. Okay.”  
I lie down next to him. 
Hours pass. The smoke untangles itself from the hills, and it is suddenly very cold. I start to shiver. He rubs my shoulder, leans out of the grave and drags a pile of dirt over our bodies. 
“Thanks,” I say. “That helped.” 
I feel him swallow. He croaks gently, stops, then tries again. “You can go, and I’ll stay,” he says. “Or I can go instead.”  
It should be easy. I don’t want to die. 
“Can we stay here a bit longer?” I ask. 
“Okay,” he says. 
We stay.
