I: From Keju to the Taiping Heavenly Kingdom
In early June, nearly 13 million graduating Chinese high schoolers will compete against their neighbors and friends in a high-stakes exam that will, without exaggeration, largely determine the trajectory of their lives. In a country defined by corruptible institutions, where the rich often pull strings to get ahead, this test—the Gaokao, or “Higher Exam”—is China’s bastion of alleged meritocracy.
As an excellent new book [ https://substack.com/redirect/defcbe65-bc16-4b0b-bb83-516074d8a99f?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] on the test explains: “For two days every year in June, China comes to a standstill. Unusual sights abound: men put on qipaos, traditional dresses typically worn by women, as a symbol of luck; police stand on street corners, silencing drivers to ensure minimal disturbance; temples overflow with relatives sending out final prayers; crowds, unusually quiet, gather in hushed vigils outside thousands of schools.”
The best scoring students may even become celebrities.
In principle, everyone has an equal shot at scoring well. In practice, the Gaokao skews toward rewarding wealthier, urbanized students. The richest 20% of Chinese students are 2.3 times more likely [ https://substack.com/redirect/b733f3fc-d078-41ab-84ea-410efed3ad3b?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] to go to elite universities than the poorest 20%. Egregiously unequal, sure, but in the United States, the richest 20% of students are 11 times [ https://substack.com/redirect/defcbe65-bc16-4b0b-bb83-516074d8a99f?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] more likely to go to elite universities than the poorest 20%.
But unlike American students, who will apply for college with standardized tests and GPAs alongside fawning letters of recommendation, sports accolades, volunteering prowess, and polished personal statements, every Chinese student is reduced to just a single number: their Gaokao score, out of a possible 750 points.
This test also provides a potential insight into a baffling puzzle. China is a collectivistic state that places the value of the nation above the value of individuals and is a society where corruption and connections often lead to preferential treatment. Why, then, do Chinese people overwhelmingly believe—far more than Americans—that anyone who succeeds deserved it and anyone who fails only has themselves to blame?
It is impossible to understand modern China without understanding the Gaokao—and how it fits into the long, consequential history of Chinese exams.
For nearly 1,500 years, almost without interruption, China has used a single exam to select its elites. The notion that meritocratic knowledge, not familial bloodlines, should confer status would have been totally alien to China’s European counterparts during the Early Middle Ages. In Europe, God chose kings and elevated the inherited nobility who surrounded them; in China, emperors ruled under the Mandate of Heaven, but staffed their bureaucracies with whomever could pass a nearly impossible test.
Starting during the Sui Dynasty (581-618), China’s Imperial Examination system, or keju, became a pillar of Chinese governance and society, serving as an aspirational pipeline from obscurity and poverty to power and wealth.
The examination, however, was one of rote learning, not creative innovation or technological advancement. Those taking the keju could be expected to accurately regurgitate up to 400,000 characters from the emperor’s chosen texts. The task was daunting and could take decades of study—time that could have been spent on more intellectually productive learning. Vast swaths of the Chinese population (or at least those already rich enough to devote themselves to endless study rather than productive work) toiled with memorization and ink, some succeeding only in the twilight of their lives.
The overwhelming majority of test takers, though, would fail. In the Qing Dynasty (1644 - 1911), the passing rate [ https://substack.com/redirect/81acf926-1046-4869-8544-5ed1644c3c35?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] was a measly 0.27% — meaning that only one out of every 370 test takers would succeed. To obtain the highest qualification, the jinshi, the numbers were even bleaker, estimated at various periods with a success rate of one out of every three thousand.
This system was therefore a double-edged sword for political stability. On the one hand, this proto-meritocratic system [ https://substack.com/redirect/0cd822dd-5d30-4826-a256-12465b748489?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] acted as a pressure valve for those who aspired to obtain a better social station. Passing a test produced genuine prosperity—and tantalized millions more with the prospect of joining the elite.
By contrast, in more established European dynastic kingdoms, one could never change a bloodline. There was therefore limited hope for an ambitious merchant to become nobility, which could produce greater resentment from the have-nots or sub-elite. In China, however, some previous nobodies would radically change their status merely through a test. Nearly half of those who passed the provincial-level exam were first-generation successes [ https://substack.com/redirect/e22104da-75ad-4add-a705-91b8caa927c8?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ], with nobody else in their ancestry having previously obtained that honour.
The system was thus cleverly designed to ensure fierce loyalty for those who became elite by examination. The keju system had conferred them with wealth and power beyond their imagination, so they had a vested interest in fighting to defend that system from any potential upheaval. Any attempt to usurp formal power or challenge the status quo came with extreme risks for the selected few. And so, century after century, China’s powerful bureaucrats defended the value of elites being chosen through memorization and deferred to the emperor to whom they owed their newfound prosperity.
China, across millennia, had therefore engineered a surefire way to guarantee a fawning, devoted elite bureaucracy while also defusing social tension because of the promise that anyone could become powerful—if only they studied hard enough. The resulting elite was filled with ranks of obedient, deferential men with a knack for rote recall. (Some studies have suggested that China’s risk of regime collapse was lower during the imperial period compared to European counterparts for precisely this reason; why bite the hand that writes the exam?)
On the other hand, plenty of ambitious figures were shut out from the spoils of the state simply because they couldn’t—or wouldn’t—successfully devote themselves to the solitary toil of memorizing texts. For example, in the 19th century, Hong Xiuquan, a schoolteacher with a childhood knack for memorizing Confucian texts, dreamed of passing the keju.
Over and over, he took the test—and failed. On the final failure, he entered a state of delirium and claimed to have experienced a profound vision, ascending into the heavens, where he met [ https://substack.com/redirect/9bc3f485-b8c9-450f-9421-ef8251adaca2?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] “a very tall man with a long beard and thick belt who had told him to come back to Earth and eradicate the demons on Earth.” As Jonathan Fenby explains [ https://substack.com/redirect/9bc3f485-b8c9-450f-9421-ef8251adaca2?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ], that vision merged with Christianity when a missionary provided Hong with a copy of the New Testament:
“He decided on reading that, that the man he had seen up in the sky was the Christian God; that he, Hong, was the brother of Jesus; and that the devils he had to exterminate on Earth were the Qing dynasty.”
Hong launched a rebellion, promising a utopian replacement to the existing power structure of the Qing, which he called the Taiping Heavenly Kingdom. Soon, Hong’s armies controlled land home to roughly 30 million people, complete with a cult of personality known, charmingly, as the “Supreme Emperor Worshipping Society.” And yet, even with all this fresh power, Hong maintained exams, albeit ones that incorporated Biblical texts and stories from Chinese folk religion rather than Confucius. Even when smashing the old system, driven by his failed exams, Hong merely shuffled the curriculum.
Lest you think this was some merely esoteric historical footnote, the Taiping Rebellion and its aftermath wiped out between five and ten percent of China’s population, with the death toll [ https://substack.com/redirect/056e1587-c842-4c42-ad8b-675665a55d39?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] most likely between 20 and 30 million people.
Today, the Gaokao hasn’t led to mass death. Instead, it provides a crucial window not only into how modern China works, but also how the kinds of exams a society creates can dictate a nation’s history—and its future to come.
