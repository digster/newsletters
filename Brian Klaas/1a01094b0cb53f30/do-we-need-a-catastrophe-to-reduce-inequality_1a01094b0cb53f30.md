A decade ago, a book called “[The Great Leveler](https://substack.com/redirect/fa73852c-debd-47a8-878a-3b4af99780f2?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o)” made a provocative claim: that throughout history, grotesque inequality has only fallen rapidly due to devastating catastrophes.
If you want society to become significantly more equal, then wait for one of the Four Horsemen of Leveling to arrive: deadly plagues, bloody revolutions, cataclysmic wars, and total state collapse. They may trigger immense human suffering, but there may be a hidden gift wrapped up in the tragedy and destruction: drastically reorienting unequal societies toward a sudden social equality.
Calamity, sometimes, reverses the relentless march toward ratcheting wealth hoarding: for once, the rich get poorer while the poor get richer.
Austrian historian Walter Scheidel starts The Great Leveler with a startling set of facts:
“In 2015, the richest sixty-two persons on the planet owned as much private net wealth as the poorer half of humanity, more than 3.5 billion people. If they decided to go on a field trip together, they would comfortably fit into a large coach…[but] not so long ago, in 2010, no fewer than 388 of them had to pool their resources to offset the assets of the global other half, a turnout that would have…filled up a typical Boeing 777.”¹
Clearly, this was a problem going rapidly in the wrong direction.
Today, wealth inequality is indeed grotesque. Take the global poor out of the equations and the stats are no less jarring. The top 1% of American households [control 32% of household wealth](https://substack.com/redirect/895ce240-60d9-43c3-8422-ec5055fa8011?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o), which roughly equates to 55 trillion dollars. Meanwhile, the bottom 50% control just over 2%, or 4 trillion dollars.
Elon Musk’s net worth is currently reported to be $823 billion, a figure which is larger than the entire annual economic output of 170 out of the world’s 192 countries. Put another way, if someone handed you a bag of cash filled with $5,000 every day, it would take you a mere 450,958 years until you had $823 billion. (For context, Homo sapiens emerged roughly 300,000 years ago.)
The status quo of shameful inequality is clearly unsustainable. But, if past is prologue, do we need to simply sit around and wait for a devastating cataclysm to usher in a more equal world? Or, is there another way?
In the mid-fourteenth century, fifteen years after the Black Death swept across England, wiping out up to half the population in agonising terror, the government had an unexpected problem on its hands: the little people had developed the audacity to dress above their station in life.
Getting too big for their ragged and roughly-made britches, they had the impudence to start eating well, too. The allegorical poem Piers Plowman describes post-plague labourers who started to live a little too much like their lords:
“Labourers who had no land and had nothing to live on except the work of their hands would no longer condescend to dine on vegetables left over from the night before. Cheap ale could not satisfy them, nor would a piece of bacon. They wanted fresh meat or fish, fried or baked, and served piping hot, so that their stomachs would not be chilled. And unless a labourer was paid very high wages, he would complain, and curse the day when he had ever been made a working man.”
The crown [sprung to action](https://substack.com/redirect/3e15804c-aee2-4446-974d-95cde416b45b?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o) to stop this ghastly affront, with Edward III providing an appropriate remedy: “[A Statute Concerning Diet and Apparel](https://substack.com/redirect/a3792db1-c3d0-446d-b4e0-994bfb960379?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o)” was adopted in 1363. It was necessary due to the “Outragious and Excessive Apparel of divers People, against their Estate and Degree, to the great Destruction and Impoverishment of all the Land".
Among its provisions: craftsmen were prohibited from wearing silk, jewels, or any gold or silver belts, buttons, rings, or chains. If they insisted on the offence of wearing furs, they were restricted to the lowly animals commensurate with their status: fox, rabbit, or cat.² Ploughmen, shepherds, and threshers were only to wear blanket cloth and russet.
This legislation was a symptom of a society that had lifted up the poor and knocked down the rich so abruptly that laws needed to be drafted to try to preserve the social hierarchy. Without strict codes of dress and dinner, people might—gasp!—mistake a swineherd for a duke, a nobody for nobility.
The Black Death reduced inequality dramatically because the surge in deaths and the abrupt reduction of population meant that land lost value and labour became much more valuable. With up to half the population wiped out, the survivors inherited enough money to fund philanthropic campaigns but there weren’t enough skilled workers around, which drove up wages.³
Anyone growing food needed to replace dead workers to harvest it, but there weren’t as many workers to go around, which further strained elite finances. In one analysis cited by Scheidel, before the plague an average worker might consume 1,154 calories per day with 45 grams of protein, a figure that rose to 1,930 calories per day with 82 grams of protein in the century that followed. This wasn’t because of surges in food production; it was due to a major reduction in inequality. Severe plagues, then, could quickly and robustly rebalance economic conditions in favour of the working poor.⁴
But as Scheidel argues, under the right conditions, mass-mobilisation war could do the same. This was particularly true during the period known as The Great Compression, from 1914 through to 1945 — the period from the onset of World War I to the conclusion of World War II. Across countries as diverse as the US, UK, France, Canada, and Japan, inequality fell sharply.
This happened for a variety of reasons: physical capital was obliterated in the war—and the rich were those who owned the capital.⁵ On the flip-side of wealth destruction, states employed those who were out of work to fill the wartime factories, thereby putting money in the pockets of society’s down and out.
But perhaps most striking of all, progressive income taxes soared to fund the wars, hitting the rich hardest. (It is no coincidence, many economists argue, that elite wealth capture has ballooned since the 1980s, when tax rates on the rich were drastically reduced.)
And yet, Scheidel’s argument in The Great Leveler is pessimistic precisely because it undercuts social agency. With considerable historical evidence to back him up, he convincingly argues that the trigger mechanisms that abruptly and systematically reduced inequality in the past were only made possible by the Four Horsemen of Leveling. After all, there was no way to realistically usher in 94 percent marginal tax rates unless war had already made such unthinkable levels of taxation seem necessary.
This argument, taken as gospel, raises the possibility of a depressing conclusion for our modern Gilded Age: that we must await devastating tragedy before we can finally fix our grotesquely unequal economies.
The rich are aware of this risk; they are preparing for the Four Horsemen. A few years ago, I attended a meeting of ultra-rich individuals. They listened, nodding along, as a wealth adviser told them not to stockpile gold bars for the coming societal collapse, but rather to hoard cigarettes, since the little people will always trade for those. By contrast, you can’t smoke a gold bar.
But economic historian Guido Alfani isn’t ready to give up, not willing to be relegated to twiddling thumbs prior to the great economic meltdown. While he sees merit in Scheidel’s arguments, Alfani thinks we’re still in charge of our own economic destiny—and that we can make a more equal economic world if we focus relentlessly on two major reforms that can level the playing field without the Four Horsemen...
