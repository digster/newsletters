A Startup to Make Brainless Human Clones
Silicon Valley, once the more benign hub of misfit computer nerds, has morphed into a bro-factory for post-modern dystopia, populated by megalomaniacs who expend their brain cells on hyping and pitching what they could build without contemplating whether they should. 
As this recent profile [ https://substack.com/redirect/e1bfa822-3a87-4e66-b956-ece18b7c4ddb?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] of Silicon Valley startup culture frames it, there’s a “pervasive mindlessness” to the place, from the aimlessly wandering and twitching mentally unwell addicts to the Waymos “whooshing around with no one inside.”
At the extremes, the winners fancy themselves as the soon-to-be permanent members of a ruling elite, believing that chumps who toil in “anything related to the human capacity for reason, reflection, insight, creativity, or thought, will be meat for the coltan mines.”
But one startup has taken that mantra about human meat a bit more literally than most. A company called R3 Bio is, according to recent reporting [ https://substack.com/redirect/8e858ea8-fdfb-4e3d-a545-8a81c78e32d4?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ], scoping out whether it would be feasible (and lucrative) to grow non-conscious human clones so that the world’s ultra-wealthy can conveniently harvest spare organs, as needed, effectively from themselves. That technology, reported by MIT Technology Review [ https://substack.com/redirect/8e858ea8-fdfb-4e3d-a545-8a81c78e32d4?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ], could potentially also be used by R3 Bio in their ambition to slap your brain into a younger clone of yourself, thereby prolonging your lifespan in a fresher, suppler replacement body.
The founder of R3 Bio has allegedly pitched [ https://substack.com/redirect/8e858ea8-fdfb-4e3d-a545-8a81c78e32d4?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] the idea as one that would initially require paid female surrogates to give birth to “brainless” human clones. But—don’t worry!—the profitability would soar once clones could themselves be used as non-sentient surrogates. (At various points, these bodies, or something similar to them, have been referred to by R3 Bio as “organ sacks [ https://substack.com/redirect/7e583ca9-d9a4-45ef-bea1-e4d8f6b87037?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ].”) The company’s longevity gurus pitched their ideas to a group of immortality-curious people who shelled out $70,000 for the event, which included R3 Bio’s leadership speaking about “Full Body Replacement.”
But wait! There’s more. What if you don’t want to have to wait for your brainless clone to fully mature? Not to fear. As MIT Tech Review reports. one scientist:
“…talked about ways to shorten the wait for your double to grow up to a size suitable for organ harvesting; a further genetic mutation could be added to cause “central precocious puberty” in the clone, he said. This condition causes a growth spurt, even pubic hair, in a toddler.”
Like many Silicon Valley startups, there’s a wide gulf between the pitch deck and reality, but the existence of this kind of pitch is reason enough to worry. (R3 Bio has disputed some of the MIT Technology Review’s reporting.)
It strikes me that we are living through a period, unlike any other in human history, in which we will be repeatedly forced to confront two era-defining questions: What makes humanity unique? And how much of that should be sacrosanct? In answering those questions, the wisest people on the planet will be those who ask not whether we could, but whether we should.
The Uni-Context
In his 1903 essay The Metropolis and Mental Life, the German sociologist Georg Simmel writes:
“The deepest problems of modern life flow from the attempt of the individual to maintain the independence and individuality of his existence against the sovereign powers of society…”
He was diagnosing the peculiar psychological effect of people moving to cities, thereby being forced to constantly morph their behavior according to the standards of those around them—which was relentlessly policed by metropolitan social norms. 
Simmel’s idea highlights a central point of human social behavior: we are ever-imitating beings who match our conduct to the context in which we find ourselves. How one behaves in church or in an elevator differs considerably from how we might behave at a music festival or a football match. That extends to our social identity, too; we may amplify certain traits with one group of friends and downplay those with another group. The norms of modern civilisation require us, to a certain extent, to be human chameleons.
A few weeks ago, in a conversation with Derek Thompson, the Hungarian-American philosopher Agnes Callard coined a new term to argue that this chameleon-like freedom is collapsing. She called it the uni-context. As she put it: “the uni-context is a scenario in which the ways you should act become the same across all different contexts. There’s just one set of norms you should follow all times, irrespective of context.”
As I’ve written previously [ https://substack.com/redirect/9f3d3693-6fef-4621-bb91-276b4d6bdc1e?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ], the globalisation of information has created a collapsed cultural framework for evaluating ourselves:
Modern social comparison isn’t just larger; it’s also constant. We are bombarded by unceasing reminders of where we stand not just on economic hierarchies, but social ones. We may look our best, only to see someone who looks better online, creating standards of comparison that are unattainable, elusive, and often, depressing. 
But Callard’s argument goes deeper, arguing that the norms of social behavior are collapsing into a single contextual space, too. One reason for this, clearly, is that everything could be filmed or documented, by anyone, at any time, plastered across the global internet. Human social norms can no longer be so chameleon-like. 
The interview [ https://substack.com/redirect/c5b5640e-9540-4bf6-9fc1-3201540f775d?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] with Callard is worth reading. As Thompson summarises, before unpacking the implications:
For most of human history, people judged norms based on local context. A home had its own rules, a cathedral its own rules, and a classroom or bar or funeral parlor had its own rules. But now it is almost like we are constantly living in universal rooms, and the universal room we occupy is assumed to have universal values and universal norms.
The Mars Delusion
It is one of the great mysteries of our time why the richest and most powerful people on Earth seem far more interested in colonising another planet than in saving our own. 
Elon Musk has proclaimed that he would like to die on Mars. The dying would be the easy bit. As this profile from Noema magazine [ https://substack.com/redirect/f8d4af5b-1570-407a-87b0-37f39123b395?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] puts it:
The Martian air is 95% carbon dioxide. Breathing this air would suffocate the average human in a few seconds. The surface air pressure is six millibars, roughly equivalent to the pressure 22 miles above Earth. Were Musk to very inadvisably step out onto the Martian surface in his “Occupy Mars” T-shirt and sandals, all the water in his body would vaporize in an instant, making it difficult to predict what would kill him faster: asphyxiation or a kind of total bodily implosion. Either way, the near-vacuum conditions would soon desiccate his corpse, leaving behind a freeze-dried mummy that — owing to the lack of moisture and putrefying bacteria — would never decompose…
…Mars is cold. The average surface temperature hovers around -80°F, fluctuating by as much as 100 degrees from day to night, and dipping to -225°F at the poles. (“It’s a little cold, but we can warm it up,” reassures [ https://substack.com/redirect/eabef3c8-b228-4d3f-b4f4-dbcc12c48dbf?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] the SpaceX website without much elaboration.)
This isn’t even scratching the deadly red surface. One group of Mars enthusiasts is trying to come up with the conceptual design for something called a “pregnodrome,” a giant centrifuge that might make it a bit safer for a woman to give birth on Mars. (But, to reiterate, death would be most likely for everyone involved.)
Earth, meanwhile, has consistently proven itself to be a rather delightful place to call home. Perhaps — and I’m just spitballing here — it would be a better use of money for some of these billionaires to look around at the raging wildfires, crippling drought, and the environmentally exacerbated global refugee crises and conclude that they might want to corral their wealth and power toward improving the only demonstrably life-sustaining planet in the known universe rather than lighting their money on fire in a doomed attempt to escape from it in a vanity rocket ship.
The Invention of Buses
Buses, one might imagine, surely emerged shortly after the advent of wheeled transport. It is not much of a leap to presume that once people started getting moved about on wheels that they might see the advantage of moving multiple people around at the same time, on a given schedule, particularly if lots of people were headed in the same direction.
This presumption, however, is totally false.
As this whimsical article from  [ https://substack.com/redirect/4e885a36-bf5c-4232-acf7-9d8860fd39b2?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ]Works in Progress [ https://substack.com/redirect/4e885a36-bf5c-4232-acf7-9d8860fd39b2?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] highlights, humans were moved around on wheeled transport for literally thousands of years before the first bus was invented. And strangely enough, that bus service was invented by none other than Blaise Pascal, the mathematician, philosopher, and physicist of the famous Pascal’s Wager thought experiment. He also launched the first real bus service, in Paris, on the 18th of March 1662. 
People got really angry about Pascal’s buses, so they were discontinued after a limited run. The idea of buses soon disappeared for another 150 years, only re-emerging in Nantes. That version was started by a guy with a public bathhouse outside of Nantes who figured that more people would come if he provided them with a fixed timetable and some transport options. Much to his surprise, “he noticed that many people used his shuttle not to go to his baths but in order to disembark at one of the other pick-up points. In effect, he had started a bus service by accident.”
At one point, his new dedicated bus company hit a financial speedbump and he shot himself. The profits later resumed—and the company soon soared. The transport system in Paris today, RATP, “descends directly from it through various mergers and transformations.”
(If you’re ever interested in reading interesting articles about topics that you might not naturally seek out, I recommend Works in Progress [ https://substack.com/redirect/efc4106f-ff1c-4a1e-8ab9-fbb51815ce84?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ], which pretty much always fascinates.)
Stuff I Like That You Might Also Like
Every so often, I share a few bits and bobs that I’ve particularly enjoyed in the hopes that some of you will enjoy them, too.
The Next Best [ https://substack.com/redirect/d40a3457-f27d-4520-ac1d-ba32c74b41d4?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] with Marcel Dirsus
One of the things that annoys me about modern media (partially explained by the uni-context mentioned above) is that outlets often feel a need to dumb things down for the lowest common denominator rather than trusting audiences to appreciate smart, complex ideas presented in an accessible way. 
There’s a great new podcast/YouTube show that just launched called The Next Best [ https://substack.com/redirect/d40a3457-f27d-4520-ac1d-ba32c74b41d4?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] by political scientist and author Dr. Marcel Dirsus. It takes on the nuance and complexity of global affairs head-on with leading experts, but breaks the ideas down into a comprehensible register without losing that intellectual sophistication. The show starts from the premise that there are rarely ideal answers in global affairs, but there’s always a next best option.
Start with these episodes on Trump, Putin, and the End of the Liberal International Order with historian Margaret MacMillan [ https://substack.com/redirect/ab911391-9040-4f59-a694-db34c1a5d826?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ]; drone warfare with Jade McGlynn [ https://substack.com/redirect/d3adf15d-f377-4c83-a8b5-c7458fff1275?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ]; or Kim Jong-Un’s assassination squads with Anna Fifield [ https://substack.com/redirect/1fa57c75-583c-4618-a3c4-6961b626fb8d?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ]. RIP Keigo Higashino
Longtime readers will know that I’ve recommended several books by Japanese murder mystery doyen Keigo Higashino. Sadly, he died in late July at the age of 68, ending a prolific life of excellent writing. His books have sold more than 100 million copies in Japan, though he’s not a household name in most Western countries.
As the New York Times  [ https://substack.com/redirect/a3935fc8-7c3d-409b-be09-e5e111aeba89?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ]obituary [ https://substack.com/redirect/a3935fc8-7c3d-409b-be09-e5e111aeba89?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] put it: “His characters were often regular people such as schoolteachers, young children and pawnbrokers, who perpetrated or suffered acts of violence hidden by their mundane lives.” He explored ideas about the gulf between what was lawful and what was just; why people choose to do things rather than just whodunnit; and the perils of loneliness. Food, and Japanese food culture, are also central motifs in his work. Reading his work feels like a temporary relocation into Japanese life.
If you haven’t read any of his books, I’d suggest starting with The Devotion of Suspect X, but I also highly recommend Malice or A Midsummer’s Equation as alternative ways into his writing.
The Bayeux Tapestry as You’ve Never Seen It
The Bayeux Tapestry is one of the most beautiful objects to emerge from medieval history. It’s now in the British Museum, as part of a temporary swap with France for some astonishing Anglo-Saxon artefacts. While much was made about the risks of transportation, everything arrived safely in one piece, and the tapestry will be on public display in London from September onwards.
To mark the occasion, the British Museum produced a beautiful animated video which tells the story of the tapestry in vivid detail, which was a joy to watch, all in just two minutes.
