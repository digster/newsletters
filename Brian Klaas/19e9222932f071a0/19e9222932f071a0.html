What is resilience?

What science knows that the self-help shelf doesn't.

I: The Perils of Faux Redundancy

At 2:46pm on March 11, 2011, two seismic plates converged, triggering the fourth most powerful earthquake in recorded history. The 9.1 quake persisted for six minutes, moving the entire island of Honshu—Japan’s largest island—eight feet to the east. It was so powerful that Earth’s entire axis was shifted by roughly seven inches. Even the rotational speed of the planet slightly increased, shortening our astronomical day length by 1.8 microseconds.

When the shaking stopped, an enormous tsunami soon followed, racing toward Japan’s coast. The maximum height of the wave was recorded at an estimated 133 feet. The unfathomable surge of water flooded 217 square miles. The official death toll was 19,747 people.

Japan’s nuclear power plants, including Fukushima Daiichi, were built with layer upon layer of redundancy to ensure that any natural disaster would not trigger a nuclear one.

At Fukushima, the three operating reactors were automatically “scrammed” when the quake hit, meaning that they would stop nuclear fission. That required electricity to cool them down safely. There were six separate external power lines rigged to the plant, but each was knocked out by the earthquake.¹

The twelve backup diesel generators seamlessly kicked into gear, but eleven of them soon failed. They required water cooling to operate but the sea water pumps had been destroyed by the tsunami, as they were built at a lower elevation than the plant itself. Ultimately, AC power became impossible to generate, so the system was primed to switch over to the backup DC electricity powered by a further layer of redundancy: batteries. However, the batteries were stored in the flooded area, so they, too, failed to operate.

“Over the next three days, one by one, the three reactors that had been operating when the earthquake struck lost core cooling capability,” leading to the nuclear disaster. The amount of radiation released amounted to 15 percent of what occurred in Chernobyl, enough to permanently displace hundreds of thousands of people, create environmental calamity, and produce a predicted surge of thousands of avoidable cancer deaths.

Meanwhile, much closer to the earthquake’s epicenter, there was another nuclear power plant: Onagawa. Despite intense shaking and a fire, the plant withstood the earthquake so effectively that it became the safest refuge for the local community. Surrounding structures were destroyed or washed away.

A towering wall held the water back; the seawater pumps had been designed to withstand a tsunami; the electricity backup generators were housed in separate, protected buildings, away from the reactor itself.

Both Fukushima and Onagawa faced a tsunami height of over 42 feet, but Fukushima’s sea wall was only 19 feet tall. Onagawa’s? 56 feet, designed in the 1960s by a stubborn civil engineer named Yanosuke Hirai, who took catastrophic risk seriously and therefore demanded a more elevated construction, a huge wall, and a special tsunami channel he designed himself.

This is clearly a parable of resilience: one nuclear power station passed the test and one failed it. But it’s also a useful gateway into a far more sophisticated understanding of that concept. The word “resilience” means a million different things, some contradictory. Nonetheless, it has drastically surged as a lexicon buzzword. It has been tattooed onto every management consultant’s tongue, while it seems to have become a strictly enforced legal requirement to slap it into the subtitle of every self-help book.

Yet, when you start to press people on what the word actually means, they often regurgitate opposite answers. Is it about “weathering the storm” and “bouncing back” to a previous state? Or, is it about changing, adapting to a new normal, and creating a different future? Both are used interchangeably as though they’re identical forms of resilience, even when they’re actually inversions of each other.

Here, we’ll dive into the fascinating science of resilience, a robust research field that grew out of ecology in the 1970s and now covers everything from mental health and bridges to coral reefs and democracies. Understanding the different kinds of resilience can help us not only understand natural systems but prevent disaster in our social world, too.

II: Three Kinds of Resilience—and the Role of Time

Let’s start with three broad types of resilience:

  1. “Bouncing back” resilience that focuses on recovery to a previous state.

  2. “Bouncing forward” resilience that focuses on adaptation to a new environment.

  3. “Antifragility” resilience, a term coined by Nassim Nicolas Taleb, which refers to systems that grow stronger from stressors, perturbations, and shocks. (Rather than trying to weather the storm, some kinds of storm can actually make these systems stronger.)

The first two categories have opposite relationships with time. Bouncing back resilience puts the past as the main goal: returning to how things were, surviving, returning to the “old” normal. Engineering resilience, the kind typically most useful for bridges or nuclear power plants, is this variety: the goal is to not break despite facing a shock or a disturbance, to return to fulfilling its function exactly the same way as before.

If you want to visualize this resilience, consider a simple model of what’s known as a “basin of attraction,” with a valley and a ball. Even if the ball gets jostled fairly hard, it will probably roll back pretty easily, returning to where it was to begin with. (This doesn’t just apply to models, but is a way of visually imagining everything from human mood to supply chains and rainforests.)

By contrast, bouncing forward resilience is about evolving for the future, a ball being jostled into a different basin of attraction. The environment is changing, so adaptation into a “new” normal is required. A company that made typewriters could try to bounce back, but that wouldn’t be of much use in the digital age. This kind of changing resilience is often required of democratic institutions facing new types of threats or individual workers in the age of artificial intelligence. If you don’t change, you might be left behind by those that adapt more effectively.

Antifragility, by contrast, isn’t defined by a simple relationship with time. It is an ongoing process about past, present, and future. It simply means that certain kinds of systems are more robust if they’re regularly stress-tested. Fragility arises, counterintuitively, if there is a long time between stressors.

III: Mistaking Stability for Resilience

In popular culture, resilience and stability are often considered adjacent concepts. But in complex systems theory, they couldn’t be more different—and it’s downright dangerous to conflate them, a lesson that we would be wise to learn to protect ourselves and our increasingly fragile societies.

The foundational text of ecological resilience, by C.S. Holling, identifies how lakes can rapidly transform from crystal clear waters to turbid waters. Both states are astonishingly stable, but when a lake flips from clear to turbid, it’s really difficult to bounce back to a healthy lake.

The natural world can tell us something important.

While SCUBA diving, I’ve seen several Crown-of-Thorns starfish, a large, beautiful multi-armed creature with terrible destructive power. When feeding, they push their stomachs out through their mouths, then use an enzyme to turn the coral into a “soup.” When they’re done with their dinner, they pull their stomach back in, then move onto the next bit of coral.

Unfortunately, these coral predators can produce up to 200 million eggs per year. 99.99+ percent of those eggs will die, never producing a viable starfish.

But in the right conditions, that mortality rate can slightly drop—and with 200 million potential starfish coming out of each female, even a tiny shift in egg viability can trigger a catastrophic tipping point.² They can overwhelm a reef and devour it, consuming decades of growth in a single season. Reefs can experience unbelievable population explosions, where the starfish go from 0.5 starfish per hectare to thousands per hectare in an ecological blink. Some reefs never recover.

From the outside, the reef looks completely healthy up until the tipping point. There are few visible markers that something is about to go very wrong. It’s completely stable…up until the moment of collapse.

That’s because reefs, like all systems that require resilience (including ourselves), operate through a mix of slow variables and fast variables.

The slow variables change gradually over time, often invisibly. They’re typically the features that you’re not looking for as signs that something is becoming fragile or on the verge of collapse. For example, on coral reefs, key slow variables include nutrient load in the water column, a reduced population of triton snails (which feed on Crown-of-Thorns starfish eggs), and the stocks of herbivorous fish that maintain reefs within a healthy state.³ When these variables align in just the wrong way, the starfish outbreak may ensue—destroying the reef completely.

The fast variables, by contrast, are usually visible markers of a system’s apparently healthy state. Do the corals look healthy? Are there lots of fish today? For years, even decades, a reef may tick all those healthy boxes, even as it’s imperceptibly lurching toward collapse because the right kind of fish aren’t present in the right ratios or because there’s a slight shift in the nutrients within the water. Stable, but not resilient.

These concepts provide useful frameworks for evaluating the built-up tension in human social systems on the brink of disaster. For example, Robert Putnam’s research on the erosion of social trust and the rise of atomistic isolation in Bowling Alone demonstrated a slow variable that has, over time, facilitated a fast variable: the breakdown of American democracy.

The Arab Spring offers another cautionary tale. Tunisia appeared completely stable for decades, even as many slow variables were creating a tinderbox. Eventually, a single vegetable vendor who lit himself on fire sparked a conflagration that spread across the Middle East, toppling several regimes and setting violent conflict ablaze from Libya to Syria.

Similarly, government civil servants purged by DOGE or the mass layoffs and devastation of local journalism are akin to the loss of herbivorous fish on reefs, the “maintenance actors” that invisibly do crucial work—but that you only realize how important they were in hindsight when collapse arrives.

Ecologists have developed a quantitative tool that features as a sort of “nature’s early warning system.”⁴ It’s called critical slowing down. Technically, it refers to systems that start to take longer to “bounce back” from a shock or perturbation. In other words, something upsets the balance in the system and it returns to normal more slowly than usual. This lag is often a blinking red light, an indication that the system is approaching a tipping point where a rapid collapse is becoming more likely.

Critical slowing down has been demonstrated in everything from lake ecology to depression, in which the phenomenon is observed with mood before someone enters a state of depression and right before their depression is alleviated. It applies before both transitions, good and bad.

The bottom line is this: many systems, from authoritarian regimes to coral reefs, can appear stable even as they race toward collapse.

IV: Redundancy vs. Response Diversity

To avoid collapse, it’s tempting to focus on creating redundancy. This is, quite obviously, a wise move. The global economy would be more resilient right now if there was another way to get oil out of the Strait of Hormuz. When Crowdstrike went down in 2024 due to an error at the company, a huge part of the global economy ground to a halt because there were no backups—and even companies that didn’t rely on Crowdstrike were knocked out because they relied on companies that were Crowdstrike clients. As much as people love hyperefficiency and relentless optimization, both can amplify fragility.

Redundancy, therefore, often helps.

But as the Fukushima example above shows, redundancy fails to create resilience when it lacks a property called response diversity. The Fukushima plant wasn’t resilient because when one system failed, the backups failed due to the same stressor. The earthquake and tsunami knocked out the main systems and all the backups.

Similarly, on coral reefs, it’s all well and good if multiple different species of fish can perform the same reef maintenance tasks, but if they all die when the water temperature goes up by 1 degree Celsius, then species diversity doesn’t actually create climate resilience. What you need is a backup that doesn’t fail for the same reasons as the function it’s replacing.

The 2008-09 crash was a vivid illustration of this principle: there was plenty of diversification and hedging across portfolios, but they were all vulnerable to the same shock. If a system and its backups have weaknesses that are correlated, then redundancy protects nothing when calamity arrives.

Diversity is therefore often a source of resilience. Consider the humble banana. Wild bananas are virtually inedible, hard, packed with seeds, unappetizing. Then, through a series of evolutionary accidents, there emerged the Gros Michel variety of bananas: sweet, seedless, with a thick peel, perfect for transporting across large distances. Without seeds, though, they had to be deliberately cultivated through offshoots, ensuring that every banana was a genetic clone—identical to those before it.

For decades, virtually every banana cultivated for human consumption was a Gros Michel clone. Yet, in the early 20th century, a fungal infestation known as Panama Disease spread across banana plantations. Since the fruits were genetically identical, they were all vulnerable—and over the next several decades they got completely wiped out.

That led to a switch to the Cavendish banana, which originated as a grown variety in the 1830s in northern England, when Joseph Paxton, the head gardener at Chatsworth House, “obtained a specimen imported from Mauritius.” The Cavendish wasn’t vulnerable to Panama Disease, so the world switched in the 1950s; every banana you eat today is a Cavendish, a genetic clone of that variety which was first cultivated by Paxton. And yet, even though the banana industry had just seen the dangers of quite literally putting all the same fruit in one basket, they simply repeated the structural arrangement, swapping one genetic monoculture for another.

Sure enough, in 1989, a strain of Fusarium oxysporum f. sp. cubense, a fungal pathogen, was found killing Cavendish bananas in Asia. It has now spread globally, threatening the heart of banana production—and without significant effort, it may be that the Cavendish will go the way of the Gros Michel.

The lesson is obvious: diversity, and especially diversity that responds differently to the same stressors, is more likely to yield true resilience.

V: Antifragility: When Stress Creates Resilience

In the summer of 1910, the newly created US Forest Service faced an unprecedented crisis. With dry conditions north of the Rockies, a raging forest fire consumed three million acres of land, equivalent to the size of Connecticut. Entire towns were burned; 87 people were killed, many of them firefighters. The “Big Burn” of 1910 remains the largest recorded wildfire in American history.

The catastrophe drastically influenced government policy, producing an unequivocal commitment to suppressing and fighting all forest fires. By 1935, the US Forest Service had a new mantra: the 10 am policy, which decreed “that every fire should be suppressed by 10 a.m. the day following its initial report.”

Better science later revealed the seriousness of this policy error. Many forests, it turns out, are dependent on occasional fires to stay healthy. From lodgepole pines to Giant sequoias, some cones require fire to melt resin and release their seeds. Moreover, many trees are resilient to routine fires but vulnerable to massive conflagrations. Without semi-regular wildfires, underbrush fuel would build up over time, ensuring that the next fire would devastate rather than refresh the ecosystem. Artificially preventing natural stressors drastically amplified catastrophic risk.

Some complex systems therefore require stressors to thrive, a property that Nassim Nicolas Taleb called antifragility. This is related to the concept of eustress (good stress) which was coined by Hans Selye, an early 20th century endocrinologist. Some systems are strengthened through specific forms of adversity.

In human societies, this property abounds, from psychological resilience forged after some emotional adversity to elections that stress-test governments.⁵ Dictatorships appear stable but are often invisibly veering toward an eventual violent regime change. Democracies appear chaotic, but that visible volatility often acts like a pressure valve, giving citizens a chance to vent frustrations, change policies, and upend governments instead of burning the whole system down. Elections and protests can function as an antifragility stressor in governance.

The same is true with ideas, in which the academic battleground of peer review and actively challenging conventional wisdom can create more resilience in human knowledge. Without regular attempts at falsification, our understanding of the world can become dangerously outdated and brittle.

VI: Resilience from Buzzword to Blueprint

These scientific frameworks allow us to understand the specific system dynamics of resilience far beyond the linguistic comfort of a buzzword. They offer a blueprint that can be used to better engineer any complex system, from supply chains and democracies to our own fragile lives. To be successful, however, we must be precise: what kind of resilience do we want?

And yet, resilience also requires intellectual humility. With complex systems, from economies to our brains, the clue is in the name: so complex we don’t really understand them. We must therefore sometimes have a healthy dose of respect for that which appears unimportant, ensuring that we don’t carelessly decimate our human equivalent of a seemingly pointless herbivorous fish maintaining a reef. And when we try to slap our feeble manipulations into a system we misdiagnose, as with forest fires, we may actually increase fragility.

On the other hand, it can be refreshing and uplifting to recognize that resilience can come from the small, slow variables of life. Democracies become stronger from citizens who volunteer at food banks or vote, even when everything feels hopeless. People become more resilient from kind words nobody else bothered to say or passions that others see as pointless.

Nature has kindly provided us a blueprint for smarter resilience. As the world careens from crisis to crisis, we would be wise to learn from it.
