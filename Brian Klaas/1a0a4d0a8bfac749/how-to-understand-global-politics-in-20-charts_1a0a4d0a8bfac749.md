History is moving faster than ever before, driven by the unprecedented pace of technological change and an interconnected, globalised world in which the ripple effects of one country can instantly upend life for billions of people thousands of miles away.
But most people still go about understanding global politics the wrong way. Political junkies tend to follow the 24-hour news cycle obsessively. That’s a problem for two reasons.
First, the news—particularly in the United States—focuses on controversies above substance. What did Trump just say on Truth Social? How did the Democrats respond? What’s the latest culture war or scandal? These are questions that too often distract from gargantuan shifts in global power and behind-the-scenes machinations of shadowy political kingpins.
Second, the news is mostly an aggregation of every bad event that happened in the last twenty four hours, anywhere on Earth. But they are events, and because the news focus lies squarely on them, we lose sight of the major, gradual trends in our world that, like slow drips of water, seem insignificant on any given day, but can eventually cause a flood. Drips add up.
That’s why, when I teach global politics, I start by quizzing students—often political junkies themselves—on seemingly basic questions. How many countries have nuclear weapons and how many do they have? How many people live in extreme poverty and is that figure rising or falling? How has the child mortality rate changed over time? What proportion of rare earths minerals are controlled by China compared to the United States? Which country’s economy is larger: Italy or Russia? Which 10 countries have the largest military budgets? Has the global use of coal increased or decreased over the last 25 years? Do any countries have majority-female parliaments?
These questions aren’t easy if you just follow the news. From CNN to the BBC, the event bias of the news crowds out explainers that help us understand the overall picture of global politics—and the trends that are reshaping our world.
With help from the astonishingly wonderful people at [Our World in Data](https://substack.com/redirect/03b6b749-acf6-4ac3-9a68-719d8fd9f66b?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o) and other data nerds, this edition will give you a clearer picture of what’s going on in global politics beyond the convoluted controversies and daily events that obscure our understanding. So, here’s how to understand global politics in 20 charts. In 1990, nearly half—43%—of the world’s population lived in extreme poverty. Today, that figure is 10%. Economic growth in China driven by globalisation lifted 940 million people out of extreme poverty, but even if you exclude China from the picture, the figure went from 33% to 12% in a relative blink of human history. This is the best good news story of the modern world and almost everyone is oblivious to it.
The chart above, from [CREA](https://substack.com/redirect/f4e1ce4c-ea67-47f1-8d2a-cf5abb3971d3?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o), shows how sanctions and Western cut-offs from Russian fossil fuels have simply led to a diversion of energy toward India, while exports to China have largely remained stable or slightly increased. This illustrates a broader problem with global politics: displacement circumvents an intended policy if only a slice of global power supports that policy.
Most countries in the world are either competitive authoritarian or electoral authoritarian regimes. Competitive authoritarian regimes are places that have some political competition, but it’s not a fair fight and the regime usually stays in power through manipulated elections. Electoral authoritarian regimes are places that hold sham elections that the opposition never wins. Few countries are pure dictatorships (like North Korea) or extremely high performing liberal democracies (like Norway). Most are in the middle.
However, after a big surge in democratisation in the post-Cold War 1990s, the tide has turned for the last two decades. Every year, since 2006, more countries have moved toward authoritarianism than toward democracy.
There is some bad news, of course. There’s a real and disturbing anti-women backlash in populist right-wing movements. And only a few countries have majority-female parliaments (Rwanda, Cuba, Nicaragua, Costa Rica, Bolivia, and Mexico). But the global trend is clear: women are gaining formal political power across the globe. Japan remains a major outlier among rich countries even with its first female prime minister; only 14.6% of Japan’s parliament is female. The US remains a laggard with 27%, whereas the UK is at 41%.
There were [511 new gigawatts](https://substack.com/redirect/936e02e6-915e-442d-9a1f-b50ba8620e0b?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o) of solar power added to the global energy picture in 2025—an increase of 27% in a single year. China is the biggest driver of that shift, but solar power is rapidly increasing in India, Europe, the US and the Middle East, too. This is great news; few people know about it.
