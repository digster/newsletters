I: Silent Sam

At 3:30pm on June 2, 1913, the tobacco baron Julian S. Carr stood before a freshly unveiled statue on the campus of the University of North Carolina in Chapel Hill.¹ To a gathered crowd of 1,000 supporters of the Confederacy almost exactly half a century after the Battle of Gettysburg, he extolled the virtues of an army that fought to perpetuate the enslavement of other human beings. The statue was lovingly named “Silent Sam,” a stoic Confederate soldier who was literally placed on a pedestal, to be admired and idolised by the future young men of North Carolina.

“No nobler young men ever lived” than those who took up the battle cry for white dominance, Carr proclaimed. He read poetry in their honour, called Robert E. Lee “the world’s greatest hero,” and lamented how young men paid too little attention to how the Confederates “saved the very life” of the Anglo-Saxon race.

Then, Carr departed from the lofty and abstract values of white supremacy into a personal memory that apparently warmed his heart, an episode that took place shortly after he returned from the frontlines of the Confederate army. “One hundred yards from where we stand,” Carr reminisced, “I horse-whipped a negro wench until her skirts hung in shreds.” Carr, wistfully thinking of that moment, called it “a pleasing duty.”

A little over a century later, the statue was toppled by protesters who denounced its symbolism of white supremacy. That statue, along with the dedication speech extolling the "noble young men” of the Confederacy, provide a compelling and disturbing framework for understanding modern white nationalism in the United States under Donald Trump.

There is a resurgence under Trump of those who wish to celebrate America’s racist past, glorify a movement that was motivated by human bondage, and make the United States a place where white hoods that conceal the racist beneath are no longer necessary. These debates over historical legacies are not abstract nor academic but have significant impact on individual lives and contested social orders. As W.E.B. Du Bois put it in a 1931 essay: “The most terrible thing about War, I am convinced, is its monuments.”

In recent months, two significant research findings have provided a new way to understand longstanding symbols of racist power such as the Silent Sam statue celebrating the Confederacy—and the horrific racial hierarchy it failed to protect.

The first, published in Nature, explores a crucial question for our modern politics:

Who becomes a white nationalist?

Their findings help us better understand the drivers of racism and how it metastasizes throughout society. Are young white men really more prone to the siren song of racist movements than those from older generations? Does social isolation and digital activity increase the prevalence of white nationalism? And does racism increase in areas with rising numbers of minorities or is higher prevalence of white nationalism purely about perceptions and a subjective sense of “racial threat”?

The second, written by Lee-Or Ankori-Karlinsky and published in the American Political Science Review, grapples with a more complex empirical question about historical memory and the monuments erected to atrocities of the past. The statues are grotesque effigies to one of the most despicable lingering threads of American history. But Ankori-Karlinsky climbed over mountains of data to ask seemingly straightforward fact-based questions:

How did the rise—and later selective removal—of Confederate statues and monuments affect the rates of racist violence in the United States? Do symbols of racist violence achieve similar goals to the violence itself?

The answers he found were surprising and perplexing, but they shine a fascinating light on the ways in which race, hierarchy, and status are communicated through symbols by those who wish to subjugate others to their hate-filled vision of the world.

II: Who Becomes a White Nationalist?

Writing in Nature, Patrick J. Egan and his co-authors set out to identify what proportion of non-Hispanic white Americans endorse the beliefs of white nationalism—and how those proportions differ across various demographic factors. They then wanted to test three main hypotheses that help explain the rise of white nationalism in modern American political life (beyond the more straightforward Trump Effect). Those three hypotheses were as follows:

  1. Strain Theory: Do shocks such as “personal health crises, crime victimhood or the dissolution of a relationship or job—create vulnerabilities that make individuals more receptive to radicalizing messages” such as white nationalism?

  2. Status Threat Theory: Does a “real or perceived diminishment of a group’s relative power fuel grievance narratives that cast blame” on others, thereby fuelling white nationalism as a backlash to greater racial equality?
