6 Lessons from the Blue Tsunami

It was the biggest wave since Watergate.

Brian Klaas
Nov 4
 
READ IN APP
 

The MAGA Republicans who predicted that a Blue Wave wouldn’t happen were accidentally right: it was a Blue Tsunami. Yesterday’s US midterm elections provided the biggest backlash since Watergate, which was, perhaps not so coincidentally, another historic rebuke of a corrupt crook in the White House.

The polling miss happened—but in the opposite direction to previous misses. The polls undercounted Democrats, not Republicans. That polling error was probably due to a combination of extreme levels of non-response bias, as I highlighted here, and subsequently adjusting the numbers to match a predicted electorate without fully understanding the scale of the swing away from Trump, particularly among Hispanic voters, men, and young people.

Republicans, who aimed to rig the election with a series of mid-decade gerrymanders, were outmanoeuvred in two ways: Democrats responded by redrawing a comparable number of their own districts and they beat the Republican candidates in many of the redrawn districts that were supposed to be safe pro-Trump seats.

Elon Musk spent half a billion dollars to try to boost Republicans. He had skin in the game: he had moved to Texas apparently to get away from Democrats who wanted to regulate and tax the world’s first trillionaire.

But Democrats didn’t just win Texas despite that grotesque spending; they won in a landslide. James Talarico won by 12 points. That’s the first Democratic victory for US Senate since 1988—38 years ago.

Gina Hinojosa stomped a popular Republican incumbent, the first Democratic Texas Governor in 36 years—and the first ever Latina Governor in the state’s history.

Bo French, the nominee for the state’s Railroad Commissioner, who has made a sport out of spewing anti-Semitic, racist hatred, vowed not to rest until “every Muslim is gone,” routinely called to publicly execute Democrats, and lamented that the US was not more like the white supremacist state of Rhodesia, also lost.

Democrats swept into power in the most unthinkable places beyond Texas. They won in Kansas, where Trump beat Kamala Harris by 16 points two years ago. They won in Iowa, which Trump carried by 13 points. They won in South Carolina, where Trump trounced Harris by 18 points. And, at the time of writing, Democrats look on the cusp of winning deep red Mississippi—Mississippi!—a state that Trump won by nearly 23 points. These are swings beyond what even the most bullish Democratic officials could have imagined.

But what does it all mean? These are my six takeaways from the election, which can help us understand not just how we got here, but what to do next:

I: Corruption Matters

Sure, a significant part of the Democratic surge can be chalked up to the rising cost of living and particularly the spike in gas prices due to the cartoonish Trump/Hegseth tag-team of a catastrophically bungled war with Iran (surprisingly, using performative testosterone and getting rid of “beardos” as a geopolitical strategy isn’t sufficient to win modern wars).

But a lot of the victory comes down to a tried-and-true strategy for taking down authoritarian populists: focus on the corruption.

Thankfully, this administration has engaged in cartoonish corruption, from self-dealing crypto meme coins that have made Trump billions richer while saddling foolish investors with nearly $4 billion in losses, to accepting a $400 million airplane from Gulf autocrats in Qatar, to peddling access and influence through investments in his various vanity projects, most notably his attempt to bring Saddam Hussein Cheesecake Factory chic to his ballroom.

Democrats, particularly in the leadership, need to recognise that this is not a sideshow that voters ignore. Voters, across countries and along the full width of the political spectrum, loathe corruption.

Make it a focus of the next two years.

This will prove one of the biggest victories for Democrats: they can now hold relentless hearings with subpoena power. By taking over the House and Senate, Democrats can transform American political institutions from a smokescreen for Donald Trump’s corruption into an engine of truth.

II: Thank You, Minnesota!

Donald Trump picked the wrong state to mess with. Never invade the home of a communitarian winter people with fascist thugs unless you’re ready to get beaten by the gritty determination of neighborliness.

When I was there in January, I saw a community transformed in defense of fellow human beings who simply lived next door. Grandparents were driving around blowing whistles at masked-up government thugs; single parents who knew their white kids would be safe took time out from their busy schedules to deliver groceries to parents who were trapped in their own homes, living in fear that their own children would be abducted.

In the fight against authoritarian brutality, two of the most potent weapons proved to be kindness and decency.

There were casualties: Renée Good and Alex Pretti were unforgivable losses in the fight against ICE. But their deaths were powerful totems for the country and the world: standing up to the thugs in the street is an effective way to stand up to the Thug-in-Chief, too.

This, to my mind, was the beginning of the end for Trump’s seemingly invincible second term. Billionaires who had nothing to fear from Donald Trump other than lost profits bent their knee to him with embarrassing fawning at White House dinners, while ordinary Minnesotans who had everything to fear risked their lives to protect their neighbors.

Thank you, Minnesota.

III: Hispanics Swung Left in a Big Way

The ICE crackdown, combined with a MAGA online discourse that increasingly fetishises deporting anyone who isn’t white, was sufficient to push Latino voters toward Democrats in droves.

This was a major factor in several key races—and it will reshape the electoral landscape for 2028 in a way that dramatically disadvantages the future Republican nominee for president.

IV: Run Authentic Candidates, Not Median Slop

Democrats won some races simply because voters wanted to reject the MAGA disaster of the last two years. But in others, the Democratic candidate won because they didn’t shy away from outspoken criticism of their opponent—and Trump—with passionate language and principled vision.

These were, by and large, not a slate of candidates who made meek statements about unity and cross-party compromise. In an era of grotesque authoritarian corruption and deep economic injustice, those who cannot plainly describe their outrage come across as out of touch, or worse, as focus group slop candidates.

For a generation, Democrats have too often shied away from political fights that were worth having. The anti-authoritarian, anti-corruption, pro-economic justice fights are all worth having. Voters may not always know the minutiae of policy, but they do often have a fine-tuned bullshit detector and antennae that are remarkably able to register inauthentic poll-tested language.

The lesson from this midterm that too many will wrongly draw will be in the tired language of centrists vs. progressives. The real lesson is this: recruit candidates who believe what they say and let them say it their own way, with unfiltered passion and authentic conviction.

V: Authoritarianism May Now Intensify

Donald Trump isn’t a man who will be content to coast through the final two years of power, ranting at dwindling crowds. He may be distracted by vanity projects—let the man spend as much time as possible on the algal blooms in the Reflecting Pool!—but ultimately I expect his authoritarianism to increase in this lame duck period as he gets constrained and threatened by Congressional hearings and biting oversight.

This could manifest in particularly dangerous ways on the international stage, since foreign policy is an area of tremendous latitude for American presidents compared to domestic legislation. Trump will quickly realise that his nominees are now dead in the water, but that will only engage the part of his authoritarian lizard brain that sees rules and laws as temporary inconveniences.

It would be better to think of him, therefore, as a caged animal, rather than a lame duck—and we’re all stuck inside the cage with him for the next 808 days.

VI: Don’t Waste Power Again

The United States remains a fundamentally broken democracy in need of systemic reform. Political scientists like me can endlessly debate whether it is a “failing democracy” or a “flawed democracy” or should now be classified as a “competitive authoritarian” regime. But what’s clear is that American institutions and the political system are on an unsustainable trajectory.

The midterm elections changed who wields power. That doesn’t magically fix the government or save democracy. And unless those who will now have power understand that the real victories come when you wield that power effectively, particularly through hard-fought attempts at system-level reforms, then the next two years will be a wasted opportunity, an era that constrains authoritarianism but fails to reform or rebuild democracy.

Today, celebrate an astonishing victory for the forces of decency and democracy.

Tomorrow, the real work begins.
