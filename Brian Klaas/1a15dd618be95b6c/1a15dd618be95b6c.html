The Hidden Cost of AI “Effort Collapse”

Drowning in slop overloads everything from job applications to customer complaints. We need fresh mechanisms to sort people.

I: Effort collapse and effort barriers

From the dawn of time until sometime around 2025, we used effort as a crude but reliable proxy for value.

If someone was willing to spend a lot of time doing something, it must mean it’s worthwhile—at least to that person. Even those who loathe art might find a way to appreciate that Michelangelo spent nine years of his existence painting the Sistine Chapel.

Many human sorting systems therefore rely on a simple mechanism to reduce the volume of applicants or complainants. They consume someone’s most precious finite resource: time. People who are worthy, or who really want something, will signal that through the costly expenditure of their limited time alive.

In the past, people sifting through job applications could be reasonably sure that the person applying was at least somewhat serious about the job. After all, they had gone to the trouble of filling out an application form that might take even the swiftest writer several hours to complete.

Today, Claude can apply for dozens of different jobs in a matter of seconds. Spray and pray.

This dynamic is a new dystopian feature of the AI age that I call effort collapse.¹ Sorting mechanisms that were designed to be crude proxies for interest because they required significant effort are now effortless. The net effect of this loss of friction is bad for everyone. The rejection rate goes through the roof. The people processing applications are overwhelmed. The system gets overloaded and breaks down.

Social systems rely on effort barriers. Those are disintegrating.

Worse, because AI slop is flooding the pool, everyone looks the same. There’s no longer a meaningful correlation between the strength of an application and the strength of an applicant. Signals previously used to reject people—sloppiness, terrible prose, unpolished documents—have mostly disappeared. As I previously wrote, for the average person, “the writing is getting better. The ideas are getting worse…I call it glittering sludge.”

Everyone sounds the same: mediocre, boring, and therefore easy to reject. Modern society relied on sorting people effectively, ideally according to some form of merit. We can no longer do that.

A few weeks ago, I applied for a small academic grant. I took my time with it, carefully polishing the prose, re-reading the articles I cited, all the old-fashioned way. I knew it would be competitive. There were only going to be ten successful applicants. I prepared for that most hallowed academic tradition: the cull.

But when I got the inevitable rejection letter, itself likely generated by AI, the mind boggled: there had been 583 applications, all for a grant to cover just £2,500 of research costs, a comparatively tiny amount for academic grants. The success rate was only 1.7 percent, down from a seven-year average for that funder of 26 percent.

II: Don’t offload anything that’s worth your effort

On the individual level, I’ve long been making the argument that people too often conflate the intrinsic process of thinking that writing forces with the output that the effort produces. Students write essays to learn to think; humans use language to express the known universe’s most advanced brainpower. We rob ourselves of something precious when we offload our cognition to machines for things that are worth thinking about.

If an LLM can help someone pay their bills or write a mundane box-ticking e-mail to Kevin from accounts, then, by all means, get Claude fired up! Give ‘em your best wishes, Claude! Tell ‘em how brilliant and “load bearing” his ideas are. And honestly, your ChatGPT-drafted e-mail can tell Kevin with empty earnestness, that’s powerful.

But humans are only hurting themselves when they take ideas they love to contemplate and convert them, through AI, into completed tasks and ticked off outputs. Nobody dies with a smile, clutching a freshly completed checklist.

Perhaps the best illustration of this grotesque category error came from the CEO of Suno, a tool that allows anyone to make a crappy AI-generated song with zero effort. Here’s how he justified the technology:

“It's not really enjoyable to make music now. It takes a lot of time. It takes a lot of practice. You need to get really good at an instrument or really good at a piece of production software. I think the majority of people don't enjoy the majority of the time they spend making music.”

This ethos, that creative effort is bad because it’s hard, would lead to the slopification of everything that’s worth doing. Oh, it takes some effort? Why not consider using this dystopian app that gives you the illusion of talent and human creativity instead of cultivating it for yourself! Ka-ching!

III: How do systems cope with a flood of slop?

Buried beneath this individual-level loss of humanity, there’s a second previously invisible truth about human systems.

With the advent of AI everywhere, we are losing something about how to run society because we constantly use proxies for effort to determine what might be worth our time.

The main reason I don’t want to read an AI-generated novel is because it matters to me that the novel sprang from a fallible, emotion-filled human brain. But it also matters that it involved difficult intellectual toil, sleepless nights, years of effort—something that another person thought was worth devoting years of their life to producing and that I can therefore imagine might be worth reading. Of course, bad writers can devote a lifetime to generating human-produced slop and that doesn’t make it worth reading, but effort often serves as a useful signal to navigate a world overrun with options.

For other features of our lives, including some that involve writing, effort is meaningless and should be minimised, even eliminated. I am perfectly delighted to encounter instruction manuals for furniture assembly that are written in clear, boring prose by ChatGPT. Let people grapple with beauty while the machines grapple with mechanical clarity.

And yet, while it seems unequivocally positive to offload boring, soul-sucking writing to Claude and ChatGPT, there is a rising, unexpected cost to our systems that govern society, from employers and grantmakers to customer service teams and governments.

AI chatbots universally make it far easier to apply for something or complain about it, gumming up systems in the process. Worse, they frequently encourage people to make frivolous complaints or to apply for longshots precisely because the tools are designed to be sycophantic—they tell people what they want to hear. Yes, you should complain, and here’s the legalese to support you! Yes, you’ll definitely get that job because your skill set is completely unique among humanity—want me to help you draft the cover letter? It’s not delusional entitlement, ChatGPT reassures you, it’s reality.

Now, with effort barriers removed, there are rapidly rising downstream effects, some good, some bad.

A poor person who was denied benefits or help from the government or a social program can appeal more easily. That’s how it should be, obviously, but higher success rates also mean that programs run out of money faster. We should all want people who deserve help to get it, but this will squeeze government finances in new ways.

On the flip side, for governments and companies alike, frivolous complaints and time-wasting disputes are far easier and therefore more common. For companies that have long screwed over consumers and now find themselves buried in a deluge of lengthy disputes written by ChatGPT, perhaps that’s their comeuppance, but for government services, the state is increasingly unable to cope with these demands.

As Sam Freeman notes:

Government has the biggest problem. Modern administrative states are built around processes that are difficult to use, often deliberately so, which helps ration demand. Regulatory and legal routes are designed on the assumption that only a small number of people who might use them actually will. AI blows this assumption apart. The chart below shows twelve different government services from around the world and the surge in applications/complaints received – the grey line shows when ChatGPT was launched.

In the United States, an early 2026 analysis of complaints to the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau shows that the drastic rise in complaints was likely produced by AI tools. But that same analysis also concluded that the complaints generated by AI were, on average, about 7% more likely to be upheld in favour of the complainant compared to those written by people. That raises the specter of people more easily gaming the system.

But even if AI is leading to higher rates of approving legitimate complaints with merit, there are unintended consequences. Higher costs, administrative overload, yes, but also a lower barrier to entry that may mean that better educated people who know how to use AI tools get even better odds of relief compared to those who don’t have those advantages.

AI tools can level the playing field sometimes, and perhaps over time this will lead to better government services. But in a world where AI-generated text becomes the norm, a poor or elderly or uneducated person with limited technical savvy or no access to a computer or internet connection is probably most worthy of support and least likely to get relief.

(It’s a bit like legitimate refugees who pay tens of thousands of dollars to make it to Europe; the ones who need help most remain invisible and stuck, while those who can bend the system to their advantage or afford to pay smugglers are more likely to get help. And in a world with finite resources, that can matter.)

So, how do we deal with this problem? How can we design better systems to effectively sort people without using effort as even a weak proxy for worthiness? Plenty of overloaded systems have started experimenting with fresh ideas already—and we should learn from their successes and failures.
