Count Binface and the Politics of Ridicule

Why authoritarian populism can't survive becoming a punchline

Authoritarian populists thrive on anger and fear. They demonise their opponents, threaten them with the politics of cruelty, and relish being seen as strongmen. Defeating them requires its own form of strength, a political resolve that is being tested across Western democracies under attack.

But sometimes, the greatest danger to authoritarian populists comes from something much more devastating than institutional constraints or mass protests: being turned into a punchline.

In Britain, that hypothesis will soon be tested in the funniest way imaginable.

I: Masterstroke?

A few days ago, Nigel Farage, the far-right populist leader of Reform UK, thought he had played a strategic masterstroke. His rivals imagined they had him on the back foot, but they had, perhaps, underestimated Farage’s wily political instincts.

Farage had found himself under attack from politicians and the press, with duelling scandals that threatened to derail his rapid political rise to leading the British polls. Allegations were swirling that he had failed to disclose a £5 million donation from British-Thai crypto-billionaire Christopher Harborne, aka “Chakrit Sakunkrit”—after which, in a totally unrelated decision, Farage decided to allegedly lobby economic bigwigs to loosen regulations around cryptocurrencies.

Further reports suggested Farage had also taken plenty of sweet cash from a criminal aristocrat, George Cottrell, aka “Posh George,” the scion of a soap empire who previously served prison time in the US after agreeing to launder money for FBI agents posing as drug traffickers in a sting operation.

Farage was struggling to contain the fallout from these metastasising scandals. So, rather than awaiting a decision from a parliamentary watchdog, he decided to grasp the nettle. Farage resigned from parliament, then declared his intention to immediately run again for his vacant seat.

The logic, Farage assumed, was bulletproof: he’d win in a landslide, vanquishing his partisan foes while showing the political establishment that the real people don’t care about his crypto-cash. Who will worry about parliamentary inquiries after the voters clearly show they aren’t worried about conflicts of interest from undeclared millions?

There was just one problem with Farage’s apparent masterstroke, a fly in the ointment, or more accurately, a pesky little “Recyclon” from the Planet Sigma IX who was about to descend on Clacton-on-Sea.

Farage hadn’t accounted for Count Binface.

II: From Lord Buckethead to Count Binface

British political humour is unparalleled in Western democracies.

Opportunity knocks for political comedy partly because candidates can run for office regardless of where they reside. That means that perennial longshot candidates can keep standing for parliament wherever they want, including in every random by-election. (A by-election is equivalent to an American special election.)

Since the 1980s, Lord Buckethead, a fictional character from the 1984 film Gremloids, repeatedly launched his candidacy for parliament. (Buckethead secured 131 votes in his race against Margaret Thatcher after campaigning to demolish Birmingham to make way for a futuristic space port.)

The character was dormant for years until 2017, when comedian Jon Harvey (a man with an unusual, moving past) contested the general election against Theresa May as Lord Buckethead.

On election night, British politicians, all the way up to the Prime Minister, must go through the humbling political spectacle of bundling onto the stage of, say, a local gymnasium, alongside every other candidate, no matter how absurd, while ballot results are announced.¹ This wonderful tradition yielded the following photo, which will surely be re-discovered with delight by an archival researcher in the year 2319:

However, the creator of Lord Buckethead claimed ownership of the character, forcing the arrival of a new persona. This intellectual property dispute is the birth story of Count Binface, an aspiring member of parliament / intergalactic space warrior who purports to be 5,900 years old.

A few weeks ago, Andy Burnham, who will soon be the new British prime minister, stood on stage alongside Binface and a fox as results were announced for the Makerfield by-election:

Now, all of this is done in good fun. Binface won 0.2 percent of the vote, hardly a serious contender. (The fox won 18 votes.) But in Nigel Farage’s constituency, all of the major parties have announced that they won’t contest the by-election since they see it as a blatant political stunt. Consequently, to borrow the obvious joke, the election is shaping up to be a showdown between just two main candidates: an absurd, over-the-top cartoon villain with garbage for ideas who is obsessed with aliens…and Count Binface.

The anti-Farage vote is now inevitably going to coalesce around Binface, meaning that there is a non-zero chance that Farage’s gambit could end in hilarious disaster, with the Recyclon beating the odds and prevailing at the ballot box. (For what it’s worth, the bookies are currently predicting a 19 percent chance that Binface will beat Farage, but if that possibility becomes perceived as realistic, the election could rapidly swing in his favour.)

Even if Farage narrowly wins, it will be a disastrous outcome for him—and for Reform UK. No crypto scandal could do the same level of political damage as being turned into an easily-memed punchline. (Don’t believe me? Lettuce ask Liz Truss.)

And Farage should be scared, too, as Count Binface is running a shrewd, policy-driven campaign. If elected, he promises to:

  • Force anyone who uses speakerphones on public transport to watch the movie Cats every day, for one year

  • Nationalise all model railways

  • Rename London Bridge as Phoebe Waller-Bridge

  • Move the hand dryer in the gent’s toilets at the Crown & Treaty pub in Uxbridge to a more sensible position

  • Nationalise Adele

  • Build “at least one” affordable house²

  • Force cyclists who break the highway code to ride unicycles

III: Barrels of Laughs and Dilemma Actions

But beneath the humour, there is a serious lesson for crafting effective resistance to far-right populists and authoritarian strongmen.

Authoritarians and far-right populists effortlessly brush off scandal, as accusations of corruption are merely fresh fuel for their perpetual motion persecution machine. Every press report of dodgy malfeasance is merely treated as further proof of an “establishment hit-job,” or “the deep state” being unleashed against the representatives of the “real” people.

Becoming a joke is far deadlier politically. Satire has been used to strike blows against dictators and demagogues for decades, including at the onset of World War II, with Charlie Chaplin’s The Great Dictator film in 1940.

Autocrats recognise the disarming power of comedy. Donald Trump, for example, regularly posts on social media about the humiliation of other countries laughing at the United States under his predecessors. As the jokes turn on him, from Saturday Night Live to late night comedians, he has launched what Andy Borowitz calls a “War on Laughter.” Using state power to prevent biting mockery isn’t unique to Trump; as Borowitz quips: “You might have noticed that there isn’t a thriving comedy scene in Pyongyang.”

Laughter is the opposite of fear. And so-called laughtivism can broaden the appeal of resistance movements, allowing them to recruit new members. It also forces snowflake strongmen such as Trump into a trap: If they threaten to crack down on comedy, they look even more ridiculous.

In political science research, this is known as a “dilemma action.”³ As the Serbian prankster and anti-authoritarian activist Srdja Popovic notes, the idea is to make it a lose-lose proposition between being laughed at or turning oneself into even more of a joke by over-reacting to amusing mockery. “If your opponent reacts, there will be a cost. If your opponent doesn’t react, there will be a cost,” Popovic explains.

In his own activism, Popovic found a rusty old barrel in Serbia, then painted the dictator’s face on it, and placed it in a busy shopping area. They put a big wooden club next to the barrel, then put a sign on the barrel that said “smash his face for a dinar.” At first, people were too scared, but eventually some joined in, laughing in delight at this subversive game. The police, unable to ignore the provocation, “arrested” the barrel, bundling it into custody, thereby producing photographs of authoritarian overreach that made the regime look ridiculous.

These comedy-based strategies are effective because they require no policy knowledge or political literacy to influence citizen attitudes. As a result, they can spread like wildfire, cutting across partisan divides and cutting through to the politically apathetic. Moreover, for regimes built around cults of personality—in which the leader is elevated to the status of a sort of bizarre demigod—jokes pierce that absurdist bubble.

In Turkey, President Recep Tayyip Erdoğan showcased the perils of overreacting to jokes when he used the whole weight of the Turkish state to prosecute a doctor who shared a meme that compared Erdogan’s appearance to that of Gollum, the unfortunate creature from The Lord of the Rings.

In a surreal twist, the judge on the case stated that he wasn’t familiar enough with the films to know whether the comparison was an insult, and commissioned an expert panel to adjudicate that question. (Peter Jackson, the director of the Lord of the Rings films, intervened on behalf of the doctor, arguing that the depictions in the meme were actually of Smeagol, the sympathetic, more innocent Hobbit-y incarnation of the soul that became Gollum.) Ultimately, the doctor lost his job but won the criminal case, with the net result that Erdogan looked absurd—far worse than if he had not reacted at all.

The great anti-authoritarian political theorist Hannah Arendt had it right: “The greatest enemy of authority, therefore, is contempt, and the surest way to undermine it is laughter.”⁴

IV: Could Binface Prevail?

In Clacton-on-Sea, Count Binface remains an electoral underdog. But as the traditional political saying goes, “never count out a Recyclon from Sigma IX before the trash is fully collected.”

Win or lose, Farage’s political masterstroke has backfired. No matter what he does, one of the biggest—and funniest—political news stories in the world over the next several weeks will be whether Binface can beat Farage, an outer space alien vs. a far-right populist who says Britain’s out of space for aliens.

Scheme as he might, it will now be Farage, not the man dressed as a bin, who’s seen as the joke in the race.
