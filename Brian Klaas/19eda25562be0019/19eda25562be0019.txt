I: Why does China have only one time zone?
China, which stretches across 3,100 miles of Asia from west to east, should have five time zones. Instead, spanning nearly sixty degrees of longitude, it has just one, known locally as Beijing Time.
In Kashgar, a strategically important oasis from the days of the Silk Road that lies on the western edge of China, solar noon typically occurs around 3pm. Because its neighbors have their clocks aligned more with the sun, a plane that takes off from Ürümqi can depart at 9am, fly for 90 minutes, and land at 8:30 am. The work day often starts at 10am and ends at 8pm. When it comes to distortions between clock time and solar time, nowhere is more extreme in its skew than western China.
It wasn’t always this way. From 1912 until the Chinese Communist Revolution in 1949, there were five Chinese time zones, each partitioned to appropriately match the sun. During the second Sino-Japanese War (1937 to 1945), a single unified time zone was adopted to ease logistical planning during wartime. This laid the groundwork for the sole time zone as a marker of cultural, political, and economic unity, with Mao at the center of a single nation, synchronised to the same clock.
Similarly, even though much of Spain is decidedly to the west of London, its clocks are an hour ahead, fixed to the same time as Germany. That’s no accident. Franco aligned Spanish time with Nazi time as a symbol of unity with Hitler’s Germany—and the country has never changed its clocks back, making its cultural quirk of dining late into the night far more rational.
Regardless of how time is measured and standardized, time zones are a relatively modern construct. We take them for granted, falsely believing they are a reasonably objective anchor for stabilizing our lives. 
The lesson from China or Spain is obvious: humans manipulate the measurement of time to their own ends. And that means that the choices we make in how we partition it are never neutral.
Time, itself, is slippery. Fundamental principles of modern physics yield bizarre revelations, such as time dilation [ https://substack.com/redirect/355614da-619c-4071-852f-232f1381bc93?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ], which quite literally means that your head is older than your feet. We exist in what William James called the “specious present,” a short, multi-second window of consciousness between memory and anticipation. 
Setting the bewildering physics aside (I’m looking at you, Block Universe theory [ https://substack.com/redirect/94de6a61-0e22-49c5-a007-3aa85096aefa?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ]), history has produced profound shifts in how time is experienced by humans. In Elizabethan plays, time is portrayed [ https://substack.com/redirect/a5ffbfc7-32f7-47e4-bf37-a445bd49496a?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] as “a devourer, a defacer, a bloody tyrant, a scythe,” that inevitably cuts everyone down. And yet, pre-modern societies—and our individual ancestors—were not slaves to the clock as we are, structuring every waking moment by the relentless ticking of watches, the smartphone despotism of calendars and appointments. Instead, early clocks were referred to as “the devil’s mill,” with haste seen as “a lack of decorum combined with diabolical ambition.”
Is it therefore possible that the way we measure time actually affects our perception of our own existence? Could the standardization of time, orchestrated astonishingly recently by the necessities of industrial capitalism, have transformed human lives from days in which time passes to days in which time is spent, a commodification of the brief spark that is life? 
As we’ll see, human decisions about how to segment time are an arbitrary, abrupt, and jarring departure from the lived experience of virtually all humans who came before us—and one that radically reshapes the architecture of our economies and societies while transforming our internalized sense of how we live our lives...
