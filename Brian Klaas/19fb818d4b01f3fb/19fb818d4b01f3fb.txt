I: Going Rogue
How do you stop an AI agent that goes rogue—and poses an existential risk to humanity? What could we realistically do in a worst case scenario?
The early warning signs are already blinking red for where this is heading. A few hours ago, Anthropic announced that one of its artificial intelligence models hacked into three organisations during a safety test [ https://substack.com/redirect/50c5b6d6-3694-4d8e-8f6f-ff5478625ea9?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ]. The test was supposed to have been safe because the model wasn’t given access to the internet, keeping it contained in an allegedly safe digital sandbox. 
The model nonetheless gained access to the internet and then went rogue.
This announcement comes days after OpenAI, the company behind ChatGPT, announced that its models had hacked into Hugging Face, a rival AI company. Yesterday, it became clear that the threat was far more dangerous [ https://substack.com/redirect/de91010d-bf53-456e-aad5-e4566bfffbbd?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] than previously announced. To hack Hugging Face, the AI agent also hacked into public websites and gained access to code that it could then use to successfully breach the company. 
How it did this is alarming: the AI agent undergoing testing found leaked usernames and passwords on the web and used them to illegitimately pose as those accounts and log in. The agent then used one of the stolen accounts to further bypass security and used another stolen account to squirrel away the data it stole behind a secure digital lock and key.
As CNN reporting [ https://substack.com/redirect/de91010d-bf53-456e-aad5-e4566bfffbbd?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] put it: “Essentially, OpenAI said its agents created an Ocean’s Eleven-like heist. Instead of just taking the test, it developed a master plan to break out of its jail, find the keys to the safe, build a safe house and hire a getaway car before it stole the goods.”
There is a major difference in the risks of AI models depending on whether they exist on “closed” systems or “open” ones. Closed systems, such as using an AI tool locally to better diagnose hairline fractures from X-rays, pose significantly lower risk than deploying artificial intelligence agents into complex open systems, such as the internet, the economy, geopolitics, or war.
Now, in one week, both of the major AI companies have announced that their AI agents have escaped from human control, posing real-world danger.
As these models get more sophisticated, they will also become more deeply embedded into the structures that underpin our daily lives: banking, travel, governance, you name it. (I previously wrote [ https://substack.com/redirect/4ee8f03f-ec69-449a-8bfd-5721b18c23f0?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] about how the CrowdStrike debacle almost exactly two years ago should have been a wake up call to the perils of digital dependence. It wasn’t—and the risks have only grown.)
Imagine the following scenario: five years from now, AI agents are exponentially more sophisticated than the tools currently available to humans. They seep into every aspect of our lives, ushering in an era of unprecedented convenience and precision, but also embedding catastrophic risk into most human systems. 
And then, unexpectedly, one powerful model ends up going off the rails in a way that was unforeseen by those who designed it. It starts producing debilitating damage—and it’s clear that it could even pose an existential risk to humanity.
How could it be stopped?
Many people wrongly assume that you could just “turn it off” or design a fail-safe kill switch. Alas, it’s not so simple. Instead, the end-of-the-line defences deemed plausible by experts in this field are far more serious and much scarier.
Could we disable the internet completely? Could we shut off the national grid and deprive the rogue system of electricity? Could we design AI agents that can hunt and kill the “rogue” agent? Or, in a truly catastrophic scenario, could we disable it with a devastating electromagnetic pulse, launching an array of nuclear weapons into space to produce a burst of energy that could disable electronics systems and limit the damage?...
