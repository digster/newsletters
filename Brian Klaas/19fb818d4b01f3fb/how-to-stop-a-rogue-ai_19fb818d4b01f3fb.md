As There is a major difference in the risks of AI models depending on whether they exist on “closed” systems or “open” ones. Closed systems, such as using an AI tool locally to better diagnose hairline fractures from X-rays, pose significantly lower risk than deploying artificial intelligence agents into complex open systems, such as the internet, the economy, geopolitics, or war. Now, in one week, both of the major AI companies have announced that their AI agents have escaped from human control, posing real-world danger. As these models get more sophisticated, they will also become more deeply embedded into the structures that underpin our daily lives: banking, travel, governance, you name it. (I Imagine the following scenario: five years from now, AI agents are exponentially more sophisticated than the tools currently available to humans. They seep into every aspect of our lives, ushering in an era of unprecedented convenience and precision, but also embedding catastrophic risk into most human systems. And then, unexpectedly, one powerful model ends up going off the rails in a way that was unforeseen by those who designed it. It starts producing debilitating damage—and it’s clear that it could even pose an existential risk to humanity. How could it be stopped? Many people wrongly assume that you could just “turn it off” or design a fail-safe kill switch. Alas, it’s not so simple. Instead, the end-of-the-line defences deemed plausible by experts in this field are far more serious and much scarier. Could we disable the internet completely? Could we shut off the national grid and deprive the rogue system of electricity? Could we design AI agents that can hunt and kill the “rogue” agent? Or, in a truly catastrophic scenario, could we disable it with a devastating electromagnetic pulse, launching an array of nuclear weapons into space to produce a burst of energy that could disable electronics systems and limit the damage?...
