Does Psychosis Make us Human?

The fine line between flourishing imagination and debilitating illness

“No great genius has ever existed without some touch of madness.”

—Aristotle, quoted in Seneca

Katharina Schmack, an effervescent neuroscientist and psychiatrist, divides her time between speaking to people with schizophrenia and trying to induce psychosis in mice. Neither is a straightforward task, but if she succeeds at making a mouse hear voices in its head, she may just help alleviate the life-destroying suffering of millions of people.

The questions she’s asking—and the answers that are coming back—are beginning to illuminate some of the darkest mysteries of our minds, with profound lessons for what truly makes us human.

I: Of Mice and Men

Psychosis is characterised by unfounded perceptions and beliefs—formally defined as a mental state wherein perception detaches from a shared reality.

The first major category of psychosis refers to hallucinations. Some people see visions. Others feel phantom sensations (if you’ve ever felt your phone buzz when it didn’t, that is a tactile hallucination). But the largest category of hallucinations for psychosis are auditory: the people who hear voices. For some people, this inescapable soundtrack can destroy any semblance of a normal life, threatening or provoking violence, causing immense stress and anxiety, or simply narrating everyday tasks without any respite.

The second category arrive with unfounded beliefs in the form of delusions. Some experience paranoia, the notion that some force, seen or unseen, is out to get them; for others, they may have delusions of grandeur. Both hallucinations and delusions are disorienting, confusing, and often scary, the unsettling rupture between one’s vividly lived experience and the shared reality with the outside world.

Unfortunately for Schmack, there is no instruction manual for triggering psychosis in a mouse. Beyond the obvious ethical limits of any study involving living animals, there are two main problems. The first is that known environmental contributors, such as cannabis use, merely increase the likelihood of an already rare condition. “You’d need a lot of mice to get one to become psychotic,” she tells me, sitting on an ergonomic orb at her desk at the Francis Crick Institute in London.

As I picture the surreal possible montage of thousands of lab mice with the munchies, Schmack moves onto another problem. Even if you could induce psychosis, how would you know what a mouse is experiencing? Even measuring frenetic movement wouldn’t do much; how would you infer whether that mouse’s mind was experiencing something similar to the inner life of a person with schizophrenia? You can, should you so wish, ask a mouse if it routinely hears phantom squeaks inside its head, but you’re unlikely to get a satisfactory or reliable answer.

Thank goodness that scientists, including Schmack, are a clever bunch. With an innovative methodology, they’ve already solved the mouse reporting problem. In her research, she has devised a test that can reliably measure whether a mouse is experiencing hallucinations. And it turns out that many of the mice, like humans, already do hallucinate naturally—but the differences in the way that they hallucinate may tell us something profound about ourselves.

II: Pearls, Perceptions, and Priors

At 7:02 am on December 7, 1941, Private Joseph Lockard saw an enormous blob appear on his truck-mounted radar screen. This was a newfangled technology, but the readings were clear: lots of planes were headed toward Oahu, coming from the north. Lockard and his colleague, Private George Elliott, called it in to Lieutenant Kermit Tyler.

Bewildered, Tyler couldn’t imagine that the reading was genuine; it was so unexpected, so at odds with what he believed to be a realistic possibility. The warnings were swiftly dismissed, chalked up as probably nothing, most likely some American B-17s that had somehow gone off their planned course. Fifty-three minutes later, 183 aircraft in the first wave of the attack on Pearl Harbor struck.

In another incident, 42 years later, a Soviet nuclear early warning officer named Stanislav Petrov detected a small array of incoming nukes on his screen. The evidence was clear. The Americans were attacking. But there was a puzzle that perplexed him. Why would they attack with just a handful of nukes? Every prior belief he had about any nuclear attack involved an all-out onslaught. The evidence just didn’t match, so he white-knuckled it and decided to wait rather than report it, lest he trigger a nuclear war through a false alarm.

These are cautionary tales about the delicate interplay between the information we get about the world and the possibilities we can imagine about reality. Is it possible that psychosis occurs when the fragile balance—between perception and prior beliefs about how the world is supposed to be—goes haywire?

At Pearl Harbor, real evidence was confidently explained away because of prior beliefs about the implausibility of a looming mass surprise attack. By contrast, in Petrov’s case, he didn’t trust the false evidence and instead relied on his mental model of the world to decide how to act.

Psychosis is, in some ways, the worst possible blend of these two dynamics: false perceptions can be so vivid and subjectively real that they can override accurate prior beliefs, including the rational knowledge that voices aren’t really speaking inside your head. Or, prior beliefs about hallucinations can become so engrained that they can drown out accurate sensory data coming in through the eyes and ears.

But there’s a wrinkle. The neuroscientist Anil Seth argues, correctly, that our perceptions are always somewhat false. “We're all hallucinating all the time,” he says. “When we agree about our hallucinations, we call it reality.”

He’s referring to the disorienting fact that our inner world, our umwelt, is a necessary byproduct of the unique sensory organs that evolution has seen fit to deliver to us. The umwelt of a dog or a dolphin or a bat or a peacock mantis shrimp is totally unlike our own, though we may never understand what it’s like to be one of those creatures.

As I’ve previously written, drawing on the work of Donald Hoffman’s “Fitness Beats Truth” theorem, these species-specific hallucinations that we call reality are forged through the wisdom of evolved survival mechanisms that help us navigate the world most effectively:

Imagine two creatures: we might call them the Truth Creature and the Shortcut Creature. The Truth Creature sees everything exactly as it is, able to visually perceive every molecule of oxygen, every stream of ultraviolet light, each atom within every bacterium lurking under each toenail.

Every possible fragment of visual information is perceived and processed by the Truth Creature’s brain. Nothing goes unnoticed. By contrast, the Shortcut Creature can’t see any of that detail, but instead only perceives and processes that which is most useful to it. All else is either ignored or is invisible to that creature’s perceptions. As a result, the Shortcut Creature cannot sense most of reality.

Which creature would you rather be?

We are tempted to side with the truth. But that would be a fatal mistake. Shortcut Creatures always win.

Once you realise how our perceptions are shaped by our sensory hardware, it’s easy to understand that our lived experience is a “manifest image” of reality, a bit like a computer desktop is a hallucinated visual image that allows us to usefully navigate a complex tangle of copper and silicon. Similarly, our bodies and brains filter out the explosion of superfluous information out there in the world, preserving most pristinely that which is most useful to us to help us survive. Evolution ruthlessly culled Truth Creatures in favour of those who use selective perception to become masters of the useful shortcut.

And, as with smoke detectors, precautionary false alarms are often less dangerous than missing a real fire. There may therefore be a survival advantage in over-detecting false positives.

But that nonetheless yields a puzzle for understanding psychosis through the lens of evolutionary theory, which is broadly based on selecting traits that make someone survive long enough to successfully reproduce. People with schizophrenia, for example, tend to have shortened life expectancies by ten or twenty years. And, as the below chart from a study of 1950s data in Finland shows, men and women with schizophrenia have far fewer children, on average.

Why, then, would that hyper-efficient engine of evolution preserve a trait, such as debilitating hallucinations, irrational delusions, or schizophrenia, when they seem so overwhelmingly detrimental to both survival and reproductive success?

III: The Mice Who Hear Things

To meet Katharina Schmack near King’s Cross in London, I venture through a secure airlock-style security door at the Francis Crick Institute. The facility houses a Biosafety Level 4 lab—the highest grade, reserved for scientific research on the world’s most dangerous pathogens. Her office, a corner glass box overlooking a tangle of central London construction and more familiar monuments, is an oasis of calm, as she explains to me the research method that allows her to ingeniously understand whether a mouse can hear things that aren’t really there.

The mice are first trained to associate a reward with hearing a tone. Sound. Reward. Sound. Reward. Repeat.

There is, however, a random time gap between the two, such that the mouse might immediately get rewarded or might have to wait for it. It is possible to observe whether the mouse is pausing and waiting for their anticipated reward or simply going about their business. The key, then, is to see how often mice start anticipating a reward without a prior sound, which would be the mouse equivalent of human auditory hallucinations.

Even more interesting, Schmack and her colleagues can measure how confident the mouse is that the sound was played based on how long they’re willing to wait around. When they hear a sound loud and clear, mice are willing to invest time into waiting for the reward rather than pursuing other behaviours. But if they’re not confident that the tone was played, they’ll move on more quickly. Through this method, the researchers can determine that mice do indeed hallucinate and can determine how often they experience those false perceptions with a high degree of subjective confidence.

With this breakthrough of measurement, they can try to figure out what can increase the rates of hallucination. This is essential because the underlying mechanisms of psychosis are poorly understood, so to unpick the mysteries of the mind they need to be able to see what makes it better or worse.

For some forms of psychosis, known as primary psychosis (which includes schizophrenia and bipolar disorder), it’s completely unclear what causes it. For other forms, known as secondary psychosis, scientists and doctors may know that it was caused by a head injury, for example, but still lack the mechanistic understanding of what’s actually going on in the brain.

This near-complete lack of understanding extends to the treatments, too. The first antipsychotic medication, chlorpromazine, was intended to help as an anaesthetic during surgery. In the 1950s, a French surgeon named Henri Laborit noticed that its use produced an unexpected calm in patients who received it, prompting him to convince colleagues to test it on those with severe psychosis. It worked to a remarkable degree, albeit with significant side effects. Now, seven decades later, antipsychotic medications have improved, but much remains unclear. They work on dopamine receptors, but beyond that, “We have no idea why it works or why it produces the specific side effects,” Schmack tells me.

But with the mice research, they suddenly had an important clue. The rates of hallucination in the mice went up when there were elevated levels of striatal dopamine. Crucially, if they artificially stimulated those dopamine receptors, they could boost hallucinations. Finally, there was causal evidence of a link between specific brain chemistry and the experience of psychosis-like states.

Schmack started to wonder: how much closer could they get to understanding exactly what was going on in the brain when psychosis strikes? That question, once answered, could be the breakthrough that unlocks the scientific cabinet of currently unknown cures.

IV: Schizophrenia in the Immune System?

Modern perceptions, rather unhelpfully, contain a sharp divide between physical health and mental health, as though the mind exists in an unknown ether detached from the body it’s housed within. But as scientists begin to understand more about psychosis, that divide is quickly shrinking into oblivion.

A little over a decade ago, a 67 year-old man with leukaemia had no viable options for a stem cell transplant other than one donated from his brother, who had schizophrenia. With death as an alternative, the transplant went ahead. While it helped with the leukaemia, the man developed severe psychosis four months later.

In the opposite direction, a 24 year-old man with treatment-resistant schizophrenia received a bone marrow transplant from a healthy donor. Soon, he showed a “remarkable reduction in psychotic symptoms” and a “drastic improvement in social functioning.”

These are anecdotes, not systematic medical trials, but they are part of a growing body of evidence that psychosis is tied, at least in part, to the immune system. Of particular interest are NMDA receptors, which are like little communication gateways on certain neurons. Intriguingly, in a rare condition known as anti-NMDA receptor encephalitis, antibodies attack these receptors, often producing significant spikes in hallucinations and delusions.

Schmack’s lab has just published preliminary research showing that when they induce these changes in mice, by triggering an autoimmune response that attacks the NMDA receptors, the mice experience elevated rates of psychosis, which is strong validating evidence. (Relatedly, there is plenty of documented evidence of psychosis onset after an acute infection in humans.)

The lab in London hasn’t yet solved these mysteries, but with the help of Schmack’s newfound ability to reliably detect mice hallucinations, scientists are—for the first time—knocking on a door that’s a gateway to some of the deepest mysteries of the human mind. And, as with any scientific endeavour that touches on life’s biggest questions, they’re beginning to see astonishing ideas through that initial keyhole.

V: Seething Brains of Lovers and Madmen

Schmack, speaking excitedly in her office, tells me about her fascination with the correlations in data that show links between psychosis traits and creativity.

While those with schizophrenia may not show elevated rates of thriving in creative professions (because their illness often reduces the rates of holding any “normal” job), their relatives do show a higher propensity to be engaged in creative work. And in her personal experience working with patients who have psychosis, she has noticed a dazzling artistic ability, complete with vivid details that may transport the viewer into experiencing another reality.

She points to a pivot point in the story of our species, when we co-existed with Neanderthals, but then we may have started to do something different: develop spirituality with burial objects, dabble with paintings and cave art, engage in arcane rituals, and eventually, play with precursors to sophisticated language. Even if a mouse can hallucinate, then, is it perhaps a completely different phenomenon because of the sophistication of our minds?

After all, there’s already strong evidence within humans that the experience of psychosis can be driven not just by brain architecture, but by culture.

The anthropologist Tanya Luhrmann studied people who heard voices in California, India, and Ghana. The Americans described the voices with clinical language, all described them in negative terms as part of their illness, and frequently reported violent language, including calls to “take their eye out with a fork, or cut someone’s head and drink their blood.”

By contrast, in India, most people experienced the voices as the guidance of an ancestor or relative, processed by the person as a sort of teacher. In Ghana, the voices were treated by the individuals as spirits, or as God. They still sometimes said to do terrible or strange things, but the experience was more positive and relational. The revelation is clear: hallucinations and perceptions unbounded from shared reality will manifest and be experienced differently based on human culture. What, then, can we say about the role that psychosis has played across our species in contrast to others?

Humans, perhaps uniquely, can imagine a drastically different world from our own lived reality. While we can never know for sure, it’s unlikely that a chihuahua or a cockroach is conjuring up wildly different possible worlds inside its own mind, dreaming of alternative futures, its thoughts overrun by thickets of tangled counterfactuals. Imagination, by definition, is when our mental world detaches itself from sensory evidence and toys with something that has not (yet) come to be.

Innovation and strategic forward thinking are thus both unleashed through creative conjuring that stretches beyond the visceral world of our immediate senses. Once that premise is accepted, it’s no far leap to ponder whether the trait of creativity—that human powerhouse of idea-making and world-building—comes with a potential evolutionary cost.¹

By introducing the neurological ability to take risks and explore unrealised possibilities, perhaps the inevitable byproduct of that trait is a risk of it being revved into overdrive, yielding debilitating and life-crushing sorrow through psychosis.

This is not to romanticise a terrible scourge that contorts the lives of countless millions. But it is to suggest that psychosis may be the unfortunate outgrowth of our carefully evolved minds, as that which makes us most human can drift beyond control and invite colossal human suffering. It is perhaps, as Shakespeare portrayed it in A Midsummer Night’s Dream, that thinnest of lines between lunatic, lover, and poet:

Lovers and madmen have such seething brains
Such shaping fantasies, that apprehend 
More than cool reason ever comprehends. 
The lunatic, the lover and the poet 
Are of imagination all compact: 
One sees more devils than vast hell can hold
That is, the madman: the lover, all as frantic, 
Sees Helen’s beauty in a brow of Egypt: 
The poet’s eye, in fine frenzy rolling, 
Doth glance from heaven to earth, from earth to heaven; 
And as imagination bodies forth
The forms of things unknown, the poet’s pen 
Turns them to shapes and gives to airy nothing
