Two decades ago, a 26 year-old schoolteacher named Kate Bainbridge came down with flu-like symptoms from a mysterious viral infection. Three days later, she was in a coma. Her brain was inflamed; she was unresponsive.
After a few weeks, the infection cleared, the coma ended, but she was diagnosed as being in a vegetative state. There were no signs that she was aware of her surroundings or that she was experiencing anything at all. Her eyes could be open, but the prevailing wisdom was that the lights had gone out inside her mind, a living physical being, but no longer a sentient one.
There were occasional movements and even [occasional outbursts](https://substack.com/redirect/2ab3760c-b173-47bf-a4f2-85b63d8b26f6?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o), but medical staff had chalked that up to bodily reflexes, the unthinking spasms of an intact body containing an obliterated mind.
Thankfully for Kate, she was in Cambridge, England, and became [the first patient](https://substack.com/redirect/c8a6e1ee-5440-4f2b-be79-f3573e45a2bc?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o) in a Cambridge research group on vegetative states. They scanned her brain and found astonishing results:
“Not only did Kate react to faces: but her brain responses were indistinguishable from those of healthy volunteers.” When she was shown faces of people she knew, her fusiform gyrus would light up, just as with any other healthy person.
Six months later, Kate emerged from her vegetative state, allowing her to explain the terror she experienced. As the BBC reported:
“They said I could not feel pain,” she says. “They were so wrong.” Sometimes she’d cry out, but the nurses thought it was just a reflex. She felt abandoned and helpless. Hospital staff had no idea how much she suffered in their care. Kate found physiotherapy scary: nurses never explained what they were doing to her. She was terrified when they removed mucus from her lungs. “I can’t tell you how frightening it was, especially suction through the mouth,” she has written. At one point, her pain and despair became so much that she tried to snuff out her life by holding her breath. “I could not stop my nose from breathing, so it did not work. My body did not seem to want to die.”
Kate Bainbridge’s experience is harrowing, a cautionary tale of the extreme but inadvertent horrors that can be perpetrated when we wrongly imagine that another being isn’t sentient and treat them according to that false belief.¹
But what if the same thing is happening, at scale, with most animals?
In the diverse menagerie of life, it’s easy for most of us to imagine that chimpanzees and dogs have sophisticated inner lives, capable of experiencing pleasure and pain, joy and suffering.
But the certainty of our beliefs about other minds grow murkier as we drift further away from human-like animals and depart altogether from the world of mammals. Is there a dividing line? If so, where is it?
Does a bird have an inner life? How about an octopus or a lobster or a sardine? Does that pesky fly, zooming annoyingly around you, have subjective experiences? Can a worm feel pain?...
