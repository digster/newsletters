The IKEA Effect: Why Effort Still Counts

In 2014, I signed my first solo apartment lease on a tiny 400 square foot studio.

It was in Palo Alto, so I'd be paying way too much for way too little, but I was thrilled. The functional benefit was that it was close to the office where I'd be working my first job. More importantly, I'd be living on my own in a place I'd be paying for with my own money.

I was proud of it.

I got the keys on the morning of August 23rd, a Saturday, and immediately made the first apartment pilgrimage to the local IKEA to get a few pieces of furniture.

I got a bed, a dresser, a small table, and one chair. I think it all cost about $350 in total.

I spent the rest of that Saturday building all of the items. I'm not particularly handy, so I can't say I did the best job. There were definitely a few crooked pieces and stripped screws along the way, but I got it done.

As I sat there that evening, eating dinner on a paper plate at my tiny table, I had a big smile on my face. It was genuinely one of the most free moments of my life.

That night, in the middle of the night, I woke up to my bed shaking violently. I actually looked under the bed, convinced in my half-sleeping state that there must have been someone playing a prank on me. But after a few seconds, it stopped, and I fell right back asleep.

When I woke up the next morning, I had about 100 unread texts from my parents. Evidently, the shaking had been a 6.0 magnitude earthquake that had rocked the area.

But oddly enough, my first reaction wasn't fear or relief.

I looked around the tiny apartment, saw that all of my janky furniture was still standing, and felt an immense pride settle over me.

I built that. Earthquake proof.

That furniture is all long gone now—I've graduated to "nicer" brands and pay professionals to build it—but I still think of those few items as the best, most highly valued furniture I've ever owned.

And as it turns out, there's real science behind what I felt. Science that extends well beyond your home furnishings and into every realm of your life.

In 2011, behavioral scientists Michael Norton, Dan Ariely, and Daniel Mochon ran a series of experiments to assess how people value things they've built themselves.

In one study, they had participants fold origami frogs and cranes from a simple set of instructions. Then they asked the participants what their creation was worth, before asking a group of neutral strangers what they'd pay for the same creation.

The participants valued their own creation roughly 5x more highly than the neutral strangers had.

In a funny extension, the participants actually viewed their own creations as being equivalent in value to those of a real origami expert.

In another study, the researchers split participants into two groups: builders and non-builders. The builders had to assemble a very simple IKEA storage box, folding it from its flat-packed state into a box, while the non-builders received an already assembled box.

When asked the price they'd be willing to pay to keep the box, the builders were willing to pay 63% more than the non-builders.

The researchers called it the IKEA Effect:

You place significantly more value on things you put effort into creating.

And a simple variation on that second study revealed the most important wrinkle:

One group was allowed to finish building the storage box while the other group was forced to stop just shy of completion.

The finishing group predictably valued the box highly. However, the researchers observed that the effect vanished for those who stopped just before the build was completed.

In other words, the act of completing the task was important. It wasn't effort alone, but effort carried all the way through to the finish line.

But this newsletter isn’t really about furniture...

The IKEA Effect is the underlying foundation behind everything you treasure in life.

You don't value the things that came easy. You value the things you've earned. Through struggle. Through effort. Through resilience and adaptability. The things you've seen all the way through to the finish.

The business you've built after surviving the ups and downs of its early years. The friendship you've forged by showing up for the person during their darkest hours. The body you've built through hours of discipline. The peace you've cultivated through uncomfortable stillness. The partnership you've grown through hard conversations.

You don't value these things despite the effort they required. You value them because of the effort they required.

You don't run a marathon for the medal at the finish and the time on a screen. You wouldn't feel a thing if you hopped in a car at the start, drove 26 miles, and then ran across the line to the cheers of the crowd. Because you didn't earn it.

The joy is in the struggle. The real satisfaction is in the effort. You run a marathon because of who you become through the process of training for it and painful act of running it.

And the harsh truth in all of this is that the modern world often feels hollow or meaningless because we've quietly optimized the friction out of our lives.

Everything is one-click, on-demand, done-for-you. No wonder we feel so unattached. Because effort sustains us.

We're living in a desert of our own creation.

But you can stage your own tiny rebellion:

  • Choose the uncomfortable path. Do uncomfortable things sooner. The delayed conversation gets harder. The ignored problem gets bigger. The small repair gets more expensive. Peace is never found through avoidance. It’s found through direct early action. Easy now, hard later. Hard now, easy later.
  • Finish what you started. Just finish things. The world has enough half-written books, half-built businesses, and half-kept promises. Show up. Close loops. Do what you said.
  • Build something with your hands. Make something for your home. Work on a garden. Put together a treehouse with your kids. Take on a small project.

Your entire life will change the moment you realize it’s not supposed to be easy. The most valuable things in life are hard to earn. That’s precisely why they’re so valuable.

My janky furniture is long gone, but the feeling I got when I looked at it that morning never left me.

Chase things that make you feel that same way. Chase what must be earned.
