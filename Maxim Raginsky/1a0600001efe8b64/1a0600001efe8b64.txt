Last week I was one of the panelists in the forum on The Tension Between Control and AI [ https://substack.com/redirect/2f578f5e-d686-49b2-a736-8462deb16f1f?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] at the 2026 IFAC World Congress (the congress took place in Busan, South Korea, but our Fall semester just started, so I was only able to participate via Zoom). The forum was organized by Robert Bitmead [ https://substack.com/redirect/c7bbc3c9-5d1e-41bf-9f3a-39a6664f2bfa?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] (UCSD) and Victor Solo [ https://substack.com/redirect/122ce88b-f713-43db-92d7-1da95e65978a?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] (UNSW Sidney). Each of the panelists was asked to present a six-minute position statement, and then the floor was opened for questions form the audience. What follows below is a loose reconstructionx of my remarks.
The frenetic pace of innovation in AI has led to a great deal of soul-searching in the control community. These identity crises seem to be a recurring theme. Forty years ago, in 1986, at the suggestion of the leadership of the IEEE Control Systems Society, a workshop was organized at the University of Santa Clara to discuss the challenges to control arising from the rapid growth of computer science. Some of the position papers presented at the workshop were published in the IEEE Control Systems Magazine in 1987. The position paper by Murray Wonham [ https://substack.com/redirect/bcf95648-4f3c-4616-95e8-9c6556960f76?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] struck an optimistic note. Wonham argued that, instead of viewing the rise of computer science as a threat, the control community should embrace it as an opportunity to both reinvent itself and to reassert the longevity of its conceptual foundations by fashioning a “new control science” and a “new control scientist.” He urged control theorists to read E.W. Dijkstra’s A Discipline of Programming so they can learn to recognize all of the key concepts of their discipline—input/output maps, states, stability, controllability, observability, etc.—even in the unfamiliar form refracted through the prism of computation.
I want to follow a similar route, but temper the optimism with a dose of pragmatism and caution. I’ll start by quoting a remarkably prescient passage from a 2001 paper by Roger Brockett [ https://substack.com/redirect/04758572-94da-4393-af68-2503747b495e?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] (emphasis mine):
Greater functionality usually means greater complexity and the standard way to deal with complexity is to introduce levels of abstraction. An important device used by humans to deal with this is the introduction of tokens which encapsulate a number of more detailed descriptions. When we substitute the phrase, “Get the milk from the refrigerator.” for a detailed description of the motion of the limbs required to get the milk from the refrigerator we achieve an enormous savings in complexity. If machines are to make use of higher levels of abstraction we must design and analyze machines that can process symbolic strings as well as the more familiar analog signals.
Brockett was one of the pioneers of what we now call “neurosymbolic systems.” He wrote numerous papers on embedding discrete systems like automata or computational primitives like sorting a list in continuous-time dynamics. He was interested in developing new frameworks for reasoning about robotic control by synthesizing the theory of formal languages and controlled differential equations. I don’t want to suggest that this was a missed opportunity [ https://substack.com/redirect/8976ce11-b20e-4245-8fba-c1b87c2f4d15?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] for control theorists to invent the transformer architecture, but I do think that we have all the conceptual and technical tools to make the best use of it now. And, just to drive the point home, I want to quote another prescient passage from the last page of Brockett’s paper (again, emphasis mine):
interesting systems often display an important dependence on the higher level tasks that are being performed. Psychologists often try to describe this dependence using ideas relating to attention. … It may be best to think of perceptual attention as a vector characterizing the direction in which lies the most relevant data. 
At any rate, the idea of interconnecting neural net modules via the linguistic interface of tokens is now mainstream. Taking a cue from natural language that can function in both descriptive and prescriptive modes, we use elaborate scripts to orchestrate the interaction between these modules and their environment. Most of the time, we do not pay much attention to internal activation dynamics, as long as the systems function as intended. But one of the ironies of LLMs is how much attention we must pay to carefully crafting the rules of engagement for the agents. While the complexity of internal activations in LLMs still exceeds the complexity of prompts in absolute terms, we are learning (sometimes the hard way) that there are plenty of gaps in the open texture of natural language [ https://substack.com/redirect/b20700c0-7598-4c1e-8e27-0d1d3b97b6ad?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] for all sorts of intended meanings and formal specifications to slip through. This applies not only to multi-page prompts that are starting to resemble legal contracts, but also to all of the baroque textual artifacts [ https://substack.com/redirect/b31b353f-ba7f-49b7-84f3-8f241a008f7c?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] the agents leave in the wake of their interactions with one another. In other words, we are confronted with the infamous frame problem [ https://substack.com/redirect/489f6810-8aad-49f4-88c5-9312d290c763?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] that plagued GOFAI.
The frame problem was posed by John McCarthy [ https://substack.com/redirect/fc4a60b0-3dab-4e77-85cc-b23a4ffe0b87?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] and Pat Hayes [ https://substack.com/redirect/64ffa3a1-a33c-45e2-82b8-63a75aaf9dec?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] in 1969 as follows:
A computer program capable of acting intelligently in the world must have a general representation of the world in terms of which its inputs are interpreted. Designing such a program requires commitments about what knowledge is and how it is obtained. Thus, some of the major traditional problems of philosophy arise in artificial intelligence.
Interestingly, control theorists were aware of these issues as well. For example, when Howard Rosenbrock [ https://substack.com/redirect/95d64b4d-5b0e-478e-b50d-fe1d1ce05592?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] was presenting his perspective on the future of control engineering in 1977, he wanted to make sure that, as system designers were relying more and more on computer-based design, they would pay attention to the problem of writing down system specifications without getting bogged down in an infinite regress of caveats or, worse, in a morass of mutually contradictory or inconsistent requirements:
We cannot usually specify what we want in any exact way. There will be many desirable properties: speed of response, reliability, insensitivity to disturbances and parameter changes, and so on. … To write down all these constraints is usually impossible however long the engineer spends he will certainly omit some. Moreover it is a very burdensome and time-consuming process to attempt the construction of such a list. 
To AI software agents loosed upon the Internet, anything that is not explicitly forbidden is allowed [ https://substack.com/redirect/cc0f0851-2fbe-4f73-a765-9fa915ea7a8f?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ]. This has nothing to do with “misalignment” or “subgoals” or “instrumental convergence” or whatever pseudoscientific terms the Bay Area rationalists throw at the wall. It’s just the frame problem rearing its head again, and we don’t have a satisfactory solution to it now just like they didn’t have it in the days of GOFAI. 
At the same time that we are grappling with the old frame problem, we are also grappling with another old problem: deskilling of humans. While engineering design has a nontrivial algorithmic component, it also relies crucially on “clinical” knowledge, an intuitive feel for what is important and what is of lesser significance, which can take years of experience to acquire. Having a well-honed intuition is a big part of a control engineer’s skill, something that one can (and should) take professional pride in. This is happening now across many fields, as companies like Mercor are hiring laid-off skilled professionals in increasingly precarious arrangements [ https://substack.com/redirect/de82b517-5dd7-468d-aff3-096fa1714c13?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] to help train the very AI systems that will one day be used to automate their skills. Rosenbrock was keenly aware of the specter of deskilling haunting the engineering profession in 1977, and so was Lisanne Bainbridge [ https://substack.com/redirect/50fffd57-97b7-4ff4-ab84-2bcc655a0982?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] when she wrote in 1983 that
the increased interest in human factors among engineers reflects the irony that the more advanced a control system is, so the more crucial may be the contribution of the human operator. … The level of skill that a worker has is also a major aspect of his status, both within and outside the working community. If the job is ‘deskilled’ by being reduced to monitoring, this is difficult for the individuals involved to come to terms with. It also leads to the ironies of incongruous pay differentials, when the deskilled workers insist on a high pay level as the remaining symbol of a status which is no longer justified by the job content.
Just like Bainbridge, Rosenbrock was also sharply critical of the misguided belief that engineering can be fully automated and decoupled from human and social concerns:
A belief that engineering reaches its highest development when it can be explained as a sequence of logical steps. A belief that engineering problems can be expressed in a closed form, then solved by an algorithmic procedure, and that these two steps are independent and consecutive. A belief, finally, that the engineer’s view should be bounded by technology and mathematics, and should stop short of social and human questions.
It is this which I have referred to as a loss of nerve. Algorithmic, mathematical, ‘scientific’ techniques have great power and intellectual appeal, but they are only one aspect of engineering. We should pay equal regard to the cultivation of its other aspects. This does not mean that we should neglect the algorithmic aspects in design; but they can be used either in a way that eliminates the designer’s skills, or in a way that assists those skills and makes them more productive.
All of these concerns are more pressing than ever. AI systems are not going anywhere any time soon; in fact, they are becoming more tightly integrated into various critical infrastructures. This means that control engineers must preserve at all costs one of the central tenets of their profession—namely, what Gunter Stein [ https://substack.com/redirect/14cca92d-bee2-4a50-bb18-5e98e65f4e28?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] referred to “respecting the unstable” in his 2003 Bode Lecture:
As society permits control engineers to operate more such dangerous systems, we who teach those engineers and fashion their tools cannot hide from responsibility under a cloak of mathematics. We dare not instill the notion that mathematical rigor is the only goal to strive for in control. We must also instill respect for the practical, physical consequences of control, and we must make certain that its underlying principles are taught clearly and well.
Stein used the Chernobyl disaster as one of the salient examples of things going catastrophically wrong when system operators fail to take into account the trade-offs and risks inherent in controlling tightly coupled complex systems poised at the edge of instability. But instability can come in many varieties. For example, Jacob Bruggerman, in his insightful dissection of the OpenAI/HuggingFace hacking incident [ https://substack.com/redirect/a593c9bc-b33b-47f9-8b7b-037ced5f69a8?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ], draws historical parallels to the 1988 Morris Worm [ https://substack.com/redirect/826858c0-f0ee-4cb2-a7b3-9575771d7f21?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ], the first instance of a massive software-induced instability due to a rapidly self-replicating piece of code that nearly brought down the ARPANET [ https://substack.com/redirect/26867c1d-f55b-45e8-bb4e-9bd0bfe0380a?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ]. He classifies the OpenAI hacking exploit as a “normal accident,” a term he borrows from the work of sociologist Charles Perrow [ https://substack.com/redirect/4e630e3e-0ed0-44a0-984d-979ea7d96dd8?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ], who argued that the increasing complexity and interconnectedness of modern engineered systems makes such catastrophic failures inevitable. As more and more critical control functions are entrusted to AI systems, it is imperative that we don’t forget Gunter Stein’s warning. This ethos of “respecting the unstable” could be one of the crucial contributions of control to AI, injecting a dose of reality into the glib triumphalism of slogans like “software is eating the world [ https://substack.com/redirect/4d5c6713-c3fb-4577-9990-9c4dfda8fac4?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ]” or “everything is computer [ https://substack.com/redirect/9e60f3ba-559d-49c4-be59-b3e61a2f9e9f?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ]” and a dose of sober pragmatism to counteract the learned [ https://substack.com/redirect/30483192-99a3-4910-8192-39780df1087b?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] helplessness [ https://substack.com/redirect/c8514ad6-b54b-4160-acf1-ba96d4f08ce2?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] of the titans of the AI industry.
Gunter Stein also emphasized the importance of teaching “the underlying principles” of control “clearly and well.” Just like Murray Wonham, who argued that the conceptual foundations of control theory are broad enough to be meaningful in computer science, I believe that the same holds in the context of AI. Concepts like compositionality, layering, state, feedback, stability, controllability, observability, system equivalence, the internal model principle, etc. etc. all have their counterparts in AI. Control is the art and science of orchestrating effective interaction via interconnection; control systems trade off the complexity of internal system organization against externally perceived complexity. Our expertise is still as valuable as ever, and we have to look back and appreciate the longevity of the key questions as we look forward to crafting a new science of control, 2026 edition.
