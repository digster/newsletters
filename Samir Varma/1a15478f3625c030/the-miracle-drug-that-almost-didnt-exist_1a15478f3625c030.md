My grandmother was a freedom fighter.
Not metaphorically. Literally. She grew up in Lahore as a Brahmin, fell in love with the guy who wasn’t one, the one who played violin under her window, and married him anyway. (If you’ve been following the India series, you know how that story goes.) She joined the independence movement. He kept working with the authorities. India contains multitudes. She was effective enough at it that the British took notice, and not in a good way. Her story is in [this book](https://substack.com/redirect/bb0d07ad-4a60-4578-a27f-3c9f78df2756?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o), if you want the details.
She also had migraines. Brutal ones. Her entire life.
She managed anyway. Raised a family, fought for freedom, built something lasting, all while her head tried to kill her on a regular basis. But “managing anyway” means operating at a fraction of your capacity. It means losing days, weeks, months to pain. It means the counterfactual version of your life, the one where you didn’t have an icepick behind your eye three times a week, remains forever unlived.
What could she have accomplished without them? We’ll never know.
The genes, as genes do, skipped a generation. They landed on me.
I’ve had migraines since I was about six years old. I’m sure I had them earlier, but I was too young to remember. Memory is a funny thing: you can’t recall the pain itself, only the fact of it. What I remember is the pattern: the aura, the nausea, the retreat to dark rooms, the whole catastrophe.
Over the decades, they got worse. By 1999 they were almost intolerable.
By 2000, I couldn’t look at a standard CRT monitor. Not “I got headaches from monitors.” I mean: instant migraine. The refresh rate, the flicker, something about those cathode rays: thirty seconds of exposure and I was done for the day.
Okay, I thought. Technology moves fast. LCDs are coming. Problem solved.
By 2005, even high-quality LCD screens (TVs, computer monitors, and later smartphones) would trigger a migraine in as little as thirty seconds.
Let me be precise about what this meant for my career. I was running a trading business. A quantitative trading business. The kind where you stare at screens all day, analyzing data, monitoring positions, executing trades.
I was a trader who couldn’t look at screens.
I discovered that blue-light-blocking glasses helped, so I wore those. They made me look like a low-budget cyberpunk extra, but I wore them. And I took Advil. A lot of Advil. Eight to twelve tablets a day, three or four days a week. Enough that my stomach lining probably filed a formal complaint.
With this regimen, I could mostly function. “Mostly function” meant: if a normal working week has 40 hours, I was working maybe 2 or 3.
Two to three hours a week. Running a hedge fund.
The absurdity wasn’t lost on me. But what was the alternative? The migraines weren’t going away. The screens weren’t going away. The business needed to run. So I built systems that could operate with minimal intervention, checked them during my good hours, and spent the rest of my time in dark rooms with a cold compress.
This went on for more than twenty years. The migraines themselves, in one form or another, had been going on for fifty.
In 2022, I met a neurologist who said something no doctor had ever said to me before:
“There’s no need for you to suffer anymore.”
I’d heard variations of this, of course. “Have you tried Imitrex?” (Yes. Didn’t work, and made me sick.) “What about Botox?” (Not recommended, for reasons never fully explained to me.) “Prophylactic beta-blockers?” (Didn’t work, and horrible side effects.) “Fiorinal?” (A barbiturate. Made me sick to my stomach and didn’t do much anyway.)
But this was different. She told me about a new class of drugs called CGRP inhibitors, which block a nerve-signaling molecule I’ll come back to. They’d been designed, from scratch and on purpose, to prevent migraines. Not repurposed antidepressants. Not borrowed antiepileptics. Not “we noticed this blood pressure medication has an off-label effect.” Migraine drugs, built on decades of research into what causes migraines.
We cycled through a few options to figure out what worked for me and settled on Ajovy, a once-monthly injection I give myself at home.
The effect was not subtle.
After the first month, I had fewer migraines. After the second, fewer still. By the third month, I realized something strange was happening: I had time. I could work a full day. Then another. Then a full week. I could look at screens without fear. I could make plans without the asterisk of “assuming I’m not incapacitated.”
For the first time in more than two decades, I could work as much as I wanted, when I wanted, and how I wanted.
So I started writing [my book](https://substack.com/redirect/ed58a733-e19e-4908-bfcd-c4fd7490bd2d?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o).
Then I started this Substack.
Then, in my spare time (spare time, a concept I hadn’t had access to since the Reagan administration), I went back to physics.
In 1993, I’d gotten my PhD at UT Austin under George Sudarshan, working on quantum scattering theory. (Nobel Laureate Steven Weinberg was on my committee.) Then the Superconducting Supercollider got canceled, and particle physics jobs evaporated.
The SSC was over budget, yes. (Name a large project that isn’t.) But the real problem was that physicists, in their naïve attempt to keep costs down, had decided to concentrate the work in Texas and Illinois. Which meant 96 senators had no pork in the project.
Congress, in its infinite wisdom, looked at two big-ticket science items: the SSC, which would have probed the fundamental structure of reality, and the International Space Station, which would... float there. The SSC was over budget. The space station cost something like ten times as much and remains essentially useless for any science of note. Congress killed the SSC and kept the space station. Because the station had contractors in more districts. I told the whole sorry story in [UGH! Undoing Greatness, Halfwits](https://substack.com/redirect/10da233d-04fe-4222-8b99-7f1bfa960217?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o).
I knew at the time what this meant. No new particle physics of any significance would occur for the rest of my lifetime. Hence the career in finance.
And guess what? I was right. It is only a slight exaggeration to say that particle physics has discovered nothing of note since the SSC was killed. (The honorable exception is neutrino mass, which came from a tank of water in a Japanese mine, not from a collider.) Our experiments are at too low an energy to probe nature where we need to probe it.
All that excitement about the Higgs discovery? Sure, solid physics. We confirmed the particle exists and measured its mass. But nothing earthshaking. Nothing even frankly exciting. We knew the Higgs was there; the only question was its mass. We measured it. Good. Not exactly revolutionary. No supersymmetry. No dark matter particles. No new physics whatsoever.
The energy is too damned low. (Just like the rent is too damned high.)
So: nearly three decades in the markets. A good career, but not the one I’d planned.
Now, suddenly, I could think clearly for more than two hours at a stretch. I picked up where I’d left off. Within a few years I had four physics papers out: one in the [European Physical Journal C](https://substack.com/redirect/42a2e4b7-4cef-4801-bde7-ca3c40d4e5f3?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o) on a universal pattern in fermion mass ratios, a follow-up in [Modern Physics Letters A](https://substack.com/redirect/72d55cf5-1687-44cd-9bcf-3edb2dd05df7?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o) deriving those ratios from orbifold conformal field theory, a [Physics Letters B](https://substack.com/redirect/d0e28f28-ff66-43cd-b6c9-1753bba86921?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o) paper picking up a thread I’d left dangling in 1993, and a chapter in an [IntechOpen volume](https://substack.com/redirect/ff3aa10e-f253-474d-a55f-b581c7dbdc78?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o) on quantum field theory in curved spacetime that I co-edited.
Thirty years away from physics, and I came back. Because a drug let me.
Oh, and while I was at it, I published some finance research too: two papers in the [Journal of Portfolio Management](https://substack.com/redirect/fa7b00df-859f-4abe-9c2b-2c5b2387b7d8?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o), one in [Business Economics](https://substack.com/redirect/945b5e3f-0c8b-4417-a7c3-c542688195c0?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o) on why the Fed should stop pretending it can hit a point target, one on [Bitcoin market microstructure](https://substack.com/redirect/373e8696-c524-4356-a8d6-30c18d6f8631?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o), and a [book chapter](https://substack.com/redirect/1afaa0a6-dd12-452f-8cad-9912d8823494?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o) on investor psychology. And a [side project to the side project](https://substack.com/redirect/71dc3ae3-6719-4ce5-b0c9-127145f0f4bc?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o). The [full list](https://substack.com/redirect/adeec2b2-6532-4a7d-b0aa-0eafcbf5973a?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o) is on my website, if you’re the sort of person who reads bibliographies for fun.
I list these for one reason (bragging isn’t it; research papers in fundamental physics don’t make you rich): this is what was locked away. This is what the migraines had stolen, the hours of pain and all the hours that could have been. My grandmother managed to be a freedom fighter despite her migraines. I managed to run a hedge fund while barely able to look at the tools of my trade. But “managed despite” is not the same as “flourished because.” The counterfactual lives, hers and mine, are incalculable.
CGRP inhibitors gave me my counterfactual back.
So why is this in an economics Substack instead of a medical memoir?
Almost everyone who talks about “US drug policy” has it exactly backwards. The conventional wisdom goes like this: American drug prices are outrageous, we’re getting ripped off, other countries have figured it out, we should do what they do. Bernie Sanders will bus diabetics to Canada to buy cheaper insulin. Elizabeth Warren will rail against pharmaceutical company greed. The whole discourse assumes that high American prices are a problem to be solved, full stop.
They’re wrong. Not wrong about everything; there are real problems in American healthcare. But wrong about the fundamental economics of drug pricing, wrong about what American prices buy, and wrong about who benefits from the current system.
The critics have it backwards. Let me show you how.
Start with a fact that surprises most people: US generic drug prices are 33% below the international average.
According to the [RAND Corporation’s 2024 analysis](https://substack.com/redirect/01732f35-83de-4b8f-842c-df25f4bc4428?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o), American generic prices are 67% of prices in 33 OECD comparison countries. [PharmacyChecker found in 2019](https://substack.com/redirect/fcc6c91a-1946-4ff7-9cb9-7ac5d8e514dc?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o) that of the top 40 prescribed generics, 30 of the 34 that could be compared, 88%, were cheaper at US pharmacies, paying cash with a discount card, than from Canadian pharmacies, with US prices averaging 68% lower than Canada’s.
Lisinopril, one of America’s most prescribed blood pressure medications: $10 for 100 pills in the US versus $72.84 from Canada. Pantoprazole for acid reflux: $17.61 for a three-month supply in the US versus $171.24 from Canada.
Why? Competition. The 1984 Hatch-Waxman Act created the world’s most competitive generic market. Before that law, only 35% of top-selling drugs faced generic competition after patent expiry. Now it’s nearly 100%. When ten or more generic manufacturers compete, prices drop to 20-30% of the original brand price.
In practice: 90% of US prescriptions are filled with generics, against 41% unbranded generics in other OECD countries. Those generics account for only 8% of total US drug spending. Nine of every ten prescriptions, for less than one-twelfth of the money.
The Hatch-Waxman framework generated $3.1 trillion in savings over the decade to 2023, including $445 billion in 2023 alone, on the generic industry’s own count. This is a policy triumph that never gets acknowledged because it doesn’t fit the “America bad at healthcare” narrative.
(An aside: Given the makeup of the current Congress, can we pause to thank legislators like Orrin Hatch and Henry Waxman for having a brain and using it? Not to mention guys like Bill Bradley? Paul Tsongas? Or consider Birch Bayh and Robert Dole, who gave us Bayh-Dole. Wonder why American universities, even today, can attract the world’s best professors at the drop of a hat? Wonder no more. Look up Bayh-Dole.
From that golden era we descended to idiocy like Sarbanes-Oxley (a pathetic excuse for a law if I ever saw one) and then the execrable Dodd-Frank. You could write a book about how dumb Dodd-Frank is and not do its stupidity justice. Where did all the smart guys go?)
Now for the part everyone already knows: American branded drug prices are high. Very high.
RAND’s 2024 data shows US branded drug prices at 4.22 times the OECD average at list price, or just over 3× after accounting for rebates. Some specific drugs show even larger gaps.
This premium exceeds what raw wealth differences would justify. US GDP per capita in 2024 was about $86,000 against an OECD average of about $62,000 at purchasing-power parity, a ratio of about 1.4x, not 4x. Americans are richer, but not that much richer.
Three facts soften this picture, though:
First, insurers and government programs pay about 87% of US retail drug spending, and patients pay 12 to 13% out of pocket on the federal health accounts for 2023 and 2024, so the cost is spread across premiums and taxes rather than hitting patients directly at the pharmacy counter.
Second, brand-name drugs are only 7% of US prescription volume, but that 7% is 87% of the money. The generic market is a triumph, and it also tells you exactly where the argument lives: in the small, expensive slice where the new drugs are.
Third, the economic theory of price discrimination suggests this arrangement may benefit everyone, including the countries paying less.
The problem pharmaceutical companies face is that drug development has massive fixed costs and near-zero marginal costs.
The median cost to bring a new drug to market is around $985 million, with other estimates at $2.6 billion and up. Only about 12% of drugs entering clinical trials ever reach the market, and later industry tallies put it under 10%, which means successful drugs must recoup the costs of many failures. But once you’ve developed the drug, manufacturing additional pills costs pennies.
This creates an impossible pricing problem. If you charge one global price, you either:
Option A: Set it high enough to recover R&D costs. Result: most of the world can’t afford your drug. Sick people in India and Brazil die of treatable diseases because the price that works for American insurance companies doesn’t work for their incomes.
Option B: Set it low enough for universal access. Result: you don’t recover your R&D costs. The next generation of drugs doesn’t get developed. Future sick people die of diseases that would have been treatable if you’d had the profits to invest in research.
Neither option is good. Both involve dead people.
The economically efficient solution is what economists call third-degree price discrimination: charging different prices to different customer groups based on their ability to pay.
You already live with price discrimination. It’s why movie theaters offer senior discounts. It’s why airlines charge anywhere from $150 to $800 for the same seat depending on when you book and how flexible you are. It’s why universities publish a tuition sticker price of $60,000 but hand out financial aid so the actual price ranges from zero to full freight depending on your family’s income.
In each case, the logic is the same: the seller has high fixed costs and can’t survive charging one price to everyone. So they charge more to those who can pay more, less to those who can’t. Total sales increase. More people get served. The business stays solvent.
For pharmaceuticals, the customer is the payer, an insurer or a national health service, not the patient at the counter, which is why the textbook logic survives everything that is strange about the demand for medicine. So Americans pay more: the richest large market, with insurance that absorbs the cost. Europeans pay less. Developing countries pay less still. The result: drugs that would only be profitable in America become profitable globally, because the American premium subsidizes lower prices elsewhere.
There is a second reason the premium is too small rather than too large. Most of a new drug’s value escapes the company that made it. Once the patent runs out the molecule belongs to everyone, at pennies, forever, and even while it runs the price captures a sliver of what the drug is worth to the people it saves. Tomas Philipson and Anupam Jena [estimated](https://substack.com/redirect/9b908489-28d4-4140-9b7b-1c57d241d8e4?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o) that the makers of the HIV drugs of the late 1980s onward kept about 5% of the social value they created; almost 95% went to patients. When the inventor keeps a twentieth, the world gets far fewer inventions than it would choose if it could coordinate, and the American premium is the closest thing the world has to a coordination device.
Now the kicker. Kate Ho and Ariel Pakes, in a [2025 paper in PNAS](https://substack.com/redirect/c3034e26-bacf-4a8b-bccf-ff2f0ff96fed?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o), modeled what happens if every rich country paid one price per drug while total pharma revenue, and therefore R&D incentives, stayed fixed. US prices would fall by about half. Everyone else’s would rise: Canada by 28%, Germany by 48%, France by 97%, Britain by 100%, Italy by 163%, Spain by 187%, Korea by more than 200%.
The premium is not a rip-off. It is the world’s R&D budget, and Americans are the ones funding it. They are also the first to draw on it: I have my migraine drug, and my life back, because that budget got spent. This is an idea Americans have been taught to hate. The executive order that launched the “most favored nation” policy in May 2025 says so in as many words: “Americans should not be forced to subsidize low-cost prescription drugs and biologics in other developed countries.” The President has the fact right and the sign wrong.
The sign error deserves spelling out, because it is the whole argument. If the rest of the world paid American-style prices, the whole world would be better off, the United States included: more revenue, more drugs, and the burden shared. That is true. It does not follow that because the rest of the world does not pay, the United States is suffering. The United States is less well off than it would be in the world where everyone pays. It is still far better off paying for the drugs than not paying for them, because the alternative to paying is not a cheaper drug. It is no drug. Mancur Olson named this structure sixty years ago: when a good benefits everyone, the biggest beneficiary pays for it alone and the small ones ride free, “the exploitation of the great by the small.” The great still comes out ahead. The free ride is annoying. It is not a loss. A policy that treats it as a loss, and threatens to stop paying unless the others chip in, is a threat to bring about the one outcome that would leave Americans worse off.
Let me bring this back to the drug that changed my life.
Calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) was discovered in 1983. Researchers noticed it was a signaling molecule in the nervous system, but its role in migraines wasn’t clear yet.
In 1990, Peter Goadsby and Lars Edvinsson demonstrated that CGRP levels spike during migraine attacks. They measured it in the blood draining from the head during attacks: the trigeminal nerve system, which carries head pain, was releasing it. Block the CGRP, maybe block the migraine?
In 2004, a proof-of-concept study showed that CGRP antagonists could terminate acute migraines.
Then began the expensive part.
Thirty-five years from discovery to the first FDA approval. Novartis alone put at least $870 million into developing and commercializing erenumab (Aimovig), the first CGRP inhibitor to reach the market, in May 2018; the figure is Novartis’s own, from its 2019 complaint against its partner Amgen.
That’s what drug development looks like. Not some pharmaceutical company stumbling onto a molecule and slapping a price tag on it. Decades of basic research. Failed candidates. Clinical trials that go nowhere. Regulatory hurdles. Manufacturing scale-up. The whole miserable, expensive, uncertain process of turning scientific understanding into a pill (or injection) that actually works.
The results were worth it.
Previous migraine preventives (antidepressants, antiepileptics, beta-blockers) were all borrowed from other conditions. They sort of worked for some people, with brutal side effects. In head-to-head trials, 38.9% of patients discontinued topiramate (a standard preventive) due to adverse events. For erenumab? 10.6%.
The CGRP drugs work differently too. About 40-50% of patients achieve at least a 50% reduction in monthly migraine days. And they work even for patients who’ve failed two to four prior treatments, the tough cases, the people like me who’d tried everything: in the trials run on exactly those patients, about a third responded, against about one in eight on placebo.
Today there are eight FDA-approved CGRP inhibitors: four monoclonal antibodies (Aimovig, Ajovy, Emgality, Vyepti) and four small-molecule “gepants” (Ubrelvy, Nurtec, Qulipta, Zavzpret). Options for prevention, acute treatment, or both. CGRP drugs don’t work for everyone, which is why the pipeline continues: Lundbeck’s anti-PACAP antibody bocunebart [posted positive Phase IIb results](https://substack.com/redirect/956347be-535c-4254-8a30-af6a9f0cb7f6?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o) at the American Headache Society meeting in June 2026, and [substance P](https://substack.com/redirect/8e1da05f-2c07-4245-aa7d-c6d9b7eca842?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o), a target abandoned in the 1990s, is being revisited.
A [Finnish national survey](https://substack.com/redirect/681bd2da-43d9-4418-96e1-ce905abdc295?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o), published in 2025, captured what this means in practice. Before CGRP treatment, 73.6% of patients experienced twelve or more migraine days monthly. After treatment: 9.0%. Monthly sick leave of four or more days dropped from 37% to 4%. The share able to work or study full-time rose from 47.7% to 76.1%.
Those numbers are people getting their lives back.
This is where the pricing story connects to the innovation story.
Aimovig launched in the US at $6,900 per year in 2018. American patients started getting injections immediately.
The UK’s National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) looked at the same clinical data and initially rejected the drug. At a list price of around £5,000 per year in Britain, NICE deemed it not cost-effective, even though Novartis offered a confidential discount. NICE eventually recommended Ajovy in June 2020 and Aimovig in March 2021, with restrictions, after further price negotiations. British patients got these drugs two to three years after Americans did.
Meanwhile, US adoption exploded. Use of the CGRP antibodies nearly tripled between 2018 and 2020. North America now accounts for about 38% of a global CGRP market that one market-research estimate put at $3.4 billion in 2024.
The “$6,900 per year” number is misleading, because it’s not what most Americans pay. Manufacturer copay programs mean commercially insured Americans can get Ajovy for as little as $15 a month out of pocket, and the rival antibodies for $35 to $49; I currently pay zero. Patient assistance programs provide free medication for the uninsured. The list price is a negotiation starting point with insurers, not a retail sticker.
(The system isn’t perfect: patients with high-deductible plans can still face real sticker shock when their coinsurance is calculated off the list price rather than the negotiated net. That’s a problem worth fixing separately.)
Four of six major CGRP drug manufacturers (Amgen, Eli Lilly, AbbVie, Pfizer) are US-headquartered companies. The US accounts for about half of global prescription-drug revenue, 53% in 2025 on IQVIA’s count. The premium Americans pay above other countries’ prices exceeds what the companies spend on R&D globally: in 2015, the last year anyone did the sum carefully, it came to $116 billion for the 15 companies selling the 20 top drugs, against $76 billion those firms spent on R&D worldwide, in a [Health Affairs analysis](https://substack.com/redirect/e7e31b81-1daf-418d-8230-5f907e6c5bf1?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o) by Nancy Yu, Zachary Helms and Peter Bach.
American prices don’t just buy American drugs. They buy everyone’s drugs.
Would CGRP inhibitors exist without American pricing?
We can’t run the counterfactual, but the circumstantial evidence is suggestive. The UK rejected these drugs initially on cost grounds. Europe got the drugs later. Novartis’s $870 million investment makes sense with a large, paying American market in view. Without that market?
The Information Technology and Innovation Foundation [put it bluntly](https://substack.com/redirect/92f15e66-40ee-4a90-a46d-32eca31b9665?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o) in April 2025: “For decades, the United States has shouldered the lion’s share of global research and development in the life sciences... Unlike most other countries, the United States pays market prices for prescription drugs—effectively subsidizing lifesaving innovation for the rest of the world.”
This isn’t a popular position. It doesn’t fit neatly on a protest sign. “American patients subsidize European healthcare” doesn’t poll well. But it’s what the economics shows.
Ho and Pakes calculate that extending Medicare negotiation to all drugs plus Canadian importation would cut US pharma revenue by about 16%, global revenue by about 9%, and net profits by nearly 20%, with R&D incentives falling to match. They agree with the CBO that the Inflation Reduction Act’s pricing provisions are too small to matter much. Extending it to everything, they say, is another matter. The Information Technology and Innovation Foundation [estimated](https://substack.com/redirect/b93956eb-6b19-423c-a3cb-0bfe4d9c99e0?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o) that if the rest of the OECD had paid market prices in 2018, the extra revenue would have funded enough R&D for roughly 25 additional new drugs per year. It’s a model, and the number depends on the assumptions. But the direction isn’t in doubt.
Call it twenty-five drugs a year that don’t exist because rich countries outside America don’t pay market prices.
How many of those drugs would have helped someone’s grandmother?
If you want to see what a drug that doesn’t exist looks like, look at antibiotics. Resistant infections killed about 1.3 million people in 2019 [on the Lancet’s count](https://substack.com/redirect/9b8289e1-f4e8-4568-a3e1-43439e828301?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o), more than HIV or malaria that year, and the pipeline for new ones is thin to the point of embarrassment: [WHO’s 2023 review](https://substack.com/redirect/5fd81cfb-5da0-47b5-9f4d-272049f07885?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o) of the 32 traditional antibiotics in development against its priority pathogens found 12 that met even one test of innovation, and of the antibiotics approved between mid-2017 and 2023, two were a new chemical class. The science is hard, and that is the smaller problem. The larger one is that nobody pays: a new antibiotic is held in reserve to slow resistance, prescribed for a week rather than a lifetime, and priced against generics that cost pennies, so its makers cannot cover development. Achaogen won FDA approval for one in June 2018, sold $0.8 million worth that year, and filed for bankruptcy the following April; Melinta, with four antibiotics on the market, followed that December. Two first-in-class antibiotics did reach patients in 2025, and one of them got there through a partnership with a non-profit, which tells you what the for-profit market thought of the job. Britain now buys two antibiotics on a subscription, a fixed fee of up to £10 million a year whether or not a single dose is used, because that is what a drug costs when the market price is too low to keep it alive. Migraine got its drugs because Americans paid. Antibiotics went without, because nobody did.
The irony is that both sides of the American political aisle are trying to “fix” this system in ways that would break it.
Bernie Sanders bused a dozen diabetics to Canada for cheaper insulin in 2019. Elizabeth Warren’s 2020 plan would have capped American prices at 110% of an international average. The left’s solution: make America pay what Europe pays.
The Trump administration’s “most favored nation” order of May 2025 demands that the US pay no more than the lowest price charged in other developed countries. The right’s solution: make America pay what Europe pays.
Same policy. Different bumper stickers.
Alex Tabarrok of George Mason laid out the economics of MFN [in February 2026](https://substack.com/redirect/f97ec56b-c915-4764-b2c4-d91760469665?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o), and it’s worth walking through, because the evidence has been piling up ever since.
The first point: MFN won’t lower US prices on existing drugs. Pharmaceutical companies are already profit-maximizing here. If they successfully strong-arm France into paying more, they don’t then turn around and announce a discount in Des Moines. The actual upside of the plan is higher pharmaceutical profits and therefore more R&D investment. Try fitting that on a MAGA hat.
The second point: if policy forces a single global price, firms pick the high price, not the low one. Pfizer’s CEO, Albert Bourla, said this out loud at the JPMorgan healthcare conference in January 2026: forced to choose between cutting US prices to France’s level or stopping supply to France, Pfizer would stop supplying France, and “they will stay without new medicines.” The American market is too large and too profitable to sacrifice. Europe is not.
The third point: some countries will dig in rather than pay more, and then everyone loses: lower profits, less R&D, no US price cut, and fewer drugs abroad.
So what has actually happened? By the end of August 2026, the administration had [signed MFN deals with 26 companies](https://substack.com/redirect/9a53064e-1d3a-4e8b-a44d-a70d69678a68?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o) covering roughly 89% of the branded market, in exchange for relief from pharmaceutical tariffs. The deals are confidential, which tells you something. What’s public is that they cover Medicaid pricing, new drug launches, and cash prices on a government website. The existing commercial portfolio, the drugs most Americans take through their insurance, is largely untouched. That’s the first point, right there.
Abroad, the second point is playing out about as the textbook says. Britain, under US trade pressure, agreed in December 2025 to raise the net price the NHS pays for new medicines by 25% from April 2026. And [GlobalData reported](https://substack.com/redirect/a86b8a24-b088-438c-826b-2c94c6afbe00?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o) in March 2026 that branded-medicine withdrawals in Europe rose 43% in the ten months after the MFN order, with new launches across Europe down 35%. A single global price is being set. It’s being set upward.
The third point has a twist. Germany went the other way, passing a law in July 2026 to squeeze prices further, and Switzerland refused to pay more. So some countries are digging in. But the countries digging in are the ones losing launches. Lose-lose-lose is arriving, just unevenly: it lands first on the countries that refused to play.
There is, I suppose, a version of this story where the Trump policy accidentally does what I’ve been arguing for all along: makes Europe pay closer to market price and thereby funds more R&D. If Britain is any indication, that’s partly what’s happening. But it is happening by tariff blackmail rather than by anyone understanding the economics, the deals are secret, and the stated goal, lower US prices, is the one thing that isn’t happening. A policy that succeeds by accident while failing at its purpose is not a policy I’m going to cheer for.
The deeper problem is that both the Sanders left and the Trump right start from the same premise: Americans are getting ripped off, and someone else should pay less so we can pay less. But the arithmetic doesn’t work that way. You can’t equalize prices downward and maintain R&D investment. You can’t bully other countries into paying more without some of them simply going without. And you can’t pretend that the system that produced CGRP inhibitors, GLP-1 agonists, and mRNA vaccines is a system that needs to be torn down rather than understood.
The politically active, left and right, agree on the diagnosis (prices too high!) and they’re both wrong about the cure.
At this point, someone will object: “If US prices fund R&D, why do pharmaceutical companies spend so much on marketing?”
This is supposed to be a gotcha. It isn’t.
Think about it for a second. I suffered from migraines for fifty years. CGRP inhibitors were approved in 2018. I didn’t get one until 2022. Why? Because no one told me they existed. Not my previous neurologists, not my primary care doctor, not anyone. I found out because I happened to see a neurologist who happened to know about them.
How did she know? Somewhere along the line, somebody spent money making sure she knew: a journal, a conference, a sales rep, a dinner. That’s what marketing is: the part of the budget that pays to move information from the lab to the doctor to the patient.
Doctors don’t have time to read every journal article. They see patients all day. They have lives. The information that a new drug class exists, that it works differently from previous treatments, that it might help the patients sitting in their waiting room, has to flow somehow. “Marketing” is the word we use when companies pay to make information flow.
The critique assumes its conclusion. It presupposes that marketing is wasteful manipulation, that doctors are rubes being bamboozled, and that patients can’t be trusted with information about their own treatment options. At some point this brainless paternalism (the public is stupid, doctors are corrupt, only regulators know best) has to stop.
The deeper point is that the anti-marketing critique is the socialized medicine critique in disguise. If patients don’t know a drug exists, they can’t ask for it, which means the system doesn’t have to pay for it. Rationing through ignorance.
Think about what NICE did. They looked at the same clinical data the FDA saw. Same efficacy numbers. Same safety profile. Same patients suffering the same migraines. And they said: not cost-effective. British migraine patients waited years longer than Americans for relief, and not because the drug didn’t work: the bureaucrats decided the suffering wasn’t worth the money.
That is the feature. Price controls work by denying care. Marketing bans work by keeping patients ignorant. Same mechanism, different packaging.
You know what’s wasteful? A brilliant molecule sitting in a warehouse because no one knows it exists. That helps exactly zero migraine patients. Marketing is how drugs go from “FDA approved” to “in your bloodstream helping you.”
The same people who complain about pharmaceutical marketing are often the ones who complain that patients don’t have enough information to make informed decisions. Pick one.
The complete picture of US drug pricing:
Generics: 33% cheaper than international averages. This is where 90% of prescriptions come from. American policy triumph, ignored by critics.
Branded drugs: 3-4x higher than other wealthy countries. Real difference. Real cost.
Who actually pays: Insurers and government programs cover 87% of costs. Copay programs often reduce patient burden to negligible levels. The sticker-shock numbers rarely reflect what patients pay out of pocket.
What the premium buys: Global pharmaceutical R&D. Differential pricing that serves poorer markets at lower prices. Continued investment in future breakthroughs.
Who benefits: Americans get faster access to innovations. The world eventually gets access at lower prices, subsidized by American revenue. Future patients get drugs that exist because someone, somewhere, was willing to pay for their development.
Is it “fair” that Americans pay more? By one definition, no. But by the definition that matters, whether the system produces good outcomes for the most people, the answer is more complicated than the critics allow.
My grandmother fought for Indian independence while migraines tried to stop her. She managed. She didn’t flourish.
I ran a hedge fund while barely able to look at screens. I managed. I didn’t flourish.
Then a drug came along—a drug that took 35 years and hundreds of millions of dollars to develop, a drug that might not exist without American pharmaceutical pricing, a drug that UK regulators initially rejected as too expensive—and gave me my life back.
I wrote a book. I started this Substack. I returned to physics after three decades away. I published papers. I have time now. I have capacity. I have the counterfactual.
The politically active don’t know what they’re talking about when they talk about American drug policy. They see the prices and miss the innovation. They see the disparity and miss the subsidy. They see the unfairness and miss the drugs that wouldn’t exist in a “fairer” world.
I’m one of the people those drugs were made for. Let me tell you: the system works. Americans could even be proud of it. They fund the world’s medicine cabinet, and they get to open it first.
It’s ugly, it’s expensive, it’s counterintuitive, and it works.
