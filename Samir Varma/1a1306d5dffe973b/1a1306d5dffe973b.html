The Tetrad Tour, Part III: Why AI Needs Its Linnaeus

Samir Varma
Oct 12
 
READ IN APP
 

William Stanley Jevons spent five years weighing gold.

Before he co-founded the marginal revolution, before “marginal utility” became the idea every economist alive now thinks with, he was an assayer at the Sydney Mint, a teenager who had left University College without finishing his degree because his father’s firm had gone bankrupt and the colonial mint paid well. From 1854 to 1859 his job was to take gold of unknown purity and determine, precisely, what the next unit was worth. Measure, record, repeat. He did it competently enough to improve the standard process.

I learned the detail about the assaying from Tyler Cowen’s generative book, The Marginal Revolution: Rise and Decline, and the Pending AI Revolution, published in March 2026, which is, fittingly, free to read with an AI companion bolted on, and which makes the assayer’s hands central to how Jevons came to think about value. Cowen uses the rise and fall of marginalism as a lens on the AI revolution he thinks is coming. I want to borrow his history and sharpen the lens, because I think there’s a cleaner account of why some intellectual revolutions take and others die on the vine, and of what it tells us about whether the AI one is real.

Start with the puzzle Jevons opens up. Marginal utility was not a hard idea to have. Versions of it had been floating around for a century: Gossen in Germany, Dupuit and Cournot in France, Longfield in the British Isles had all brushed against it. Jevons, by his own account, had read none of the first three when he formulated his theory, and nobody noticed Longfield’s version at all until 1903. The idea was available. It kept not taking. Then, in the early 1870s, three men working in striking independence (Jevons in England, Menger in Vienna, Walras in Lausanne) converged on it, and this time it stuck. It became the foundation of a science.

Why? Why do correct ideas sometimes sit inert for a hundred years and then, suddenly, take?

I want to argue that being right has almost nothing to do with it.

That sounds like a provocation, so let me make it precise. Of course being right matters: a false idea can be polished into usability and still be false, and falsehood eventually tells. But correctness is the cheap part. It is necessary and radically insufficient. The marginal utility insight was correct in 1770 and correct in 1830 and correct in 1860, and for most of that stretch it did nothing, because the work of making it take had not been done. What work? That is the question, and I think it comes in four kinds, one for each lens of a framework I keep returning to. (By the way, it is still correct today, even though some economists find it convenient to forget the moment the subject turns to taxation.)

I’ve argued in earlier posts that you can read almost any hard problem through four irreducible lenses: physics, which asks what the substance is; mathematics, which asks what the structure is; computer science, which asks what the process is; and economics, which asks what gets selected. I call it the Tetrad, and I keep returning to it because it keeps paying out. The first two installments pointed it at standing fields (anthropology, psychology, law, political science) and watched the real structure fall out. This one does something different. It runs the Tetrad not as a description but as a theory of revolutions, or, more modestly, as four places to look for the bottleneck. Each lens names a kind of work an idea needs done before it can spread, and each kind of work has a price. An idea takes when the price at every lens has been driven low enough for strangers to pay it; it stalls at whichever lens is still too expensive. Marginalism is the case where we can watch the price fall at all four, slowly, over decades — and watch the revolution become real exactly as it does.

Substance: where Jevons started, with his hands

Recall what the assayer actually did. He took a quantity of gold of unknown purity and determined what it was worth: not in the abstract, but this lump, this next unit, measured against a standard until the number was exact. Cowen makes the point that this is where Jevons’s lifelong instinct for quantitative measurement came from, and the broader biographical record agrees: the Australian Dictionary of Biography records that his mint work was highly competent, that he improved some of its standard processes, and that he spent his spare hours as a systematic, careful observer of the Sydney weather, publishing his daily readings at intervals in a local newspaper. The job was not marginalism, and Jevons was the first to say so: “We cannot weigh, nor gauge, nor test the feelings of the mind,” he wrote in the Theory, and he never claimed otherwise. What the mint gave him was the habit underneath the theory (define the object, measure against a standard, treat value as a quantity attached to a unit) and, more than that, it gave him his model of how measurement gets invented. When he had to explain how a vague thing like utility could ever become calculable, he reached for the balance: before “the simple contrivance of the balance,” he argued (quoting De Morgan), we had no clearer idea of weight as a magnitude than we now have of talent or prudence. The instrument made the quantity. His answer for utility was to build the instrument out of behavior: “The will is our pendulum, and its oscillations are minutely registered in the price lists of the markets.” The assayer did not weigh utility. He learned, at the scale, what it would take to.

This is the physics lens, and it asks a deflating question of any would-be revolution: is there a thing here, something with the texture of a measurable quantity, or are you waving at an abstraction? Marginalism had a thing. Utility, narrowed to the worth of the next unit, behaved like a quantity you could in principle measure: it went up, it went down, it had a margin. That is why it could anchor a science when the broad, sloshing, moral-philosophy sense of “utility” never could. You cannot build calculation on a mood. You can build it on a measurement. The narrowing that later generations would complain about (and they were right to, as we’ll see) was the same narrowing that gave the idea a substance to stand on.

Notice already that the substrate did not have to be glamorous. It was a teenager doing well-paid colonial labor he’d taken because his family went broke. The revolution’s foundation was poured by accident, in a mint, by someone trying to make rent. Keep that in view, because the unglamorous-labor theme is about to become the whole argument.

Structure: and here Jevons does something revealing. He goes back to school for it

Having seen the insight, Jevons grasped at once that intuition wasn’t enough. In an 1860 letter to his brother he wrote that he had struck out the true theory of economy, “entirely mathematical in principle,” and that what he called the law of utility, our diminishing marginal utility, “opens up the whole of the subject.” He could feel the structure the idea required, and he was developing the theory while finishing the degree he had left behind. The mathematics and the marginalism came up together, and he stated the conclusion flatly in print: economics, “if it is to be a science at all, must be a mathematical science,” decades before the profession would agree. The structure wasn’t handed to him with the insight. It arrived alongside it, and he could see that without it the insight was inert.

This is the mathematics lens: an idea needs a structure large enough to hold it, and often the structure has to be built or borrowed before the idea can move. The right comparison here isn’t even economics. It’s geology. Cowen tells the story of how naturalists had to make one specific structural move before the history of life became legible: they had to enlarge the time scale. As he puts it, the time scale “had to be enlarged greatly and to be seen as involving change,” rife with information and lessons. A static six-thousand-year Earth simply had no room in it for the phenomena. Stretch the frame to deep time and the same fossils that were inert curiosities become a record. The data didn’t change. The structure that could hold the data did.

Jevons’s version of marginalism got exactly this kind of enlargement, and calculus was it. “The next unit” is a vague phrase until you have a language of continuous functions and rates of change, at which point it becomes the derivative of utility with respect to quantity: rigorous, manipulable, connectable to everything else. (Not every founder took that route: Menger’s marginalism was verbal, and he kept it deliberately on the far side of the line between the natural and the social sciences. It is the Jevons strand — mathematical, and later statistical — that became the profession’s live tradition, which is why it is the one this essay follows.) Jevons saw the structure in his twenties and most of his contemporaries couldn’t. Cowen’s diagnosis is that they had a local rather than a global vision: they were looking at the same idea, and they didn’t have the frame that would let them see it, in Jevons’s own words, “open up the whole of the subject.”

But, and this is the hinge of the whole post, a substance and a structure still aren’t enough. Jevons had both by 1862. The revolution didn’t arrive until 1871, and didn’t really propagate for years after that. Two lenses were filled and the idea still mostly sat there. To understand why, we have to talk about the least glamorous lens of the four, the one that decides almost everything, and the one the AI revolution is currently struggling to fill. Keep the two straight as we go: structure is the conceptual architecture, the frame you reason inside, while process is the executable routine others can run without you. You can have one without the other, which is exactly the trap that’s coming.

Process: the lens that does the real work

Leave economics for a moment and walk into a different laggard science. Botany should have been easy. No telescopes required, no particle accelerators; the subject matter is lying around in every meadow on Earth. And yet, as Cowen points out, taxonomic botany was hard to figure out, and it arrived embarrassingly late.

Consider John Ray, the leading British botanist of the seventeenth century: anatomist, Royal Society fellow, Cambridge man, a serious scientist by any measure. Ray built a classification system the way a thoughtful person would: he kept too much truth in the model. His 1682 scheme asked you to weigh whether a plant was a monocotyledon or a dicotyledon, and to consider the seeds, the fruit, the flower, the leaves, and whether the whole thing was a herb, a shrub, or a tree. Every one of those features carries information. Ray was, in an important sense, more right than the man who beat him. He even made a crucial theoretical move toward a reproductive species concept — species as groups whose distinguishing traits propagated stably from seed, like giving rise to like — the very direction the winning system would lean on. And his empirical work covered thousands of plants.

Ray lost. Not because no one could use his system; that would be the tidy version, and it’s false. The young Linnaeus himself catalogued Swedish plants by Ray’s method, alongside Tournefort’s and Rivinus’s, before he had a system of his own. Ray’s scheme ran. The trouble was what it cost to run. In Cowen’s phrasing it was too cumbersome and too difficult to replicate or teach; it was never clear which rule to apply, so every classification demanded judgment, and judgment demanded training, and two trained botanists could weigh the same criteria and file the same plant in different places. The question was never whether another botanist could pick up Ray’s system. It was how much expertise the system required of whoever picked it up, and how cheaply the results could travel from one worker to the next. Ray had the substance and the seed of the right structure, and he left the price of participation high.

Carl Linnaeus beat him with something worse, and that is the lesson. Linnaeus classified plants mostly by the number and arrangement of their sexual parts: the number of stamens fixed the class, the pistils or styles helped fix the order. This is, on its face, biologically crude. It throws away most of what Ray insisted on keeping. It succeeded, Cowen notes, precisely because it was arbitrary yet uniform: count the stamens and you are most of the way to the class, with far fewer judgment calls than Ray asked for. Not none. Magnus Lidén, in a 2025 history of the system in TAXON, counts 27 genera in the 1735 Systema that key out under two classes at once, each with a cross-reference to the other, hundreds by the 1748 edition, plus a note under any genus whose species broke the rule; by one 1830 count a quarter of the genera in the British flora did. Linnaeus never eliminated judgment. What he did was cut the amount of it a beginner needed, and then pair the count with binomial naming (two Latin words, genus then species, two words and only two, consistently), so that you no longer have a theory of plants. You have, in Cowen’s lovely phrase, a filing cabinet for information about plants. And a cabinet with see-also cards is still a cabinet; the cross-references are filing technology, not a failure of it. A searchable system, keyword-indexed, that any botanist anywhere could read from and write to without translating someone else’s private synonyms. Linnaeus knew exactly what he was optimizing. Laying the rival systems side by side in 1738, his own among them, he wrote that whatever their differences, “all aim at the same purpose and target, namely to guide the amateur of botany to genera expediently.” The amateur. Expediently. He was not asking which system was truest. He was asking which one a beginner could run. (The system was also so frank about plant sex that the first edition of the Encyclopaedia Britannica sniffed that “obscenity is the very basis of the Linnaean system,” and a bishop who helped found the Linnean Society complained of the “gross prurience” of Linnaeus’s mind. It ran anyway. Runnability does not require good taste.)

Read that as a computer scientist and it snaps into focus. Ray wrote elegant, expressive, multi-criterion code that only an expert could run. Linnaeus shipped. He defined a rigid, almost dumb schema, enforced a strict and uniform naming convention, and produced a system whose chief virtue was interoperability: many researchers reading and writing one shared data structure without collision. This is the process lens: an idea that “makes sense” is inert until someone does the unglamorous engineering of making it executable, teachable, and replicable by people who are not its author. Correctness loses to runnability. It loses routinely.

And now watch Cowen hand us the bridge, because he says the quiet part himself. Why did Linnaeus’s system actually propagate? Not because it was right — Ray was righter. It propagated because, in Cowen’s words, Linnaeus didn’t just make a system that made sense, he “put in the work.” He personally named 5,900 species in a single publication. He trained an army of students and, in a clever move, named plants after the researchers who pleased him, so that adopting his scheme came with a standing incentive. He turned classification into what Cowen calls a “more-or-less automatic research program”: here is a method, here is a manageable task, go sail to the far side of the world, name what you find, report back, and collect your professional reward. And then Cowen writes the sentence this essay is built on: just as the Marginal Revolution gave economists a lot more to do, so did Linnaeus’s approach to botany.

Hold that against the puzzle we left hanging. We had Jevons with the substance by the 1850s and the structure by 1862, and the revolution still didn’t fire until 1871 and didn’t propagate for years after. The nine years are mostly Jevons. The 1862 paper was a brief sketch, read at Cambridge in his absence, boiled down to a still briefer abstract in the proceedings, not printed in full until 1866, and, in his own words to Walras, “either neglected or criticised”; he did not write the book until 1871. The Tetrad tells us where to look for the rest, and so, it turns out, does Cowen; he just files it under a different name. He observes that early marginalism, without the accompanying advances in measurement and method, did not give economists “all that much to do,” and that until those advances arrive the idea “just doesn’t seem all that impressive.” That is a diagnosis of the process lens as the binding constraint. The substance was there. The structure was there. What was missing was the executable apparatus (the demand curves, the diagrams, the optimization problems, the equilibrium models and comparative statics, and eventually the estimation methods) that would turn a correct insight into a job, into a thing a working economist could pick up and run every morning. That apparatus has dates, and they fall after the book, not before it: Walras’s Elements in 1874, Edgeworth’s Mathematical Psychics in 1881, Wicksteed’s Alphabet in 1888, Marshall’s Principles in 1890, which put the supply-and-demand diagram in front of the whole profession, and Fisher’s Mathematical Investigations in 1892. Those are the two decades in which marginalism went from a correct idea to a working day, and they are the two decades in which it spread. Marginalism became a revolution not when it became true and not when it became rigorous, but when it became something to do. Cowen supplies the history; the process lens says where in it to look, and it is the same place John Ray came to grief.

So: three lenses, and the revolution finally moves. One left, and it is the one that decides what the revolution leaves behind.

Selection: what gets kept, and what waits for an instrument

Every one of these victories was also a narrowing. Linnaeus won by throwing away most of the plant. Marginalism won, in part, by setting aside a question the early marginalists had actually cared about. Here I have to be careful, because the lazy version of this story is wrong, and Cowen is at pains to correct it, so I will state the careful version, which is stronger.

The lazy version says: the marginal revolution narrowed “utility” from a rich moral quantity down to a thin private one, banished interpersonal comparison and distribution, and reigned as cold, individualist, ordinal economics until Amartya Sen rode in from the 1970s to restore the human dimension. It’s a good story. It’s also, as Cowen demonstrates at length, false in its premise. Social marginalism was baked into the revolution from the start. Jevons, Menger, and Walras all entertained cardinal utility and believed it had social implications; Gossen, an even earlier precursor, was an outright Benthamite obsessed with land reform who applied diminishing marginal utility across individuals; Edgeworth wanted to build a literal “hedonimeter” to compute maximum social pleasure. As Cowen puts it flatly, social marginalism has been part of the marginal revolution from the beginning.

But here is where I have to be more careful than the lazy story’s critics usually are, because three different claims are hiding inside “social marginalism,” and the founders did not hold all three. Wanting economics to improve the lot of the poor is one claim. Believing each person carries a cardinal scale of pleasure (a ruler in every head) is a second. Believing the rulers share units, so that my utility can be laid against yours, is a third. Having a ruler in each head does not give the rulers the same units. And Jevons, the most influential founder, held the first two while flatly rejecting the third. In the same chapter where he signs on to utilitarian morals, he writes that his theory never once attempts to compare feeling across minds: “I see no means by which such comparison can be accomplished.” Then the tell: even if we could, “we should not need to do so.” The theory of exchange runs without it.

So what actually happened is neither destruction-and-resurrection nor a quiet demotion. Interpersonal comparison was left out of the formal core at the founding, by its most influential founder, on stated grounds: it couldn’t be measured, and the runnable theory didn’t need it. The social aims stayed, as motivation, as the reason the whole enterprise was worth doing, but they lived outside the machine, in a drawer marked “philosophy, not science.” Recall why Linnaeus beat Ray: a rule cheap enough for a beginner to apply, and a cabinet that everyone filed into the same way. A theory of exchange that required you to compare my happiness to yours would have had no such rule, because by Jevons’s own lights there was no instrument for it. So Jevons excluded the comparison, honestly, as unmeasurable and unnecessary. What Sen did, starting in the 1970s, was not to restore something that had been demoted. It was to argue that Jevons had been wrong about how much instrument the job needed. Sen showed, first, that you do not need my happiness and yours on one shared scale to say most of what welfare economics wants to say: partial comparisons, made explicit, carry a surprising amount of the load, and a blanket refusal to compare carries none of it. And second, he changed what goes on the scale. The thing to compare need not be a mental state at all; it can be what a person is able to do and to be, their capabilities and their opportunities, which, unlike pleasures, can be observed. He was careful to say that the empirical discipline of actually making such comparisons was unfinished, and it still is. But he largely won the analytical point, to the point that partial interpersonal comparisons have re-entered standard normative economics and now occupy a large part of the welfare-economics frontier. The thing the founders had ruled out as unmeasurable was re-admitted once someone showed how much of it could be weighed, and with what.

This is the economics lens, the one that asks what gets selected, and it turns out to rhyme with the first lens, which is the part I did not expect when I started. Recall Jevons at the scale: the balance made weight into a quantity; before the instrument, weight was as vague as prudence. Now look at what he did with utility across persons. No instrument, so no quantity, so out of the science: not suppressed, not demoted, just left where talent and prudence sat before De Morgan’s balance. Every revolution I can find does this. It narrows to what it can measure, because measurement is what makes the process cheap enough for strangers to run, and it sets the rest aside as philosophy. What a science excludes as unmeasurable is not destroyed. It is a standing invitation to build the instrument, or to find out how much can be said without one. That is the pattern under all four lenses, and it’s less dramatic than a debt coming due and more useful. The danger was never abstraction; Linnaeus threw away most of the plant and botany was better for it. The danger is forgetting what you abstracted away, so that when someone finally builds the instrument for it, the field can’t remember why it mattered. Sen didn’t collect a debt. He showed that something Jevons had said could not be weighed could be weighed in part, and on a different balance, and the science, to its credit, put it on the scale.

There is a second half to the economics lens, and I have been using it without naming it. Economics asks what gets selected, and the answer is never only what can be measured; it is what someone has a reason to keep doing. Linnaeus understood this better than Ray. He named plants after the botanists who adopted his scheme, which meant that filing into his cabinet paid, in the only currency a working naturalist had. Marginalism spread when it became something to do, which is to say a career. The routine has to be cheap to run, and someone has to be paid to run it, and those are different conditions. A routine can be cheap to run and still go unused when the people who must change their work bear the cost while someone else collects the gains. Hold on to that, because it is the third price in the AI prediction to come, and it is the one the process lens cannot see.

The pattern, and the objection that tests it

So the claim assembles into something simple. A correct idea is not a revolution. It becomes one only when four kinds of work have been done cheaply enough for other people to use: it needs a substance calculation can bite on (or it stays philosophy); a structure large enough to hold it (or it stays anecdote); a process that strangers can run without the author’s judgment (or it stays, like Ray’s, a tool only experts can afford); and it has made a selection, narrowed to what it can measure, and set the rest aside. These are not four boxes whose completion guarantees a revolution. They are four places the price can still be too high, and the historical question is which one was binding, and what brought the price down. Leave one high and the idea sits inert, or dies, or gets beaten by something dumber that was cheaper to run, sometimes for a century after it was first correct.

A careful reader should be annoyed by now, and I want to honor the annoyance rather than dodge it. The complaint: the Tetrad is unfalsifiable. Hand me any winner and of course I can carve it into four lenses after the fact, the way you can find a face in any cloud. A framework that explains every outcome predicts none.

Two answers, one cheap and one that matters. The cheap one: the framework earns its keep on losers, which is why I spent so long on John Ray. Ray is a test, not a description: he passed substance and structure and stalled at a named lens, the one where the cost of participation lives, and the framework says that is where to look for why he lost. It doesn’t claim to have ruled out every other cause. It claims to have named the likeliest one, and to have said where to check. Marginalism is the second test and it passes the same way: a correct book in 1871 with almost nothing in it for a working economist to do, and two decades in which the apparatus got built and the idea spread as it did. The framework doesn’t just narrate the success; it postdicts the delay.

The answer that matters is about discipline: a framework has to be able to turn examples away. The most famous “inferior thing won” story in economics is the QWERTY keyboard, the deliberately slow layout that supposedly locked in while the superior Dvorak languished. It looks like a gift to my thesis. I’m not going to use it, because when Liebowitz and Margolis went and checked, the evidence for Dvorak’s superiority largely evaporated: the splashiest claims traced back to Dvorak himself, and controlled studies showed little or no advantage. QWERTY may simply be fine. Ray fits because we can name the likely bottleneck and check it against the record; QWERTY doesn’t, so it stays out. A theory that greedily swallows every example is exactly the cloud-reading I conceded a moment ago; a theory that turns examples away at the door is doing work. If you ever find me an idea that left a lens conspicuously expensive and still swept the field, you will have broken it. And a postdiction is the weak form of the test. The strong form names the prices before the outcome is in, and the AI section below makes that bet.

The turn, which is the reason any of this matters in 2026

Cowen did not write a history for its own sake. His subtitle is and the Pending AI Revolution, and his thesis is that the calculation-heavy, theory-light methods now overrunning fields like finance are doing to intuitive economics roughly what marginalism once did to what came before. I think the Tetrad lets us ask his question more sharply than “is AI going to be a big deal.” Of course it will be a big deal; that is the cheap part; that is correctness. The real question is the one this whole essay has been sharpening: will it take? Run AI through the four lenses and ask, at each one, what it still costs to participate, because a revolution stalls at whichever lens is still too expensive, no matter how right it is.

On substance, the picture is strong and overhyped at the same time, which is why you have to weigh the measured thing and be honest about which thing you’re weighing. Three questions get run together in every AI argument: can the systems do useful work; are organizations deploying them well; and have those deployments moved wages, employment, or productivity in the aggregate. Those are three different scales, and a null on the third does not impeach the first. A workplace that produces better output in the same paid hours registers a precise zero on earnings and hours while the tool is doing real work. So: the capability is real and not in dispute. What’s thin is the third reading. I’ve written before about the gap between capability and measured economic footprint: a comprehensive Danish study tracking 25,000 workers across AI-exposed occupations found precise zeros on earnings and hours through December 2024, two years after ChatGPT launched, despite widespread adoption, even as it recorded real changes in tasks and how work was organized.

The evidence is not uniformly null, and the exception is instructive. Stanford’s “Canaries in the Coal Mine?” study, in its August 2026 revision using payroll data through June, finds no economy-wide displacement, but it finds that employment of workers aged 22 to 25 in AI-exposed occupations now sits about 19% below where it would be had it kept pace with their less-exposed peers, a gap that has widened from 15% a year earlier, operating mainly through reduced hiring rather than increased separations. The authors treat the pattern as descriptive rather than causal. And the detail that matters for this essay, which they offer as suggestive rather than settled: the declines are concentrated in roles built on codified knowledge the models can reproduce, while experienced workers in the same occupations, running on tacit judgment, show no comparable gap. The broader point survives: economy-wide transformation, as measured, is still far smaller than the capability curves would lead you to expect. There is a substance here; this is not the metaverse. The part we can presently weigh is just a great deal thinner than the part we’re being sold, and a physicist’s first instinct should be to trust the scale.

On structure, the question is whether we have a frame big enough to hold the thing, and here the warning light is on, not as a philosophical complaint, but because the frame decides which tasks a firm points the tool at. The dominant frame for AI is “automation”: a faster machine, a cheaper worker, the next increment in a two-century trend. Under that frame the obvious move is substitution: find a job a model can do, and stop hiring for it. Now look at what the payroll data actually show. The Stanford study’s sharpest finding is that the young-worker declines are concentrated in occupations where AI is used to substitute for human tasks; where it is used to complement workers, the gap largely isn’t there. And the clean deployment win we have, the call center we’ll come to in a moment, was complement-mode all the way down: the tool sat beside the agent and made the novice better, and the firm’s gains came from that. Be careful about what that shows. It shows that the two uses leave different marks on the payroll, not that substitution loses money; a firm can cut its hiring and raise its profits, and the call-center paper itself says its gain could mean fewer agent-hours if the demand for support stays flat. So what follows is my bet, not a finding: the automation frame is the expensive error, because it points a firm at the one use whose payoff is a thinner bench and hides the use where the measured wins have come from. Cowen’s deeper point is that the new methods aren’t a faster version of the old thing at all; a factor-pricing model with 360,000 factors isn’t automated economics, it’s a different way of knowing, with no digestible story inside it. Jam that into the automation frame and you get exactly what the geologists avoided when they enlarged the time scale: a new phenomenon forced into a category too small for it, and, in this case, a category that tells you to stop hiring the person you should have been equipping. Getting the frame wrong is a structure failure, separate from whether anyone can run the thing, which is the process failure we turn to next. AI is currently paying a price at both.

On process, this is the one that should keep the enthusiasts up at night, because it is the lens that decides almost everything, and right now AI is far more John Ray than Carl Linnaeus. It is breathtaking in the demo, in the benchmark, in the controlled setting. It is much weaker at the unglamorous thing that actually determines whether a revolution propagates: being picked up and run, reliably, by people who aren’t its authors, inside the mess of a real organization, meeting a defined quality standard every morning. We have at least one look at the size of that gap. A preliminary MIT NANDA report (based on interviews, senior-leader surveys, and analysis of 300 public implementations, not a peer-reviewed census) found a brutal enterprise pattern: high adoption, low transformation. In its sample, 95% of organizations were getting zero return from generative AI. That figure is interview-based and its denominator is soft, so treat it as a sighting of the problem rather than a measurement of it. But the sighting is consistent, and MIT’s diagnosis of why is the process lens in different words: not model quality, but a “learning gap,” brittle workflows, tools that dazzle in a demo and won’t integrate with how the work is actually done. That is the compile error, seen in the wild. The work that closes it is Linnaean labor, and more capability alone will not do it: building the schemas, the conventions, the rigid and teachable apparatus that turns a brilliant one-off into a routine anyone can perform.

And we know that labor pays, because we can watch it pay outside code. The most instructive deployment study I know is Brynjolfsson, Li, and Raymond’s, published in the QJE in 2025: a generative assistant rolled out to 5,172 customer-support agents, raising issues resolved per hour by 15% on average, with the largest gains going to the newest and least skilled workers. Look at why it worked. The tool was trained on the transcripts of the firm’s best agents; what it actually did was codify their tacit practice and hand it to everyone else; in the authors’ words, the system was “capturing and disseminating the behaviors of the most productive agents.” Agents who followed its suggestions more closely gained more, and adherence rose over time. That is Linnaeus in a call center: expert practice, extracted and codified, offered as suggestions a novice could take or leave, run by people who did not invent it. Customer support worked because its outputs are cheap to check, its mistakes are cheap to contain, and someone with the authority to redesign the workflow did the filing. That is the recipe, and it has nothing to do with software.

So the recipe is not a secret, and it makes a prediction. Holding model capability roughly constant, durable adoption should spread fastest where three prices are lowest: outputs are cheap to check, mistakes are cheap to contain, and someone has both the authority and the incentive to redesign the workflow and do the filing. The first two are process prices. The third is the economics lens asked of an organization: who pays for the routine, and who collects. That predicts the call center. It predicts variation within professions, not just between them — the checkable parts of law and medicine before the judgment calls. And it predicts, above all, where the revolution should be furthest along right now, which is exactly where it is: code. Coding agents are the leading edge of the whole thing, and that is not because programmers are special. It’s because software is the domain where all three prices are lowest at once, low rather than zero: automated tests make much of the output cheap to check, version control makes changes cheap to inspect and reverse, and the person with authority to change the workflow is usually the one typing. This is the same ordering I’ve described from the practitioner’s side: systematic traders were the first white-collar workers whose expertise became legible enough to externalize, decades before lawyers or radiologists, because a trading rule can be written down and checked where courtroom judgment cannot. What’s now being built around coding agents, the carefully written instruction files that catch an agent’s specific, recurring failures and convert its enthusiastic competence into reliable output, is Linnaean labor performed at the level of the individual expert. It is the filing cabinet, rebuilt for one person’s domain. Linnaeus didn’t beat Ray by being a better botanist; he beat him by doing the filing. Code is simply where the filing got cheap first.

On selection, ask what these systems have set aside as unmeasurable, because that is where the next instrument gets built. The answer is the very thing Cowen watches marginalism’s successors discard: the digestible human explanation. A factor-pricing model with 360,000 factors cuts pricing error, and it does so at the price of any story a person can hold in their head about why. We have narrowed to what we can measure, predictive accuracy, and filed understanding under philosophy. That is what every revolution I can find does on the way in. But the pattern above says it is also an invitation, and the invitation has already been accepted: the whole field called interpretability is an attempt to build a balance for the thing the models left off the scale. If it succeeds, it will be the Sen of this story: not collecting a debt, but weighing something the founders said couldn’t be weighed. If it fails, we will have a science that predicts and cannot explain, and we will have to decide whether that counts as knowing. I’d watch that instrument.

What to watch

So here is where the four lenses leave us, and it is neither the breathless version nor the dismissive one. The capability is real, and its measured economic footprint is still thinner than the press release. The structure is wrong, on my bet, in a way that costs money: the automation frame keeps pointing firms at substitution when the measured wins have come from complement. The process is expensive almost everywhere and getting cheap in a few places (code first, the checkable corners of other work next), and where it has gotten cheap, the revolution looks real, because that is what a revolution looking real is. And the selection has been made in the usual way, narrowing to what can be measured, with the instrument for what was left out still under construction, and with who pays for the filing and who collects still deciding where it gets done. It is a map of where the price is still too high, which is what the Tetrad is for.

It is also, historically, the configuration that frustrates everyone: not because the idea is wrong, but because being right was never the hard part.

William Stanley Jevons was correct in 1862, and the world made him wait nine years to matter and decades more to win. The thing that finally cashed his correctness was not a better insight. It was an army of people building the boring, runnable, teachable apparatus that turned a true thing into a thing you could do, and, a century on, someone finding a way to weigh the part they had left off the scale. The marginal revolution did not stick because Jevons was right. It stuck because, after he was right, somebody finally put in the work.

That is the bet on AI, and it is not a bet on capability alone. The benchmarks will probably keep climbing, but that is no longer the interesting question, because capability and filing are complements: a better model needs less scaffolding, and better scaffolding makes the model you already have usable. The interesting question, the one that decides whether this is 1871 or another correct idea waiting in a drawer, is whether the filing gets done, and whether someone builds the instrument for what the filing left out. The verdict is not in. But you know where to look. Watch where the checking is cheap, the mistakes are contained, and someone gets paid to build the routine. That is where the revolution is real today, and it is why it is furthest along in code. Watch the code. Then watch what follows it.
