Outsiders

Or, How the World's Most Historically Open Country Became Almost Impossible to Move To

Samir Varma
Oct 5
 
READ IN APP
 

The India Paradox has been running for a while now, and I keep returning to the same uncomfortable question: why does a civilization that spent five thousand years welcoming everyone — traders, conquerors, refugees, mystics, merchants, the merely lost — now have so few international migrants in it? This post is my attempt to answer that. As usual, the answer involves the British, Jawaharlal Nehru, and a bureaucratic form that must be submitted in triplicate.

Here is a number that should be embarrassing.

According to UN DESA’s 2024 International Migrant Stock, India has about 4.8 million international migrants. That sounds like a lot until you remember that India has roughly 1.45 billion people, which means migrants constitute just 0.3 percent of the population. Less than one-third of one percent. The UAE, by contrast, is 74 percent international migrants on the same measure. Singapore is 48.7 percent. The United States — which is culturally suspicious of immigrants in a way that India historically never was — runs at about 15 percent.

(Data note: UN’s “international migrant stock” counts long-term residents, mainly by country of birth where available and by citizenship where not. That is a different count from legal citizenship, expat headcounts, or visa categories, and it is the cleanest apples-to-apples comparison available, so it is the measure used throughout this post.)

India, the civilization that built its entire prosperity model on being the crossroads of the ancient world, has one of the lowest migrant shares among major economies. This is what economists call a suboptimal outcome. What I call it cannot be printed in a family-friendly newsletter.

A low migrant share has more than one cause. Wages, infrastructure, and quality of life would keep the number low under any visa regime. That leaves a narrower question: why make life harder for the people who already want to come?

And here is the part that should make every Indian who has ever lived abroad want to put their head through a wall: India is simultaneously the world’s largest exporter of people. India’s overseas population (NRIs, people of Indian origin, and their descendants) numbers roughly 37 million, according to the Ministry of External Affairs. It is the world’s largest modern diaspora. We have colonized Silicon Valley, the NHS, Downing Street (briefly), and the C-suite of American corporations with the enthusiasm of a civilization that has discovered the concept of “somewhere better to be.”

We send everyone out. Almost no one comes in. We have achieved the demographic equivalent of a one-way valve, except the thing flowing out is human capital.

And the human capital leaving is the expensive kind. In its report published in June 2025, Henley & Partners projected a net outflow of 3,500 millionaires from India in 2025, with an estimated collective investable wealth of $26.2 billion. That is 3,500 out of roughly 310,000 resident millionaires on Henley’s own count, a little over one percent in a year, which sounds small until you recall that it compounds, and that the one percent leaving are the ones with the capital. Henley is an investment migration advisory firm, so it has a commercial interest in making migration seem consequential; take its numbers as directional estimates. The direction is clear: India is the third-largest net loser of millionaires on earth in their ranking, behind only the UK and China. In the same forecasts, the UAE was projected to receive a net inflow of 9,800 millionaires with an estimated $63 billion in investable wealth. India is specifically identified as one of the top source countries for those UAE arrivals. India’s wealthy citizens are, in statistically meaningful numbers, choosing Dubai over staying home.

The particular cruelty of this is that India’s millionaire base grew by 72 percent over the past decade, one of the fastest rates in the world. India is minting new millionaires at remarkable speed. It is just as efficiently exporting them. The valve flows one way.

Five thousand years of “come on in”

To understand how strange this is, you need to understand what India used to be.

In antiquity (we’re talking the late third millennium BCE, when Europe was still mostly mud) Indus cities were already tied into maritime trade with Mesopotamia, exporting cotton textiles, ivory, and shell goods. When the Mauryan Empire connected the Silk Road, Indian ports were doing business with Rome. When the Cholas dominated the Indian Ocean in the 10th and 11th centuries, Arab merchants were setting up shop along India’s coasts, introducing new ideas, new cuisines, new religions, and not being arrested for it.

By 1700 CE, India accounted for roughly 24 percent of global GDP, on Angus Maddison’s estimates. Think about that number. One country, or rather one civilization organized as a loose collection of kingdoms, producing a quarter of everything the world made. That did not mean modern per-capita affluence; much of it reflected population and agrarian scale. But it meant something real: India sat at the center of dense commercial networks, manufacturing traditions, and oceanic trade routes. It was a magnet.

Persians came and stayed. Greeks came with Alexander and some of them never left, their descendants adopting Buddhism and local customs within a generation. The Kushans, the Huns, the Turks, the Central Asians — they all arrived as outsiders and became Indian, conquerors and traders alike, which is a considerably more demanding test than welcoming the willing. When Vasco da Gama rounded the Cape of Good Hope in 1498 looking for spice routes, India was not a discovery. India was a destination. It had been a destination for so long that the Periplus of the Erythraean Sea, a Greek merchant’s navigation guide from the first century CE, reads, at moments, like an ancient Lonely Planet: good anchorage, friendly rulers, cotton available.

Pliny the Elder, Rome’s great chronicler and permanent grouch, complained that India, Arabia, and China were draining the Roman Empire of 100 million sesterces per year in trade deficits. Romans were complaining about their trade deficit with India two thousand years before Donald Trump discovered the concept. And unlike Trump, Pliny had actual spice to show for it.

The Parsis came in the 7th century fleeing the Muslim conquest of Persia. India said: fine, come in. Jewish communities arrived, with some tracing their presence to the fall of Jerusalem in the first century AD. India said: fine, come in. Arab merchants, Portuguese sailors, Armenian traders, Jesuit missionaries: India absorbed them all into its remarkable civilizational ecosystem, which had, as its default setting, the assumption that foreigners brought things worth having.

The economics, because this is a Milton Friedman newsletter wearing a Dave Barry costume: openness behaves, over time, like compound interest. Talent arrives, capital follows, and ideas multiply when people from different traditions collide in the same room.

The proof is sitting in plain sight, right now, in the civilization that displaced India as the world’s dominant economic force. The English-speaking world’s extraordinary two-century run owes less to the English language, parliamentary democracy, or cricket, charming as all three are, than to one habit: the United States above all, but also the UK, Canada, and Australia, have been, with many imperfections and periodic hysterical exceptions, reasonably welcoming to foreigners. America didn’t invent the atomic bomb; it attracted the Europeans who did. Silicon Valley didn’t grow its own talent; it imported it, from India among many other places. Google, Sun Microsystems, Instagram, WhatsApp: co-founded by immigrants. The entire American technology complex is an immigration story dressed up as a meritocracy story.

India was the proof of concept for four thousand years. Then two things happened.

The British left. The suspicion stayed.

The East India Company was incorporated in 1600, reached Surat in 1608, and during Jahangir’s reign secured the right to establish a factory there. They were, at that point, a middling English trading company whose primary distinction was an impressive royal charter and a willingness to sail very long distances for pepper.

A century and a half later, they owned most of the subcontinent.

The lesson India drew from this was the correct one: be careful who you let in. The lesson India applied from this was considerably less correct: let no one in. Which is the difference between “don’t leave your front door unlocked” and “brick up the front door and also the windows and possibly the chimney.”

The British, to be fair, had already done real damage before India got to brick up its own windows. They had dismantled India’s textile industry, destroyed its shipbuilding, deindustrialized Bengal, imposed punitive trade policies that converted a quarter-of-world-GDP economy into a 4-percent-of-world-GDP economy by 1950. They had conducted a masterclass in what economists call extractive colonialism. The empire was profitable for Britain and catastrophic for India.

So India arrived at independence in 1947 with entirely understandable trauma about foreigners, foreign capital, and foreign economic influence. What it did with that trauma was build the License Raj, which I covered in Post 4 and the rest of which I’ve been trying to recover from ever since.

But sympathy stops being accurate about 1947, and by 1950 the honest version starts getting embarrassing.

The East India Company was able to take over India because India in 1600 was a collection of competing kingdoms with no unified military command, no concept of corporate sovereignty, and no way to anticipate that a trading company with a royal charter could function as a sovereign state with an army. It was a genuinely unprecedented situation. The vulnerability was real.

India in 2026 is a nuclear-armed republic of 1.4 billion people with one of the world’s largest militaries, a roughly $4-trillion economy, a permanent seat-adjacent status at every major international forum, and the ability to launch Mars missions for less than the budget of a Hollywood film. Nobody is taking it over. Not a German software entrepreneur. Not a Kenyan investor. Not a group of Singaporean fintech founders who’ve decided they want to build in Mumbai. The East India Company’s trick (show up as traders, acquire a monopoly charter, raise a private army, seize territory) is not available in the current geopolitical environment. It hasn’t been available since roughly 1947. The Immigration and Foreigners Act, 2025, which came into force on 1 September 2025 and replaced the Foreigners Act of 1946 while keeping most of its administrative machinery; the FRRO; the salary floor; the absence of a digital nomad visa: these are not protecting a collection of medieval kingdoms. They are protecting a nuclear power from the threat of... software developers.

This is colonial trauma that has outlived its threat model by three-quarters of a century.

The License Raj treated foreign investment as a threat to be neutralized. Foreign companies needed special permission to operate. The government reserved whole sectors for domestic players. Tariffs reached 146 percent on intermediate goods by 1985, a number so preposterous it belongs in a Monty Python sketch about economic self-harm. Capital controls prevented money from flowing in or out. And the actual physical foreigners? Welcome to visit. Working in India required navigating a bureaucratic labyrinth that made Kafka’s The Trial look like a user-friendly app.

And then, here is the irony, India constitutionally declared itself socialist in 1976. The 42nd Amendment to the Constitution inserted “socialist” into the Preamble, right there between “sovereign” and “secular,” in case anyone needed official confirmation of what the License Raj had been doing anyway. We made it the law. Other democracies tried social democracy. India tried actual socialism, wrote it into the founding document, and then spent the next fifteen years discovering that writing “socialist” in your constitution does not make things work better. It primarily makes your constitution longer and your economy slower.

India’s share of world GDP by 1991: 3 percent. Down from a quarter.

The cure had, not to put too fine a point on it, been worse than the disease.

The current situation, which is only somewhat better

The 1991 reforms helped enormously. Post 19 covers the forgotten heroes who made it happen: P.V. Narasimha Rao, Manmohan Singh, and Montek Singh Ahluwalia, who looked at foreign reserves of three weeks’ worth of imports and decided the socialism experiment was officially over. Since then, India’s economy has grown from $270 billion to roughly $4 trillion. Progress. Real, undeniable progress.

But some instincts are harder to reform than GDP figures.

Today, a foreign national who wants to actually work in India generally must: obtain an Employment Visa before arriving, demonstrate specialized skills “not readily available in India,” be sponsored by an Indian-registered employer or project, and, except for narrow categories such as non-English language teachers and certain embassy staff, earn a gross salary in excess of ₹16.25 lakh per year. If the Employment Visa is valid for more than 180 days, they must register with the Foreigners Regional Registration Office within 14 days of arrival, obtain a Residential Permit, notify the FRRO if they change address, and keep the paperwork gods appeased.

India does have a Permanent Residency Status scheme, but it is essentially an investor track: commit ₹10 crore within 18 months or ₹25 crore within 36 months, and generate at least 20 resident-Indian jobs every financial year. That is a velvet rope with a balance sheet.

There is no digital nomad visa. There is no dedicated founder-residency or startup visa with a clear path to long-term residence, the kind that Portugal, Germany, and Estonia offer. India has a Business Visa for people exploring or establishing ventures, but that is not the same as saying to a foreign founder: come here, build here, live here, and if it works, stay. There is no straightforward path for a foreigner who simply wants to arrive with energy and capital and remain.

Compare this with the UAE, which on UN’s 2024 migrant-stock measure has 8.2 million international migrants, 74 percent of its population. The UAE looked at a desert and said: “What if we invited the whole world?” The result is a roughly $500-billion economy built almost entirely from imported human capital, with few natural advantages beyond hydrocarbons and location. Dubai is the Manhattan of the East because its rulers in the 1970s made a deliberate bet that openness would compound, and they were right.

India sat on 5,000 years of civilizational proof that this strategy works (it was India’s strategy) and decided the UAE had invented it.

The numbers don’t lie, even when they’re embarrassing

Let me put this in proper comparative perspective, because the Friedman in me demands it. All figures below are from UN DESA’s 2024 International Migrant Stock, so we’re comparing like with like.

The UAE: 8.2 million international migrants, 74 percent of its population. Singapore: 2.8 million, 48.7 percent. The United States: 52.4 million, 15.2 percent. The United Kingdom: 11.8 million, 17.1 percent. Ireland: 1.2 million, 23.1 percent. India: 4.8 million, 0.3 percent. (Separately, World Bank data put India’s net migration in negative territory: more people leave each year than arrive.)

China, which is not exactly known for its welcome mat and which I’ll get to in a moment, sits lower still: fewer than 850,000 foreign nationals by its 2020 census, roughly 0.06 percent of its population. China is so closed to foreigners that Tyler Cowen’s Marginal Revolution recently ran the following fact, submitted by a reader, as a genuine data point worth marveling at: Ireland, with a population of 5 million people, has more foreign-born residents in absolute terms than China has foreign nationals. The 2022 Irish census counted 1,017,437 foreign-born residents; China’s 2020 census counted 845,697 foreign nationals in mainland China. The categories differ (Ireland counts the foreign-born, China counts foreign nationals), so here is the like-for-like. On UN’s migrant-stock measure China has more migrants in absolute terms, 1.64 million to Ireland’s 1.2 million, in a country roughly 280 times larger. Ireland’s migrant share is 23.1 percent. China’s is 0.1 percent. This is the kind of statistic that makes you sit quietly for a moment.

Now apply the same logic to India.

India has 4.8 million international migrants in a country of 1.45 billion. Ireland has 1.2 million in a country of 5 million. Ireland has roughly one-quarter of India’s migrant count in a country 280 times smaller. The proportion is 23.1 percent versus 0.3 percent. If India matched Ireland’s migrant share (just Ireland, not the UAE, not Singapore, not America) it would have more than 300 million international migrants living and working and building inside its borders. That is, to be precise, almost the entire population of the United States. Transplanted into India. Contributing to the Indian economy.

The world’s largest democracy, the world’s most historically open civilization, the country that had a quarter of world GDP in part because it was populous, productive, commercially dense, and attractive to outsiders, is nearly 80 times less open to migrants than a small island on the western edge of Europe famous for rain, Guinness, and having produced an improbable number of writers.

This is policy. Specifically, it is the intersection of colonial trauma, socialist ideology, bureaucratic inertia, and a deep cultural suspicion of the very openness that built everything India ever had worth building.

The China comparison, which is instructive in both directions

China being out-opened by Ireland in absolute terms is funny. What China is doing about its internal diversity, however, is not.

China’s National People’s Congress passed, by 2,756 votes to 3 with 3 abstentions, which in Chinese legislative terms counts as vigorous debate, the Law on Promoting Ethnic Unity and Progress, which came into force on 1 July 2026. The law mandates a “shared spiritual home” for the Chinese nation, makes standard Mandarin the basic language of instruction in schools (minority languages are “respected and protected,” which is the kind of sentence that tells you what comes next), and grants prosecutors the power to file public interest lawsuits over violations of ethnic “unity.” In practice: everyone will be Chinese now, and “Chinese” comes to mean a state-managed identity with Han characteristics.

This is, in any ordinary political vocabulary, assimilation legislation. Ethnic minorities (Tibetans, Uyghurs, Mongolians, Zhuang, and the other fifty-plus groups that constitute China’s officially celebrated-but-apparently-insufficiently-unified diversity) are to be folded into a single national identity, by law, under prosecutorial enforcement, as of July 2026.

Now: could anything remotely like this ever be considered in India?

A law this blunt would run headfirst into India’s federalism, its courts, its languages, its states, its parties, and its ancient national hobby of argumentative combustion. Not even under the current BJP government, which has done real and serious damage to India’s secular fabric and which I have criticized throughout this series for precisely that reason. Even Narendra Modi, who has presided over a genuine and troubling narrowing of India’s pluralist tradition, has not proposed, and could not propose, legislation that tells Tamils to stop being Tamil, or Bengalis to stop being Bengali, or Sikhs to stop being Sikh, or the Garo people of Meghalaya to adopt a “shared spiritual home” defined by someone in Delhi.

India has twenty-two officially recognized languages in its Constitution. Hundreds more in actual use. It has never had, in its entire recorded history, a successful project of forced linguistic or cultural assimilation. Such projects were attempted; they failed because India is simply too large, too plural, too argumentative, and too constitutionally committed to diversity for them to work. The Eighth Schedule of the Constitution lists official languages. It does not list a correct one. The Supreme Court has repeatedly protected linguistic minority rights. The federal structure devolves enormous cultural authority to states. And Indian civil society (lawyers, journalists, academics, opposition politicians, and at least one very loud television channel on every side of every argument) would produce a noise level, at any such proposal, that would be audible from low earth orbit.

Here is the paradox in full, crystalline, almost poetic form.

India won’t let foreigners in, but would never try to erase the diversity that’s been accumulating inside for five thousand years. China keeps foreigners out, and now actively legislates away the internal diversity it already has.

India’s problem is a failure of external openness. China’s problem is a terror of internal pluralism. Both are wrong, but they are wrong in completely opposite ways, from completely opposite civilizational instincts.

The Chinese state looks at diversity and sees a threat to be managed. The Indian state looks at diversity and sees — well, India, and would not know how to manage it even if it wanted to, which it doesn’t, entirely. You cannot pass a Shared Spiritual Home law in a country where the spiritual homes include Vaishnavism, Shaivism, Islam, Sikhism, Christianity, Jainism, Buddhism, Zoroastrianism, Judaism, and approximately forty thousand local traditions that don’t have names in any language the government speaks. The spiritual homes are not shared. They are adjacent, overlapping, occasionally feuding, and somehow still standing after five thousand years of proximity.

India is, in this specific sense, built to resist the Chinese move, and that is the right instinct, applied internally. The tragedy is that the same instinct, don’t try to homogenize what works precisely because it isn’t homogenous, has never been applied to the question of who gets to show up in the first place.

What India actually needs

What I am arguing is narrow, because this is the kind of post that invites the wrong kind of misreading. India is leaving extraordinary value on the table by making it difficult, really, genuinely, needlessly difficult, for foreign entrepreneurs, investors, professionals, researchers, and traders to build their lives and businesses in India. Importing cheap labor to compete with Indian workers, selling citizenship to billionaires shopping for a tax haven, becoming the UAE, with its complicated relationship to its non-citizen majority: none of that is on the menu.

The historical template is sitting right there in the archives. The merchant guilds of ancient India didn’t ask visiting traders for their educational qualifications and employer registration documents. The Mughal courts didn’t require incoming Portuguese craftsmen to prove their skills were “not readily available in India.” The Chola ports didn’t demand that Arab merchants demonstrate a minimum annual income before they could unload their boats. And the result of that absorptive commercial order? A civilization that, at key benchmark moments, accounted for roughly a quarter of world output.

The Friedman point: what you open to compounds. What you close depreciates. Human capital flows to where it’s welcome, and so does the capital itself. India doesn’t appear anywhere on the Henley inflows list. It has an investor-residency route (the PRS scheme, that velvet rope with a balance sheet), and it lacks an accessible, predictable route for people whose principal asset is what they can build, not what they can already invest. Nine of the top ten millionaire-destination countries in 2025 run formal investment migration programs that people actually use; India has one on paper and appears nowhere on the list. India’s diaspora, roughly 37 million strong, many of whom have never held Indian citizenship and never will, is working, building, innovating, compounding in other countries, and sending remittances home because the country their families came from won’t make it easy for them to come back, let alone for anyone else to show up.

India did, in a fit of administrative generosity, invent something called the Overseas Citizen of India card — the OCI — introduced in 2005 and launched in 2006. The name suggests dual citizenship; the card is a lifelong multi-entry visa with exemption from FRRO registration, allowing holders to live and work in India indefinitely, which is a meaningful set of rights, more than most countries offer non-citizens. The joke about the name works. The more interesting point is that OCI demonstrates that India already knows how to offer durable residence without granting citizenship. The question is why that welcome should depend so heavily on ancestry and family connection when it could depend on what someone might contribute. A German engineer, a Kenyan entrepreneur, a Brazilian researcher who wants to spend a decade building something in India: they cannot get an OCI card. They can get a maze.

India has built something. The ambition should be circulation, not confinement.

Meanwhile, the foreigners who do manage to navigate the system and get to India tend to report an experience that is (and I say this as someone who loves the country with the complicated love of someone who left it at eighteen) chaotic, warm, overwhelming, infuriating, occasionally magnificent, and absolutely incapable of leaving a well-calibrated person unchanged. India does something to you. Ask any long-term expat. They come for a project and stay for a decade. They come for a yoga retreat and end up running a software company in Bengaluru. They come for a conference and find themselves learning Tamil because the food in Chennai is too good to leave.

This is what economists call a comparative advantage and what I call a civilizational superpower that India is currently squandering with bureaucratic creativity.

A brief word about the irony

There is an irony at the center of all this, and it hurts.

The country that has sent the most people into the world, that has, through sheer civilizational momentum, placed its sons and daughters at the commanding heights of Western technology, medicine, finance, and culture, is the same country making it hardest to come the other direction. We have supplied the CEOs of Microsoft, Google, Adobe, and IBM. We have produced a British Prime Minister, a former American Vice President, a former Mastercard CEO, and an IMF chief economist and first deputy managing director. Our diaspora sent home $129 billion in remittances in 2024, more than any other country.

And yet: to move to India, to build something there, to put your capital and your energy and your working years into that soil, you will spend months with the FRRO, prove that your skills don’t already exist in India (good luck), demonstrate the right salary level, keep copies of everything in triplicate, and still have no clear path to permanent residency.

India has the world’s largest diaspora selling India to the world. India cannot, in return, successfully sell India to foreigners who might want to come.

My great-grandmother Lado Rani Zutshi fought for Indian independence. She was a Sanskrit scholar and a freedom fighter who wanted India to be free from British rule. What she wanted India to be free for was the kind of civilization India had always been: open, plural, commercially dynamic, absorptive of talent from anywhere. The civilization that had merchants from Rome, Persia, Arabia, China, and Southeast Asia in its ports simultaneously. The civilization that was so welcoming to outsiders that it accidentally let the East India Company in.

(We won’t revisit that last part. We’ve all learned the lesson. The lesson is: trade yes, monopoly charters no.)

The fix, which is obvious and therefore will not happen quickly

What should India do? The answer is depressingly simple and therefore politically complicated.

Create a digital nomad visa. Dozens of countries have done it. An Indian version would attract exactly the kind of globally mobile, high-income professional who will spend money, transfer skills, establish networks, and tell everyone they know that India is the place to be.

Create an entrepreneur visa that doesn’t require the applicant to already have an Indian employer. The entire point is to attract people who want to create the employer. Portugal does this. Germany does this. Chile does this.

Build a clear, time-bound path to permanent residency for people who have been contributing to India’s economy for five or more years. Not eighteen months of maybe. A legible process with predictable outcomes.

And, most radical of all, make the Foreigners Regional Registration Office something other than a source of trauma for anyone who encounters it. The FRRO dates from an era when the government’s default assumption about any foreigner was that they were probably up to something suspicious. That assumption was understandable in 1947. In 2026, it is a tax on the future.

None of this is hard in theory. All of it is politically complicated in practice, because the suspicion of foreigners that comes from having been colonized by what started as a trading company still shapes how India thinks about economic openness. That suspicion is not irrational. It is, however, expensive.

Singapore also has a colonial history. It decided to be open anyway, and it became one of the wealthiest countries on earth. Its per capita income is over thirty times India’s at current exchange rates. The lesson is not hiding in the decimal places.

The lesson is available. The case study is documented. The model works.

All India needs to do is remember what it used to be.

A personal note

I’ve been writing about India for long enough now that I sometimes wonder what my own history in this would look like if the flows had been different. My father, a Naval Commander and one of the finest sports commentators India produced, spent his career in a country where foreigners were an anomaly. My mother spent nearly six decades practicing law in a system largely inherited from the British and heavily modified by Nehru’s planners. The India they knew was magnificent and maddening in equal measure, but it was not a place where someone showed up from Hamburg or Nairobi or São Paulo and built a life.

Imagine if it were. Imagine if the cooking, the culture, the chaos, the climate, all of it, were accessible to anyone who wanted to show up with energy and ideas. Imagine if the next wave of Mughal craftsmen, Roman traders, and Persian poets arrived not on dhows but on Emirates flights, and found, instead of the FRRO, a country that said: “You want to build something here? Great. Here’s your visa. Let’s go.”

That India existed for five thousand years. At its height, it produced roughly a quarter of everything.

It’s still in there somewhere. Behind the registration forms. Behind the e-FRRO portal. Behind the bureaucratic accretion of a century of fear.

The question is whether India will find it again before the rest of the world stops waiting.
