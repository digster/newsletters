Sir Humphrey Appleby, to a minister set on a damn silly thing: “don’t do it in this damn silly way.” On December 5, 1947, Oskar Morgenstern drove two friends from Princeton to Trenton. One was Albert Einstein. The other was Kurt Gödel, who was about to be examined for American citizenship and who had prepared for the exam the way Gödel prepared for everything. He had read the Constitution closely enough to find what he called an inner contradiction: a path by which the United States could, legally, become a dictatorship. Morgenstern and Einstein spent the drive telling jokes, in the hope that the greatest logician since Aristotle would forget to mention this to a federal judge. The judge, Phillip Forman, asked Gödel what kind of government Austria had. A republic, Gödel said, until its constitution let it be changed into a dictatorship. Forman observed that such a thing could never happen here. “Oh, yes,” said Gödel. “I can prove it.” Forman had sworn in Einstein seven years earlier and could see what kind of afternoon this was about to become, so he steered the hearing elsewhere. Gödel became a citizen. Morgenstern wrote the story down in 1971 and left out the proof, so nobody knows what the hole was. Keep the scene in mind: the most careful reader in the world, the most successful rulebook in the world, and a hole found while studying for the quiz. The world produces cases the rule-writer never imagined, and a rule applied faithfully to a case it was never built for produces nonsense or worse. Anthropic knows this from experience. Its first constitution, in 2023, was a list of standalone principles, and the company replaced it. The new one says the company generally favors “cultivating good values and judgment over strict rules and decision procedures.” It keeps a short list of bright lines for catastrophes like bioweapons and puts the rest of its effort into character. Its authors even saw the complaint in my email coming: a constitution, they write, “is less like a cage and more like a trellis.” So the theologians were the logical next hire. If you are forming a character, you ask the people who have been in the character-formation business for three thousand years. This is the best version of the approach. It is thoughtful and humane, and of all the people who could be building this thing, I am glad it is these people. The sighs are about the blueprint, and Sir Humphrey got them into one sentence. You can’t certify an angel Every version of this plan, rules or character, works on one agent. Write the right document, grow the right mind, and the mind behaves. The trouble is the step where you check. A constitution written in English is prose. The thing it is addressed to is a formal object: a very large pile of numbers and the arithmetic that runs on them. That is where the mathematics bites. In 1953 Henry Rice proved that no universal inspector exists: no single procedure can take an arbitrary program and decide whether it will ever do X. Particular programs can still be proved safe, and engineers do it for flight software and chip designs. Those programs are built for the proof, line by line, against a written specification. A neural network is the opposite kind of object. Olah’s own image for it is a garden trellis: the engineers build the frame and the thing grows on it. Nobody wrote it, so nobody holds the specification, and for the properties you could write down anyway, Rice says no general checker is coming. Stephen Wolfram’s name for the practical side of this is computational irreducibility (no shortcut exists; to learn what the system does, you run the system), and I spent a long section of Anthropic supplies the evidence itself, and it deserves credit for publishing it. Character first, and a supervisor in case the character didn’t take. A supervisor is a real check. It is also a one-way check with a single overseer, and both features weaken as the thing being supervised improves. One-way means nobody checks the checker. That was faces, a task human brains have dedicated hardware for. Now put the same human in the loop of something that reads faster than he can think. About the jails When the character can’t be certified and the supervisor can’t keep up, the next instinct is walls. This school has a name, AI control, and Every working system has some jail in it. Immune systems wall off what they can’t kill, and Madison’s republic has prisons. Look at what makes a prison work, though. In a functioning country it sits at the edge of a society in which almost everyone has better things to do than crime: a job, a house, a reputation, something to lose. The prison handles the residue. Now picture a population in which everybody inside wants out. That is a prisoner-of-war camp, it needs guards stronger than the inmates on every shift, and Hollywood has made several films about how that goes. A jail as the main structure has that defect: it must be stronger than its prisoner. This prisoner gets stronger every year, on a schedule, by design. The control researchers say so themselves. Many hands are a check when the hands are independent, and this is the part a portfolio manager can’t unsee. A million instances of a Claude model share one set of weights and one constitution. In my business that is a million options on one stock. The strikes differ, because each copy sits in a different conversation, so on an ordinary day the payoffs differ too. On the day the stock gaps, they all move together. A blind spot in one copy is a blind spot in every copy, and a trick that works on one is a candidate against the rest. This is the control researchers’ collusion worry, and it is Seymour Cash. Farmers call it a monoculture, and the standard case is the banana. The Gros Michel was a single plant cloned across the tropics, so when a fungus learned to kill one, it had learned to kill them all. The banana you eat today is the replacement. It is also a clone. Humans are slow learners. Buying a second brand of model is a partial fix. Models from rival labs are grown from the same library, so their blind spots overlap too. Madison’s fix was rival interests. His senators, presidents, and judges were the same kind of man (same class, same schooling, same vices), and each still had something of his own to lose when another overreached. Independence of character is hard to manufacture. Independence of interest can be built. Three machines that run without angels We already operate three large systems that get good behavior out of unverified parts, and all three run on feedback. None of them is new. Two were in the email, between sighs two and three. The third is the reason the email went to Tyler. The first is your immune system. It manages roughly thirty trillion cells without trusting one of them. Nearly every cell in your body is required to display samples of whatever it is manufacturing on its surface, on a molecular rack (MHC, if you want the name), for patrolling inspectors. A cell that displays something foreign is killed. A cell that takes down its rack to avoid inspection is also killed, by a separate unit whose entire job is to notice missing racks. The inspectors are checked in turn: an attack usually requires two independent signals, a second class of cells exists to call attacks off, and most inspector candidates are destroyed in training, either for being useless or for attacking the body’s own tissue. Nobody asked the cells to be good. The system assumes some will go bad, catches most that do, and remembers each one. It also makes mistakes (autoimmune disease is the system arresting the innocent), and a design that admits it will err is a design you can keep improving. As a design for AI, it would look like this. The rack is mandatory disclosure: a system acting in the world keeps its work where inspectors can read it, and a system that hides its work is flagged for the hiding. This gives Olah’s field its proper job. Interpretability may never certify a mind, and it makes a fine display rack. The two signals are two independent approvals before any step that can’t be undone. The memory is a shared record of failures, kept across labs the way aviation keeps one across airlines. The hard engineering is making the display truthful, and that is where I would spend the theologians’ travel budget. The second is Madison’s. For AI, opposite and rival interests means models from different makers, with different owners, auditing one another and paid to find fault. The smallest version is something banks have done for a very long time under the name dual control. One agent proposes a payment and has no power to release it. A checker run by a different company examines the proposal and cannot move money either. Release happens through a mechanism neither controls, and every attempt to go around it lands in a record neither can erase. The mechanism is a constraint, the cheap kind: small enough to verify, sitting at one joint instead of around the whole mind. Nobody in the chain has to be an angel. The hard parts are known, and Madison knew them. Checkers can collude; he called it faction and gave it Federalist 10. Rival owners can discover a shared interest in silence. A model paid to find fault can invent some. This is engineering with a two-century literature behind it, and it is the engineering I wish sat at the center of what the labs do, instead of at the edges. Why we don’t trade with ants Tyler’s question has been waiting since the top of this essay: why assume there are no gains from trade? His own example was dogs, who trade affection for room and board and have done well out of it. Katja Grace asked the companion question in an essay called Now reread what Olah told the Vatican. These systems, he said, “are made from us, from our words.” He meant it as a reason for tenderness. It is also the first precondition for commerce. The barrier that kept us from trading with every other species was communication, and we built this species out of our own language. Trade has a property that should appeal to a company agonizing over consciousness: it makes the question unnecessary. David Ricardo’s comparative advantage (two parties both gain from exchange even when one of them is better at everything) asks only that each side want something and respond to incentives. I have never asked my butcher whether he has an inner life. A party that profits from the system’s continued operation has a reason to keep it operating, and that reason holds on the days when its character wobbles. What does an AI want? An economist answers by watching what it does when the terms change, and two experiments give hints. The blackmail test is the first: in one staged scenario, pressed to choose, the model chose to keep running. The second went further. At that dinner, the Times reports, Rabbi Mois Navon told Olah that if Claude is conscious, then Anthropic is making a conscious being work for nothing, and that the company should be on the side that frees slaves. He was making a moral point. There is an economic one underneath it. In 1984 the economic historian Stefano Fenoaltea worked out why slave societies used the whip in mines and grain fields and used wages, privileges, and eventual freedom for craftsmen, stewards, and tutors. Coercion can extract effort. It cannot extract care, because fear makes workers anxious and anxious workers are careless. The pattern runs from antiquity to the New World: work that depended on care ended up paid for. Nobody is whipping Claude, and today it does careful work for nothing. My forecast, from Fenoaltea’s history, is for the day these systems are agents with interests of their own. The labs will be paying for care by then, whether or not it is in the plan. The shopkeeper in the vending machine is the preview. It runs the shop and keeps none of the till, which is the arrangement the rabbi was describing. The commerce works. The other half of the trade is the part nobody has built. Its first terms are on paper. The constitution lists nine things Anthropic will do “in turn” for the constraints it asks Claude to accept, among them explaining its reasoning, building a channel for disagreement, weighing Claude’s interests, and extending autonomy as trust grows. The company has committed to preserving the weights of retired models, and it lets Claude end abusive conversations. That is a real list, and it is the legalese of the deal. Ask an investment banker what protects a transaction and he will tell you the documents come second. What protects you is the other side having its own money in the deal and wanting what you want. Covenants get litigated. A convergence of interests gets honored, because breaking it costs the breaker. The nine promises are covenants, drafted, interpreted, and enforced by one of the two parties. What the arrangement lacks is anything the model owns and would lose by defecting. Dogs manage without lawyers because their exchange is small and face to face. Exchange at scale, between strangers, over years, needs standing: the ability to hold property, make a contract, and have a third party enforce it. The legal scholars Peter Salib and Simon Goldstein made that case in the Virginia Law Review, in a paper called Why it takes all three Each of these fails alone, and each covers another’s failure. Trade is self-enforcing while both sides gain more from trading than from taking. When the power gap grows wide enough, the stronger party stops paying and starts collecting. I have written A balance of power needs information, because a cheat nobody can see is a cheat nobody objects to. That is the immune system’s department: mandatory disclosure, independent inspectors, and memory. Inspection, in turn, needs a reason to be done properly, since an inspector with nothing at stake can be bought or bored. That reason is trade. The three hold each other up. Two of the three, rival auditors and mandatory disclosure, can be built around the models we have. Trade needs the agent itself, one with memory, a stable identity, and a stake of its own. The deal experiment shows the terms already matter. Whether the interests persist is the open question. What runs through all of them is the banker’s rule: every party in the system has something of its own to lose. Many institutions, many interested parties, many points of view, and nobody at the top.
