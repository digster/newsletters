When Hernán Cortés landed in the New World, the Aztec capital of Tenochtitlan was one of the greatest cities anywhere on earth. It had more than twice the population of Seville, and was (as my grade school history textbooks always nervously assured me) cleaner, more orderly, and more magnificent than any contemporaneous European city. Its ochre towers and pyramids rose like a dream from the shimmering waters of Lake Texcoco. Unlike the organic maze of alleyways that characterized most Spanish cities, Tenochtitlan’s public gardens and temple districts and grand processional ways were a product of the sort of high modernist urban planning that Europe wouldn’t really see until Haussmann’s Paris in the 19th century. The walls of the city shone with lime and were decorated with paintings, so it glimmered like a bauble in the Mexican sun. The soldiers who followed Cortés on the one hand described it as so ethereal it seemed the three causeways joining it to the mainland were anchors without which it might float away, but on the other hand were overawed by the towering height and hallucinatory bulk of the Great Pyramid topped with its twin temples to Huitzilopochtli and Tlaloc. In addition to being an architectural miracle, the city and the empire which fed it were also an economic and a social miracle. The Valley of Mexico has a dangerously irregular growing season, lacks crucial minerals, and is frequently subject to earthquakes, volcanoes, drought, and destructive floods. The ancestors of the Mexica wandered into the fertile basin that was to become their empire sometime in the late 1200s or early 1300s. They found it already settled with a mosaic of mostly Nahuatl-speaking city-states, and spent a few decades as mercenaries or tributaries for various stronger powers. One of those petty wars went badly, and they finally settled down as bedraggled refugees on the muck-covered islands that were to be the site of their future glory. Within a hundred years they were the most powerful city in the valley. Fifty years after that they were the unquestioned hegemon and begin to launch wars of imperial expansion towards the Pacific and Caribbean coasts. Another fifty years of imperial glory, and then they were destroyed utterly. Or as Clendinnen puts it, “a man whose grandfather had fought as a hireling in another city’s wars in the Mexica’s early days in the valley would himself have seen the glory of their achieved empire, and his son watched its destruction.” None of this would have come as a surprise to the Mexica.¹ Their entire cosmology revolved around the absolute precarity of the world, and they believed that all earthly things were balanced on a knife’s edge between ravenous abyssal powers on one side and the emptiness of the void on the other. Every fifty-two years the entire nation waited breathlessly at the ceremony of the New Fire to see whether the world would continue or not. On a lonely hill outside of the city, senior priests feverishly worked a hand drill upon the naked breast of a bound captive. If the gods sent fire, it meant the world was saved, and the victim’s chest was immediately torn open so that the flame could be fed with his heart and flesh, bubbling and growing within the chest cavity to the relieved cheers of the populace before being carried to the major temples of the city. Everybody knew that if the fire did not come, the world would slip into nothingness and stop existing. Before we are tempted to roll our eyes at this and ask how suspenseful it really could have been, remember that they only got to do it three or four times between the foundation of their empire and its extinction. That empire was not gentle. Few empires are, but even by the sad standards of human history, the Mexica were extreme. I’m not speaking here about their legendarily creative and inventive ways of humiliating subject peoples,² I’m speaking about the human sacrifices. No, these were not exaggerated by the colonizers, as some revisionist historians would have you believe.³ If anything, the Spaniards lacked the conceptual framework to fully comprehend the industrial-scale, ever-so-civilized and bureaucratic evil of the Mexica, and so they inadvertently downplayed it. The reality, as Clendinnen brilliantly documents, is that human sacrifice was not an occasional or incidental religious custom for the Mexica, like an extra-spicy church service for the people who only show up on Christmas or Easter. No, the mass-butchery of wailing, pleading captives of both sexes and all ages was the core activity around which their entire civilization and society revolved. Their art, their music, their poetry, their approach to warfare, their entire economic system, their kaleidoscopic array of holidays and festivals, all were singularly dedicated to the killing (often preceded by inventive torture) of helpless human beings. And everybody had their role to play:
The scale of it boggles the mind. The Mexica record with pride a single festal occasion on which over twenty thousand victims were sacrificed, the blood rolling in a slow curtain down the steps of the pyramids as four lines of manacled slaves trudged along the great processional ways towards their doom. It was the job of special priests to greet the captives as they entered the city and to explain in detail the honor that was to await them. And it was always human beings. Many primitive cultures have practiced forms of animal sacrifice. The Mexica were neither primitive, nor interested in animals. There was also no “othering” at work here. The Mexica knew that they were killing people who thought and felt and dreamed and loved as they did. Indeed, that was the entire point of it. They constructed an efficient and well-run machine that had as its sole purpose delivering living, breathing, conscious and informed human beings to the killing-stone or the furnace. A whole battery of sophisticated psychological techniques were employed to ensure the compliance of the victims to the end.⁴ Yes, the Spaniards lacked the vocabulary to describe it, but we who live in the aftermath of the twentieth century do not. Clendinnen writes: “It is the combination of violence with apparent impersonality, the bureaucratic calculation of these elaborated Mexica brutalities, together with their habituated and apparently casual incorporation into the world of the everyday, which chills.” And it’s obvious that she’s thinking of the Holocaust. Indeed, of all the twentieth-century genocides that one still holds a special horror because its perpetrators were not uneducated peasants and yokels but mannered men with a taste for Beethoven and Brahms. The baby was addressed as a “little captive” and at his naming ceremony the neighborhood boys were recruited to stage a fake triumph in imitation of those that accompanied noble prisoners being led into the city to the killing stone. Every bit of ceremony surrounding the birth of a baby boy was designed to reinforce one thing to the shaken, post-partum, emotionally and physically vulnerable mother: “This boy is not yours. He belongs to the state. He was made for warfare. He is a resource. He will die young, violently, in a faraway place.” Prior to the advent of twentieth century totalitarianism, I cannot think of another society that thought to challenge the bond between mother and newborn like this. Most of us see the image of a woman with a babe at her breast and see in it the ultimate rebuke to kings and priests, we see something primordial, something older and more powerful than any claim to authority made by mere men. Not so for the Mexica. The world these boys were being prepared for was a brutal one. There’s a recurring temptation, baked deep into our worldview here in the enlightened West, to think that every society is basically the same. It’s partly condescension, partly ideological allergy to anything that could even tangentially imply that some people are better than others, and partly because for the Clendinnen has no time for any of this. She knows that of course, as soon as she starts talking about human sacrifices some lib is going to bring up medieval European public executions and judicial torture, and she bristles at it: “Such events were relatively infrequent, and certainly peripheral to the dailiness of life, while their victims, however pitiable, could be seen as culpable to some degree… Mexica victims were purely victims.” Well, it wasn’t just the sacrificial victims. Mexica society’s treatment of people in general was very different from anything to which we’re accustomed: “Public physical humiliation and extreme violence is used economically in most polities, and against the prestigious rarely and as a last resort: in Tenochtitlan it was used extravagantly, publicly, and as a first resort.” Much of the education of the Mexica child was designed to prepare them for this future by giving them a taste of it. As soon as they were weaned (more about Mexica babies in a moment) they would be subjected to discipline that was physical, immediate, and savage. The abuse had a purpose beyond preparing children to face society; it also prepared them to face the gods. The Mexica brought their characteristic fatalism to child-rearing as to everything else. Faced with the bleakness of reality, the only true tenderness was to toughen up a boy so that he could bear the existential weight of it. Beyond a certain age, the family could not be trusted to carry out that toughening process properly, and so all Mexica boys were removed from their families and placed in special schools. The two main types of school that Clendinnen covers are the warrior schools and the priest schools. In theory, every Mexica man was a warrior and an initiate in the cult of Huitzilopochtli, so everybody started out in warrior school, and then some people stuck around and specialized while others transferred to other occupations. The main thing that’s noteworthy about these schools, besides their brutality, is how absolutely obsessed with individual performance and how anti-teamwork they were. Success in the schools was zero sum. Whether in physical challenges, social competition, or the scrum of combat itself, you could only succeed and rise if your peers failed. Priest school worked much the same way, but even more surreal — a priest was expected to have perfect poise, and so the boys would be tested on their ability to place small round objects in very precise locations after enduring days of starvation, constant plunges in cold water so their hands would shake, and horrifying bodily mortification. One of the duties of those who succeeded was to punish the failures, publicly humiliate them, and expel them from the school. In this way, the lesson was drummed home that one man’s rise meant another’s fall. This same zero-sum competition extended to the Mexica army, which as soon as battle was joined became not an army but a swirling melee of individual glory-seekers. The purpose of the Mexica warrior was not the victory of the group, but competition with his peers, and the way to win the competition was to secure captives for sacrifice to Huitzilopochtli. This singular fact drove almost everything about Mexica combat strategy, or the lack thereof. Great warriors wore towering feather headdresses to attract opponents so that they could be disabled and sacrificed. The Mexica had projectile weapons, but specifically designed them to be non-lethal and only employed them for demoralization. The Mexica wielded long, shallow obsidian blades and flint-studded clubs that were designed to incapacitate by cutting a hamstring or crushing a knee, rather than to kill. The only way to advance as a Mexica warrior was to sacrifice more captives than your peers,⁸ and the incentives of the system shaped everything else. So long as their only opponents were the brutalized members of smaller neighboring tribes it all continued to work, but I think this odd approach to combat almost entirely explains their extreme underperformance on meeting the Spanish. This leads us to another whole category of Mexica cosmology, somehow darker and more awful than anything we’ve encountered yet. You see, there are the “gods” as in the celestial beings that are not of this world — remote, unconcerned, casually destructive. But there is another category of “gods” that are as bound to the earth as humanity is; they are the lords of the earth, and they will die with the earth.⁹ What distinguished the earth lords was their nightmarish hunger. In all the Mexica creation myths there is strife in heaven (this mirrors their social arrangements — one must fall that another may rise). The earth deities are cast out, or torn asunder, or flayed, or dismembered. But they are gods. They cannot die. The earth gods came in all different shapes and forms, and they filled the interstices of the Mexica myth-world. At the top of the pyramid of Huitzilopochtli captives had their hearts torn out for his glory, but at the base of the pyramid was an image of his outraged older sister and victim, the earth goddess Coyolxauhqui, eyes closed in death, bones jutting from torn flesh, but fanged mouth wide open and eternally hungering for the plundered corpses flung down the stone steps. Less grandiose was the terrible phantom ‘Night Axe’, a “headless torso scuttling along the ground, its split chest opening, then shutting with the dull ‘chunk’ of an axe blow heard in the night.” (The Mexica believed that a man who encountered Night Axe and reached into its gaping wound could pluck out the signs of his individual fate.) The earth lords were vindictive and sadistic, and much of Aztec ceremony — the daily offerings of blood, the human hearts pulped into stone mouths — was designed to temporarily satisfy their hunger and keep them at bay. But unfortunately they could not be avoided. In fact, the entire structure of earthly life conspired to interweave human destiny with that of the earth gods. As lords of the earth, the fruits of the earth belonged to them, and by ingesting those gifts every human being entered into involuntary servitude to these beings. Agriculture, the systematic extraction of the flesh of the gods, was a metaphysically dangerous game to play.
This is why for the Mexica, the moment of weaning was the single most important event of a person’s life.¹⁰ A baby who had only ever suckled at his or her mother’s breast was free, mercifully free, from the human condition. If you died before weaning, your spirit would return to the warm garden of Tonacatecutli to suck upon one of the innumerable breasts of the ‘Wet-Nurse Tree.’ Weaning would irrevocably and destructively alter a child’s relationship with the gods — as soon as they had taken the plants of the earth into their mouth, the child was flung into a total and involuntary obligation to the ravening spirits of the earth, an obligation that could be paid for only with one’s flesh and death. The Mexica were some of the original ecological thinkers, in that they recognized all human life and civilization as just a transient stage in the natural cycle of vegetable growth, decomposition, fertilization, and regrowth. But unlike their ideological descendants, the Mexica savored the bleak reality and existential dread that the phrase “circle of life” ought to connote. Women had an especially close connection to the earth deities, because the fertile womb that brought forth children was obviously tied to the fertile earth bringing forth maize and beans, in an endless cycle of which human life was but one temporary stage. The terrible presence that overcame women in childbirth, as men were possessed by Huitzilopochtli on the battlefield, was Cihuacoatl — she of the gaping mouth, the ferocious teeth, the endless hunger. The mere shadow of her presence could injure any living thing, so even in the case of a normal birth, great care was taken that visitors to the household in the days that followed should ward themselves against her influence. But if a woman died in childbirth, in the “fatal time for us poor women… our kind of war,” then the dark power of Cihuacoatl would suffuse her body and extreme measures had to be taken. The corpse could not be removed from the house by the doorway, but instead the mourners would break open a back wall and remove it in the dead of the night. The husband would carry the corpse, escorted by a party of old women and midwives, to bury it at a crossroads. There he would keep vigil for four days as the magical power slowly dispersed, fending off any grave-robbers desperate to seize a fragment of her flesh for use in potions or talismans. After he departed, leaving no marker or indication at the place, she would be reborn as a “Celestial Princess” or a “Devil Woman” — a malevolent spirit, eternally angry, haunting the peripheral places and inimical to all life but especially to children. The Celestial Princesses caused birth defects and deformities, struck down teenagers with convulsions or illness, and when at some future New Fire ceremony the sun should fail and the world should end, it was they who would lead the ravening horde of earth powers to destroy the world. So much for the earth gods. But in the great sweep of Mexica religion, there was one more being who fit neither with the indifferent and aloof celestials that devastated humanity with natural disasters out of sheer disinterest, nor with the bound and mutilated earth lords to whom one’s life was owed as soon as one had tasted maize gruel. There was one entity who broke the pattern. He stood above and outside the world, but the world was a place of exile and danger precisely because he held it within his hand. Tezcatlipoca. “The Lord of the Here and Now.” Tezcatlipoca. “The Enemy on Both Sides.” If the other celestials were lumbering giants who occasionally kicked over the anthill that was the earth, Tezcatlipoca was a sadistic child with a magnifying glass. Tezcatlipoca was intensely interested in the doings of men, and the Mexica cowered under his gaze and prayed for it to pass them by. Tezcatlipoca’s laughter signaled total destruction, and of Tezcatlipoca it was said: “He only mocketh. Of no-one can he be a friend, to no-one true.” His other names were Moyocoyatzin, ‘Capricious Creator’, Titlacahuan, ‘He Whose Slaves We Are’, and Moquequeloa, ‘The Mocker’. He was fickleness personified, his will was utterly unpredictable and implacable, and he delighted in elevating a man, making him virtuous, rich, and proud, and then suddenly and inexplicably destroying him. Tezcatlipoca was the god of kings and earthly rulership. Tezcatlipoca was also the god of slaves, who were known as ‘beloved sons of Tezcatlipoca.’ This was not out of some pseudo-Christian “last shall be first” beatitude, but because “their calamitous position dramatized the pure arbitrariness of individual destiny.” The influence of Tezcatlipoca pervaded every bit of Mexica society. Good fortune must always be accepted warily, because more than likely it was the setup to a cruel joke by Tezcatlipoca. Young mothers were cautioned not to be too pleased with their babies, lest the Lord of the Here and Now notice and destroy the child. Bouquets of flowers were to be sniffed “only at the outer edges: the swooning sweetness at the centre belonged not to men but to Tezcatlipoca… We are all slaves of Tezcatlipoca, ‘He Whose Slaves We Are’, victims of the giant unpredictability of the world.” The will of Tezcatlipoca was also made manifest in the brutal, public torture and execution of a warrior or priest who stumbled: “his warrior regalia stripped from him, his scalp lock shorn, his heart excised, his emptied body broken into its parts and dispersed to be eaten… That looming reality cast its shadow back over the adulation, the triumphs, to reveal the desperate fragility of the… seasoned warrior. Just as the works of Lovecraft and Mexica religion both hold that we occupy a small and insignificant corner of the cosmos, perhaps every human society that we have seen or experienced makes up just a tiny sliver of the possibility space of human organization.
