Longreads

 * Dwarkesh Patel interviews Robert Caro [https://www.thediff.co/r/534dc19e?m=f90780fc-498d-4ffe-a546-8a3e61574e99]. This one's a long time coming: the prediction market on who his "biggest guest yet" was launched two years ago [https://www.thediff.co/r/da5f8aa2?m=f90780fc-498d-4ffe-a546-8a3e61574e99], and here's a bit from Dwarkesh's podcast five years ago on similarities between Caro's subjects and his research style [https://www.thediff.co/r/7f7cadea?m=f90780fc-498d-4ffe-a546-8a3e61574e99]. It's a very fun interview, though it helps to have done the couple thousand pages of reading in advance. One thing it does well is to capture that Caro is, of course, not writing hagiographies, but is writing stories about people who sought power for what were often healthy reasons, and, over time, got consumed by wielding it. One thing that makes him so exceptional is that a powerful person produces lots of sources—they're important enough to have a driver, assistants, etc., not to mention lots of people who carried out their will, got thwarted by them, etc. But it's also hard to reach them! So our view of what power is and what it does to people is shaped, in part, by decisions Robert Caro made decades ago about which people to study.
 * Patrick Fitzsimmons in Works in Progress on the collapse of the bronze age [https://www.thediff.co/r/9a297cac?m=f90780fc-498d-4ffe-a546-8a3e61574e99]. One of the craziest things about the bronze-to-iron-age transition is that, like the mid-twentieth century productivity boom, it was accompanied by a reduction in economic complexity, at least as measured by trade. Bronze is an alloy of copper and tin, and tin is hard to find, so the bronze age was an age of states large enough to arrange imports from as far as Cornwall and Afghanistan. Once ironworking, with its shorter supply chains, was a viable option, the optimal size of a state was smaller, and there was less need for trade. (Trade tends to be viral in that a ship isn't necessarily going to be 100% full of exactly one commodity, in both directions, so one high-demand commodity introduces people to other commodities, and round trips encourage exports as well as imports.) This trend, of course, continues to the present.
 * Joseph Heath has a piece that's surprisingly broad given its length, touching on topics that range from commercial ethics to the history of ideology as a motivator to the dynamic where big abstract ideas are built on, but compete with, more specific ones [https://www.thediff.co/r/0302ea7a?m=f90780fc-498d-4ffe-a546-8a3e61574e99]. Highly recommended. One of the things that struck me early in parenting is that "civilization" refers, not to a static thing, but to a process, like "cultivation": it's the constant, relentless effort by parents (and teachers, and peers, and sometimes law enforcement) to take humans who are born with instincts designed for hunter-gatherers and mold them into well-behaved taxpayers. It's not a thing that is, but a thing we do.
 * Nathan Heller in The New Yorker profiles James Daunt, CEO of Barnes & Noble [https://www.thediff.co/r/410c1f77?m=f90780fc-498d-4ffe-a546-8a3e61574e99]. One reason retail is tough is that if you fix up an in-store experience, the customers you've lost still have to find their way back. So the temptation is to go in the other direction: Barnes & Noble kept giving more store real estate to non-toys, which temporarily drove some sales but also gradually turned them into a mediocre toy store that happened, incongruously, to have books. To turn that around and make a chain of bookstores a chain of good bookstores, you need someone in charge who has a mandate to accept lower revenue for a bit in exchange for restoring customer foot traffic over time. But who can do that? They need to have credibility as both a respectable bookstore operator and as someone who understands business and won't just use the long term to excuse poor performance. Barnes & Noble, and Waterstones in the UK, really lucked out here, in that James Daunt used to be a banker, then quit to start an independent bookstore, and only then got recruited to run a big chain.
 * John Collison interviews Sam Altman on Cheeky Pint [https://www.thediff.co/r/e1887f88?m=f90780fc-498d-4ffe-a546-8a3e61574e99]. One fun way to be contrarian and right about current events is to look at the absence of headlines. Altman notes that so far, high-profile software vulnerabilities haven't led to the kind of chaos we'd reasonably expect (Binance might disagree, but they're a very tempting target to hack). Altman also points out that we may be due for yet another new scaling law, since we don't entirely know the optimal tradeoff is between agent count and agent intelligence, nor do we know what happens when you mix together heterogeneous agents. (We've seen agents coordinate, but will they get more or less effective if they argue more?)
 * This week in Capital Gains, we have more thoughts on tranched rounds [https://www.thediff.co/r/fb825122?m=f90780fc-498d-4ffe-a546-8a3e61574e99], where different investors in the same venture round pay different prices. In some ways, it's an extension of what's always happened in venture, where bigger investors get board seats, information rights, etc. But it also creates some bad incentives.
