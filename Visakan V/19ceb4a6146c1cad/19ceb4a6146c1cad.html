13 questions I ask my marketing clients (and why)

distilled from 20 years of marketing experience

visakan veerasamy
Mar 14
 
READ IN APP
 

Since leaving my full-time marketing job in 2018, I’ve done marketing consults for almost a hundred clients. Many of them have had great results: clarified their positioning, grew their followings, got more users/customers for their products, made more revenue– all without compromising their sense of what they’re doing. Better still, many of them feel less constrained, see opportunities more clearly, and enjoy their work more.

I write this because I’m ready to take on more clients (DM me!), and also because I’d like to consolidate what I’ve learned, and share it freely with others.

Also just to be clear, I don’t just bombard people with all of these questions at once. I start somewhere, pay attention to what they say, and intuit what would be the most useful path to take.

1. “What do you do, and what problem are you looking to solve?”

@visakanv

1:47 PM · Jul 4, 2023 · 190K Views
40 Replies · 423 Reposts · 3.33K Likes

This is the question I always open with. I typically already have a sense of the answer beforehand via our DMs or emails, but I ask again so that I can witness them talking through it, because there’s always valuable meta-information in how they answer.

Occasionally– sometimes because of the preparation they had done for the call– the client will explain things to me more compellingly than they’ve articulated anywhere in their work, which is a huge win right off the bat.

More often, they will stammer and flounder a bit, which is also good because that’s an opportunity for us to sharpen their thinking and their communications, which benefits every other aspect of their work.

And sometimes there will be a deep sigh, which usually means we need to do some foundational re-organization. This is a larger challenge, but I like it because it can sometimes be far more fruitful that the client expects.

2. “What specific outcomes are you looking for? Why? What have you tried so far, what are you thinking of trying next?”

6:33 PM · Jun 9, 2021
18 Replies · 133 Reposts · 1.55K Likes

Specificity is the goal. Sometimes clients get tremendous value simply from being persistently challenged to articulate what they actually want. The conversation dispels fog, reveals new pathways, and occasionally even reshapes the whole endeavor.

Here’s a fictional worst-case run– every real client has given me better answers than this, but I can still work with this:

“I want things to be better.”
“Better how?”
“More customers.”
“From where?”
“Anywhere, really.”
“Anywhere is nowhere. Where do you think your next customers are most likely hanging out?”
“Maybe [place]?”
“What are you currently doing to engage them there?”
“Not much.”
“What do you think they would be engaged by?”
“Well, I suppose…”

When a line of conversation goes dead like this, it usually means there’s a wrong assumption upstream, ie the client doesn’t actually want to work on the problem they said they wanted to work on. Catching this can be worth more than everything else combined, sometimes saving a client years of time they would have otherwise wasted. Someone once paid me twice my fee because they were so relieved to realize this!

If the outcomes are clear and a strategy exists, we look at execution. What’s working? What isn’t? What to fine-tune? Sometimes there’s a perfectly good strategy that the client simply isn’t following through on. That’s worth troubleshooting too, and it’ll involve some of the later questions.

3. “How would you define the expedition you’re on? How can you make people feel good about supporting your expedition?”

Whatever it is that you’re currently working on, you’re asking for people’s time, money, attention. How can you make them feel good about giving it to you? Approach this with confidence. Oftentimes the best answer is shaped like an inquiry: the point of an expedition is to Find Out. So what are you looking to discover?

The useful thing about this framing is that it nudges the client towards conceptualizing their work as a gift, as something that is of value to other people.

Perhaps most importantly, it gets them to see themselves as a professional who is in charge of their own operation, rather than a meek craftsperson whose fate is determined by chance and the whims of others.

Few people really think this through, so it’s worth reiterating: it is lucrative to know precisely what makes your audience happy to support you. Most people have only a vague sense of this, so they’re left hoping someone somewhere will be generous for no particular reason. Once you understand how and why people like your work, you can make it much easier for them to act on that.

The best outcome of this exercise is that the client themselves gets more motivated and energized to go on the expedition that they’ve just defined more clearly.

4. “How would you describe your current ‘brand’? How do your peers describe you to other people? What do you like and what do you not like about this description?”

Dolly Parton famously said to find out who you are and do it on purpose. It’s staggeringly good advice if you really follow through on the implications.

People are seldom very rigorous about this. You cannot improve your brand if you don’t really know what it is. If the word ‘brand’ isn’t right for you, we could use other words. Your positioning, your persona, your reputation, the way you are perceived both by others and yourself. These days you can get a useful read on this just by asking an AI what impression your work gives off.

Also, importantly, you don’t need to overfit yourself into whatever archetype you’re most similar to. It could be “X meets Y”, or “X, but also Y, and sometimes Z.”

A concrete exercise that pays off for almost anyone who tries it: Collect instances of how people describe you or your work to others, and analyze the patterns. If there’s no clear pattern, it usually means one of two things: you’re not yet distinctive enough to stick, or you’re too scattered to be made sense of. Both are solvable.

With the more reticent clients, I’ve often gotten breakthroughs by getting them to consider that everybody, or “other people” (wink wink) begin with stereotypes and assumptions before they get into the details. Alien (1979) was conceptualized as “Jaws (1975) in space”. Sylvester Stallone was always getting typecast as a thug, and rather than dispute that, he parlayed that with a “yes, and...” to “a sensitive thug with a soft touch”, which is at the heart of what made Rocky (1976) one of the greatest movies of all time.

Everything is a remix, and even if the creator doesn’t see it that way, the audience will still interpret her work through the lens of what they’ve seen before. Being sensitive to this can make all the difference between being appreciated and being ignored.

5. “What constraints are you operating under? Which of these are actually real, and which can we dismantle or bypass?”

@visakanv

7:01 PM · Apr 2, 2019
18 Replies · 160 Reposts · 1.06K Likes

Everybody is constrained, and so my clients are, too. And if you’re trying to do anything substantial at the edge of your ability, your constraints are going to reveal themselves. Some constraints are hard ones, like chronic health conditions. But some constraints can be conceptual, or even entirely imagined. It’s very worthwhile to do an audit of your constraints, because you’ll always find that you have more room to maneuver than you assumed.

A common constraint I’ve encountered is the fear of posting cringe– which is totally understandable and even good, since it protects the work from certain kinds of failure. But people often overcorrect on this and become stiff and lifeless. Here it helps to have a precise conversation about what makes a work lively or not, compelling to whom, cringe to whom, etc. This reveals the fear to be more specific and more manageable than it seems.

A related and rampant constraint I encounter is what I’ll call “the ghost of TED”. People seem to have internalized the assumption that you’re supposed to present your work like a polished TED talk: a neat origin story, carefully choreographed beats, a tidy conclusion. But that’s just one way to communicate. It’s not the only way, and for many people it’s not the right way. The ghost can be dispelled.

An important thing to understand here is usually that polished keynotes and performances are “late stage” outcomes, and not how things start out. So people tend to mistakenly compare themselves against the others at dfiferent stage of development, and worse, they often try to emulate the later stage, which distracts them from doing what would actually get them to the next stage.

Most clients already have better material than they’re making visible. When I look through websites and blogs, I typically find a lot of unnecessary detail up front and the most interesting stuff buried or scattered. As David Ogilvy said, when you’ve written your headline, you’ve spent 80 cents of your dollar.

The same applies to stories. Most people have anecdotes they tell in person that land really well, and still haven’t gotten around to writing up or recording. Sometimes getting that one story into a shareable format is worth more than everything else we discuss.

The Ghost of TED shows up here too: people keep assuming that packaging a story means producing something formal. It usually doesn’t. Some of the most effective versions of this are informal chats, a friend’s podcast, an impromptu talk recorded on a phone, even in someone’s car. If we’re talking about writing, it doesn’t necessarily need to be a crisp landing page. Ray Dalio’s book Principles was previously a PDF that itself looked like it grew out of a bunch of emails and notes.

Assemble good things and make them accessible, and don’t worry too much about the polish layer on top until you know it works. Look again to the Apple I.

7. “What on your todo list are you afraid to do?”

It’s not even that you ever have to do them, but examining it can be very revealing about yourself and the situation you’re in, or think you’re in.

Sometimes the scary thing is high-value and de-risking it is straightforward: preparation, a sanity check from someone you trust, a smaller first version. Inspiration tends to arrive in grandiose forms, rudely indifferent to your constraints– but you don’t have to manifest the final form on day one. Lady Gaga played parking lots before she did the Super Bowl.

Sometimes, though, even after you’ve right-sized the task, it’s still just scary. For some people the parking lot isn’t meaningfully less terrifying than the stadium. That’s a different problem, and it connects to the question of core motivations, which we’ll get to in #9.

8. “What are you conflicted about?”

Being conflicted is normal, even healthy. The problem is that performance pressure– to ship the next post, close the next sale, push the next update– creates an incentive to suppress conflict rather than examine it. This works until it doesn’t. Suppressed conflict becomes internal friction, and enough internal friction becomes mysterious stuckness.

If you’ve been head-down long enough, you may not even perceive the conflict anymore. Which is precisely when it’s worth stepping back and articulating it properly. Sometimes doing this reveals a path through. Sometimes it reveals that certain tasks you’ve been grinding on are actually irrelevant to what you care about. Either way the result is more clarity, and sometimes I’ve seen this lead to products becoming more honest and opinionated, which tend to resonate more with the people they’re for.

Everyone is conflicted, and it’s almost always better to name the conflict than to pretend it doesn’t exist.

9. “What do you love about your work? What do you really enjoy? When are you most in flow?”

This is especially important to talk about if the client is coming to the session with a weary energy, but it’s always relevant even if they aren’t. Because it’s difficult to artificially manufacture enthusiasm for doing something difficult. And we can usually safely assume that whatever it is that’s blocking them from reaching their goals is something difficult for them to overcome, otherwise they’d already have done it!

It’s not sustainable to expect to do something that you don’t want to do, or don’t like to do. Le Guin said that love doesn’t sit there like a stone, it has to be remade like bread. And it is the love you have for your work that will keep you going when things inevitably get tough. All happily married couples know that they have to spend real quality time together. And all marriage therapists will ask questions like, “What made you fall in love with each other?” Because there has to be something good worth fighting for. And this applies to everything. What are you fighting for, and why?

It’s strangely rare that anybody is already spending most of their time in the zone where they’re enjoying themselves the most. Almost half my clients will admit to something like, “I’m working on A, B and C. I love doing C, it makes me come alive.” And I ask, so C is the first thing you do each day, right? And they go, “Oh, no, I don’t really... it happens here and there sometimes.” I ask, “Is that because A and B are critically important and need to get done first?” And they go, “Oh, none of these things are more important than the other, I just kinda respond to whatever comes up.”

So, there’s an obvious opportunity there for them to to enjoy their work more, which has all sorts of benefits. Anything that produces good energy is good for morale, and colleagues and customers and investors pick up on that. We all want to be part of something wonderful.

10. “What are your aesthetic inspirations?”

3 Replies · 7 Reposts · 55 Likes

What marketing has appealed to you as a consumer? What are your favorite movies, musicians, advertisements, products? I ask these in part because I enjoy hearing the answers, but also if we have any shared references, that can let us go much deeper much quicker. They also reveal a lot about what someone actually responds to, which is useful information for figuring out what they should be making.

A more expansive version of the same question: if you were a media mogul, what would you produce or invest in? Suppose you’re a record label, a publishing house, a film studio. What’s in your catalog? What artists are you signing?

People tend to answer this one freely because it feels hypothetical, but the answers are usually quite revealing. There’s often a clear sensibility in there that isn’t showing up anywhere in their actual work, and that gap is worth examining.

Aesthetic doesn’t necessarily have to mean pretty or fancy. You could prefer something that’s simple, austere and so on. The challenge is put together something that is coherent and compelling to the people that you want to engage.

11. “Who are some people you’d like to be mutuals/peers/collaborators with?”

Who would you be most excited to do some sort of collaboration with? Why exactly? Everybody typically has some sense of the kind of people they’d want to have in their lives.

Oftentimes people in fact are already acquaintances with people they admire, but feel like they don’t want to impose, or to risk any awkwardness. I think this is natural and even healthy, but it shouldn’t be that it stops you from trying. Rather, you can take it as a challenge to make a worthwhile effort.

You can think of any endeavor as a kind of group project– even if it’s just a solo operation, you’re still collaborating with your past and future selves. And the quality of peers you manage to involve in your operation, whether as cofounders, employees or customers, will have a very big impact on the quality of the project. So it is always very worthwhile to think about who’s the next person you’d want to get involved with, and what adjustments you might have to make to make them want to be involved.

12. “If you could hand off your entire operation to a clone of yourself, and trust that they will manage it skillfully and enthusiastically, what would you like to do next?”

After taking a vacation, resting, clearing your head – what do you think you’d do next?

A lot of my clients are struggling with the tension between stewarding something that has been successful, with trying something new that they’d like to be doing. They might be so burdened by the current thing that they haven’t even allowed themselves the space to articulate what the next thing might be.

But the very reason they were successful in the first place was because of the playful energy they had that they’re now suppressing. And this self-suppression ends up casting a dark cloud on everything else.

So sometimes, just having some sense of possibility of what they might want to do “eventually”, “when they’re done”, can be eye-opening, relieving.

(As I revisit this, it really is striking to me how much of this rhymes with what happens in so many marriages.)

13. “Having thought through all of these things together with me, what do you think is the most compelling thing to do next?”

This is roughly how I close my sessions. Sometimes over the course of the session it becomes very clear that there’s one very important thing to do, and we focus on that. Sometimes there might be multiple things, which is fine too.

All of this said, the most valuable thing I do for my clients isn’t anything I tell them or ask them. The most valuable thing I do is pay attention. I look at not just what they say but how they say it. Sometimes they’ll recite a spiel (about what they’re ‘supposed’ to do, for eg) but I can see that they don’t really care about what they’re saying. And sometimes there are moments where they light up, get excited, start gushing. Sometimes they’ll hesitate before saying something, and digging into the hesitation is where all the magic is.

Oftentimes I ask them to tell me their backstory, why they’re doing what they’re doing, how they’ve helped their customers– and then I reflect back to them what resonates with me, and the result is messaging that’s more earnest, lively, effective.
