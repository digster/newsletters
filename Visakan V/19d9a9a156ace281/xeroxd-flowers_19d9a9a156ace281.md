Sometime in 2024, when feeling like I had lost touch¹ with my own capabilities as a creative, I put together a messy post on visakanv.com/archives/ titled ‘ "My archivist impulse” makes it sound like a quirky little habit– like a tendency to absent-mindedly arrange objects neatly on one’s desk. But in my case this impulse percolates across the entire ecology of my being– you could say that my compulsion to save and record things reorganized my entire life from the inside out. Since I was about 13 years old, I started living my life with the intention of documenting it, and the living became almost secondary to the documenting. To get a little grandiose about it I might proclaim that this is what all authors do: we create a life for ourselves out of arranging and rearranging words. As Borges put it, “The work of a poet never ends. It has nothing to do with working hours. You are continuously receiving things from the external world. These must be transformed and eventually will be transformed. This revelation can appear anytime. A poet never rests. He’s always working even when he dreams.” Everything serves the process. The process is everything. I’ve had all kinds of feelings about this over the years. I’ve been thrilled about it, I’ve been depressed about it, I’ve been silly about it, I’ve been meh about it, I’ve been “ah well” about it, I’ve wanted to give it up, I’ve found myself crawling home to it. It’s messy and hard to summarize. To attempt to summarize it feels like a fool’s errand, and yet, as I said in may the boisterous laughter of our children sanctify the weary graves of our grandfathers may the life that springs forth, all-dancing, bring solace to our sour, stagnant souls may our polluted hearts find redemption, may we return at last to wholeness, to home. It’s so easy to fall into the trap of documenting for documenting’s sake. But when I step back and really take it all in, I have to remind myself that the documentarian impulse was, again, rooted in grief and remembrance. It was always meant to serve life. Not supplant it. There’s a lot more I have to say about this but I’m going in circles and I need to pick up my kid from school, so let’s move on for now. everything’s a copy of a copy of a copy…I once got nerdsniped into looking up everything I could find about xerox copiers. They’re an interesting and important part of media history, since it was the first time people could cheaply and easily make copies of anything near-instantly. Here are some quick details from a It took over 20 years of experimentation before Xerox released the 914, the ‘first easy-to-use photocopier’. It took 7 seconds to make a copy. It was the size of a desk, weighed ~650lbs/300kg, and some offices had to remove doors to install the giant machines. Xerox expected customers would make about 2,000 copies/month, but they easily made 10,000/month, and some as many as 100,000/month. Before the 914 machine, Americans made 20,000,000 copies a year. By 1966, Xerox had 700x’d it to 14 billion/year. “The button waiting to be pushed, the whir of action, the neat reproduction dropping into the tray—all this adds up to a heady experience, and the neophyte operator of a copier feels an impulse to copy all the papers in his pockets,” wrote John Brooks in a 1967 New Yorker article. Police would use the photocopier to capture a snapshot of a perp’s pockets when jailing him, to save themselves the trouble of describing the physical contents “For everyday people, replicating nonsense was the best part of the copier– an illicit thrill. Hiding behind the anonymity of a duplicated document, office workers began circulating off-color jokes and cartoons.” “Artists, too, flocked to the device, thrilled by the high-contrast, low-fi prints it produced—so unlike either photography or traditional printing. As they showed, photocopying had an aesthetic. “When I show it a hair curler it hands me back a space ship, and when I show it the inside of a straw hat it describes the eerie joys of a descent into a volcano,” said Pati Hill, an artist who became famous for using a photocopier.”
I was fascinated to learn about Pati Hill³, so I looked up her work… which led me to this stunning image: Why do I find it so compelling? I’ve surely seen thousands of photographs of flowers over the years, and I hardly ever stop to examine them or think about them much. Photographs tend to serve us the illusion that we’re directly looking at the thing being photographed. I suppose this is especially the case when we’re looking at photographs online– I imagine it would be quite a bit different if say, someone had a box full of printed photographs of flowers. Even so, I’d imagine that the typical flower in a photograph remains untouched. Pati Hill’s carnation, on the other hand, was clearly removed from its environment, which meant that it surely died an unnatural death, as part of the artistic process. Of course, all flowers die eventually, and yet there’s something slightly ominous about this, about killing it on purpose. The stark black-and-white of the image erases some of the subtlety of the flower. It feels cold and clinical, like an x-ray. And yet you can discern, you can infer some of the tenderness of the carnation, feel the contrast of its soft petals pressed against the harsh glass of the copier. Stare at it long enough and you glimpse eros and death. I’ve kept this image with me, in a twitter thread and in my mind, for almost 2 years now, and still i feel it calling to me, asking for something to be made of it, made with it. But first, a little more about Pati Hill: Pati: “Making copies is very near to speaking.”There’s a 1980 New Yorker article about her titled “ “Other artists, too, are currently working with copying machines, fascinated by a process that can take an utterly ordinary thing and dissociate it from the context in which we normally see it, thereby making it particular and sometimes magical again—an effect, it seems, of the shallow yet dramatic depth perception of photocopiers, which give the copied object the effect of floating in space.” Pati Hill sat next to Charles Eames on a flight from Paris to New York, and she showed him some of her work, and he formally introduced her to IBM, which gave her a grant and let her a machine for 2.5 years! Pati: “A copier works like a magnet, attracting or rejecting things. It separates things, gives them an edge. On the grays, it’s always making up its mind which way it will go, toward black or toward white. A photocopy seems to me much more truthful to detail than a photograph. On a copier, the actualities of something become quite marvelous. Everything is symbolic.” Pati: “Photocopiers enable every person in every office to be an artist-though I know that when I say that sort of thing it troubles I.B.M. and Xerox. They can visualize all the problems that will result from art and experiment—the burnups and paper jams, and copier repairmen submitting huge bills. I think it’s wonderful that there are copy places like laundromats, where artists can go and use the machines for not much money. Copiers bring artists and writers together. Copies are an international visual language, which talks to people in Los Angeles and people in Prague the same way. Making copies is very near to speaking.”

By this I mean… thousands and thousands of static snapshots of dynamic processes. How does that feel? It doesn’t feel simply good or bad. I find myself feeling… sheepish. I’m sorta grateful to my past self for assembling so many xerox’d flowers– it was meaningful in a way. He was trying to get me to see something. And yet, the way I went about that project was itself suboptimal, and the project itself is ultimately kind of futile, or misplaced. He was killing himself to get me to see something. If I could have done everything better in the most fundamental sense, I wouldn’t have needed any xerox’d flowers at all. What I really wanted, all along, was to live, and to be alive.
