One of my favorite examples of Singaporean art was from a tumblr (pls-revert.tumblr.com) that posted from 2010 to 2013. The author collected examples of singaporean bureaucratic english (she called it civilservant-speak) out of frustration. Initially it was just a catalogue of quotes and screenshots, but one day she posted a 1000+ word post titled ✱ What is art, and what does it mean to be an artist? I wrote about one of my preferred definitions in I think this definition is correct, but I don’t think it’s widely shared, especially by people who don’t think of themselves as artists or caring about art. My sense is that most people– especially the people I grew up around– see the word “artist” as something that has to be conferred upon someone who has attained “artistic accomplishments”, ie have their art verified as valuable by cultural gatekeepers and authorities. Talking about it this way already starts to feel dated, because the Internet and social media has removed the gates, allowing anyone to talk to anyone. This was really exciting for a while, in that it seemed like we were on the cusp of some kind of big revolution. But I think the past couple of decades have shown that, for a lot of people, the ‘legitimacy’ of the magazine cover has simply been replaced by the ‘legitimacy’ of viral numbers. ✱ The thing I’m noting about KPI+Synergy=Love is that it was hardly noticed. The people who liked it sure liked it a lot, but there weren’t very many of us. Also I’m noticing that the work feels like it was meant to happen at a particular moment in time– in 2011 it felt surprising and subversive, in 2026 it feels a little ham-fisted and unsubtle. I think that’s something kind of inevitable for any good art. My friends and I tried watching 2001: A Space Odyssey (1968) as teenagers and we found it difficult to get into, because we’ve seen so many parodies of it over the years. (I’m sure it’s possible if we locked in, but we were being silly kids.) Re: the decay of subversion, I feel similarly about the t-shirts that I made around the same time period, largely centered around the gimmick of taking Singlish colloquialisms, spelling them out in vaguely literary typefaces, and slapping them onto t-shirts in a stark simple way. At the time, it felt subversive because Singlish was still something we were supposed to be ashamed of. Today it’s something we’re mostly proud of, and consider a precious marker of our identity. And now Disney does what I was doing (but more sanitized, since they leave out the swear words), so it’s become mainstream.
✱ Growing up in Singapore in the 1990s, I very much got a strong message from the culture, from the adults around me, from everyone, really, that the arts were inferior to the sciences, and that you couldn’t make a living from it. This came all the way from the top– in 1968, Lee Kuan Yew said “poetry is a luxury we cannot afford”. It took me a while to properly contextualize this as a very particular post-war, newly-independent cultural anxiety. I remember being a little confused when learning about opera companies and dance troupes in Singapore the 1800s. How come our forefathers could afford the arts, when they could barely afford anything at all?

How do you do it in a way that is beneficial without being exploitative?
