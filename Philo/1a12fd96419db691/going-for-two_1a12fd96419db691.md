Last Thursday, the Dallas Cowboys fell to the Tampa Bay Buccaneers, 24-16, a football game in which the heavily favored Cowboys fell behind early and came up short after a late comeback attempt. What makes this game notable is the furious public reaction to the Cowboys’ sound¹ but counterintuitive endgame strategy, which echoes the furious public reaction to sound but counterintuitive public policy that we have discussed here previously. First, a little necessary background about American football. Each team’s offense tries to move the ball down the field into the opponent’s end zone, and the defense tries to stop them. If the offense successfully reaches the end zone, they get 6 points, plus the chance to make an easy extra point kick, which would give them a total of 7 points. If they get stopped close to the end zone, they may opt to attempt to kick a field goal from wherever they are, which is worth 3 points. Finally, if they get stopped far away or lose the ball, they score zero points. Then the other team’s offense gets the ball and tries to score. That’s all we need to understand about football: teams generally score in 7s and 3s, and they trade possessions. In 1994, the NFL began allowing teams to go for a two point conversion after a touchdown instead of kicking the extra point, emulating a popular option that had long existed at the college level. To earn two points, the offense gets one play to get the ball into the end zone from the two yard line; if they fail, they get zero points. Historically, teams convert two point conversions at about a 50% rate ( Teams rarely go for two early in the game, when doing so just adds a lot of variance for no extra expected return. For example, scoring two touchdowns and attempting two two-point conversions nets you 16 points a quarter of the time (two successes), 14 points half the time (a success and a failure and vice versa), and 12 points a quarter of the time (two failures). If you score only 12 points off of two touchdowns, and you ultimately lose by one point, you will look like a complete idiot, whereas no one will remember when you heroically got 16 points. However, as all finance nerds know, options are valuable! As the game nears the end and the range of possible point outcomes narrows, there will be situations where the upside from scoring 2 additional points is high while the downside of coming away with zero points (instead of one point) is low. Specifically, there is a discontinuity right where regulation ends in a tie: a 2 point loss is worth the same as a 1 point loss (that is, nothing), whereas a tie gets you to overtime, where you have a 50% chance of winning.³ That is exactly the scenario that the two point option is useful for. A trivial example would be a team that trails by 8 at the very end of the game and scores a touchdown, leaving it down two points. A kicked extra point is worthless because it leaves them with a one point loss. Going for two gets them to overtime half the time, and leaves them with a two point loss half the time. The expected value of this touchdown is still 7 points (half the time they get 6, and half the time they get 8), but the additional variance from going for two takes this team from a sure loss to a 25% chance of victory (since they need to convert the two and then win in overtime). Since teams almost always score in 3s and 7s (or 8s, if the strategy calls for it), you can work backwards to find other situations where going for two is valuable late in the game. Take a team that is losing by 11 and scores a touchdown with a few minutes left, leaving them down 5. They still need to score again to win, but a two point conversion here would get them within 3. It is much easier to score a field goal than a touchdown, so getting to a 3 point deficit gives them an option to kick a field goal for a tie later, whereas they will need a touchdown later regardless of if they are behind by 4 or 5. Similarly, a team that is up by one after scoring a touchdown late in the game will want to go for two, because it gives them a chance to get to a 3 point lead, just in case the opposing team is able to respond with a field goal. Otherwise, there is no difference between a one and two point lead (which would transform into a two or one point deficit if they give up a field goal). You can work through every possible point scenario in this manner to get an idea of when it might make sense to go for two late in a game. Assuming they actually managed to score two touchdowns, they would expect to win 50% of the time. According to ESPN, At some point, some bright analyst realized that if you have to score two touchdowns, you have two sequential two-point options to work with. You can combine these to your advantage, as long as you start by going for two after your first touchdown:⁴ If you go for two on your first TD and make it (50% odds), you should choose to kick on your second TD (100% odds) and score 15 total points, for a one point regulation victory. This will be the outcome (50% x 100%) = 50% of the time. If you fail on your first two point attempt, you have to go for two on the second TD, which will give you 14 total points and overtime if you succeed. This will be the outcome (50% fail the first time x 50% succeed the second time) = 25% of the time. Or you might fail both attempts and get only 12 total points and lose by two in regulation. This will be the outcome (50% fail the first time x 50% fail the second time) = 25% of the time.
Now you’ve shifted the outcomes so that you have a 62.5% chance of winning the game instead of 50% (again, assuming you score two TDs): 50% if you make the first conversion and win by one, plus 12.5% because you score your second conversion and get to overtime a quarter of the time, and once in overtime you win half the time. Note that you will still score 14 points in expectation; you’ve just shifted the distribution around so you get 15/14/12 points 50%/25%/25% of the time, and that translates to a 62.5% overall chance of winning instead of the 50% you get from scoring 14 points 100% of the time. Put another way, you are rigging it so you get exactly 3 points from 2 conversion attempts 50% of the time, which gives you a win in regulation by exactly one point, and the sacrifice you have to make make is that now you get zero points from 2 conversion attempts 25% of the time, which gives you a two point loss in regulation. The other 25% of the time, you get two points from two conversion attempts and go to overtime just as you did when you were kicking, just in a pattern of 0+2 instead of 1+1. Also note that this approach follows the exact same principle as the original trivial scenario where you are losing by 8, score a touchdown, and go for two. In that scenario you are trading a sure one point loss for a 50% chance of a tie and overtime and a 50% chance of a two point loss. The only thing that makes the new scenario unfamiliar is the combination of two conversion attempts; you can see that the underlying intuition and logic is still the same, in that you are simply strategically exercising your options to squeeze out a higher overall chance of winning. Some critics avoid this trap and claim that they are more capable of seeing the pitfalls of this approach as executed by humans on the field, as opposed to numbers in a spreadsheet. The idea is that kicking the extra point is psychologically valuable and keeps the momentum going, whereas a missed two pointer stops the momentum and makes it harder to score again later. The problem here is that 80% of the win probability in this strategy comes in the scenario where you make the first two point attempt, setting yourself up for a regulation win instead of overtime. If this is the case with something as simple and trivial as going for two down 8, one can begin to understand how populist politicians self-destruct with more complex high-stakes topics like tariffs or price controls or housing restrictions.¹⁰ Criticisms of going for two down 8 will likely continue to persist for at least a few years because the teams that try it will usually lose, just as the Cowboys did. They won’t lose because it’s a bad strategy, they will lose because teams that are down by a touchdown late in a game and about to give the ball back are in a really bad position and no strategy can do much to help them. For example, if you are down by 8 points late in a game after a touchdown and you kick the extra point, you still need 3 independent events to all happen: You need to stop the other team from scoring, which happens on about 67% of drives in the NFL; You then need to score a touchdown yourself, which happens on 22% of NFL drives; You need to win in overtime, which is a 50% proposition.
This is an unscientific approach, but multiply 67% x 22% x 50% and you get a 7.4% chance of winning. Going for two down 8 lets you replace 50% with 62.5%, which only raises your odds to 9.2%.
