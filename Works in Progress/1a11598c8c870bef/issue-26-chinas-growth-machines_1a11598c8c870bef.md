FeaturesIn the 1980s, Geely was a refrigerator parts manufacturer in the provincial city of Hangzhou, China. Today it is an auto giant that owns Volvo, Lotus, Polestar and part of Aston Martin. Look almost anywhere in China and you’ll see similar stories of transformation and explosive growth. The world’s largest suppliers of electric vehicles, LiDAR, display panels, and batteries are all young Chinese companies. Many attribute China’s success to wise and foresighted winner picking from the Chinese Communist Party – its willingness to be strategic in a world otherwise governed by the ruthless pursuit of the bottom line. But really, write Robin Rivaton and Ben Southwood, China’s secret ingredient is that it takes many of the things that make America so successful to extreme, exaggerated degrees: cheap capital, a huge market, and intense competition at every level. China’s mayors and regional leaders effectively work as venture capitalists, investing to bring about growth in their areas, and creating powerful ‘local growth machines’ that make the country rich. Everyone loves old cities like Bruges, Prague, and Avignon. But this isn’t just because they have beautiful historic buildings: the layouts of medieval city centers make them feel cosy, human, and organic. The Austrian architect Camillo Sitte theorized in 1902 that the recipe for lovable cities like these involved small, enclosed squares, curved streets, public art sitting on the edge of plazas, not in the middle, and asymmetry. Above all, cities were the voids between buildings, rather than the buildings themselves, which should join together into a warm, hugging wall. His work was hugely influential in its own time, but has since been forgotten – an oversight that Phoebe Arslanagic-Little and Samuel Hughes try to correct in their article explaining Sitte’s principles. There’s nothing quite like porcelain, writes Charles C Mann. Invented three thousand years ago by Chinese potters, it is produced from a special kind of clay, kaolin, that essentially produces glass when heated, creating the material’s distinctive, translucent delicacy. It later became an object of obsession for European royalty and alchemists who experimented for centuries to discover its secret. Today, innovation in porcelain and related materials is producing high-tech kitchen equipment and bone replacements. Porcelain is a reminder that the drive to create new types of matter has long played a powerful role in human history. Successful people get up early, work long hours, never drink or smoke, and submit themselves regularly to high-intensity interval training, meditations, and ice baths. Right? Well, the Victorians didn’t think so, writes Samuel Hughes. The upper class of nineteenth-century England learned relatively little at school or university, and instead spent much of their time playing rugby and hazing each other. As adults they often started their days late after long nights of feasting and drinking. You might be skeptical that this was a recipe for success, but it created a powerfully bonded elite with very high levels of internal trust, which oversaw the largest empire the world has ever seen. Mosquito-borne disease is, in theory, a solved problem. In the last decade biologists have come up with several techniques that can either make mosquito populations immune to diseases like dengue and yellow fever or suppress their populations by making them sterile. These techniques are effective and, because their effects are so precise, have minimal effects on the environment (unlike the insecticides traditionally used).
