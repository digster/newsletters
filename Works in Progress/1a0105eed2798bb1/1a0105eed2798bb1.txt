In April, I gave a TED talk on the main stage in Vancouver, and it was released online today. You can watch it here [ https://substack.com/redirect/efcfd507-66c0-4f9c-88c2-57ff5f8fc3e7?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ].
I’ve also written a longer companion article for Works in Progress, which was published last week – you can read it here [ https://substack.com/redirect/4346fe32-d3e5-4ec0-b337-e65f28adfca2?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ].
I think we’re at a turning point when it comes to medical innovation. Our tools are improving rapidly, but our ambitions to use them have fallen behind. Science funding has slowed down [ https://substack.com/redirect/c87f1606-ff66-4658-984e-51beae17bbba?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ], aid budgets have been slashed, and immigration restrictions mean we’re driving away expertise. Even when things have come back online, the disruption and loss of expertise will make it hard to return to our previous trajectory. We could be doing so much more. Medical innovation might not seem urgent in the abstract, but most of us know someone whose life it could change, and eventually I think it affects us all. We can change that.
That’s part of the motivation for my writing on global health and medical innovation at Works in Progress and my work on clinical trial reform at the Abundance and Growth Fund [ https://substack.com/redirect/0c1607bf-2745-4b36-9d75-fde571329daa?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ], but the talk is also larger than that: it’s a call to arms for more people to work on medical innovation, and explains why you don’t have to be a scientist to do it.
I hope you enjoy it!
