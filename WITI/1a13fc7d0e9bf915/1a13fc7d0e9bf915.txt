In 1916, a Unitarian minister and American essayist named Samuel McChord Crothers coined a word for something humans had been doing for centuries: bibliotherapy, or treating what ails us with books. His essay in The Atlantic Monthly, “A Literary Clinic,” was a clever, semi-fictional account of a man who treats people’s troubles by prescribing poetry and prose.
The First World War turned out to be a real-world laboratory for the idea. When the American Library Association established its Library War Service, librarians wheeled carts of books into military hospitals and worked directly with wounded and convalescing soldiers. Reading offered distraction, companionship, and a temporary escape from the horrors of war. Books were considered part of the recovery process, not merely a way to pass the time.
In Britain, literary scholar H. F. Brett-Smith reportedly devised a “fever chart” of therapeutic reading, recommending different books for different psychological states. Among his prescriptions for soldiers suffering from shell shock: Jane Austen. Just imagine returning from the trenches and being handed Pride and Prejudice. Not a self-help book explaining trauma, but a story about the ordinary complications of being human. (Side note: Rudyard Kipling later wrote “The Janeites,” about a secret fraternity of WWI soldiers bonded by their enthusiasm for Austen. We can only speculate whether he was influenced by Brett-Smith’s fever chart.)
All of this thinking stemmed from the idea that literature can offer something beyond information or instruction: distance from suffering, a temporary home in another person’s consciousness, or language for emotions that were otherwise difficult to express.
Why is this interesting?
We live in an era when we diagnose ourselves and each other constantly, and we’ve developed an enormous infrastructure for explaining our problems. We listen to podcasts about attachment styles, read books explaining grief and anxiety, and turn to message boards to compare symptoms. We’ve become very good at prescribing information, but information doesn’t always provide consolation. Knowing more about grief, for example, isn’t necessarily the same as feeling less alone in it.
Literature offers another way in. Rather than telling us how to solve a problem, fiction and poetry help us experience it differently. Contemporary practitioners are exploring how literary forms themselves might be useful: short stories may offer a journey into someone else’s consciousness; crime fiction can provide absorbing distraction; elegies give language to the complicated emotions of grief and loss.
And people are still putting this idea into practice. Britain’s The Reader brings shared reading into hospitals, mental-health settings, and community spaces, where people gather to read stories and poetry aloud. Participants may listen without pressure to speak. The goal isn’t to master a text or learn a lesson. It’s to see what the literature brings up, and what happens when people experience it together.
We instinctively know there’s something comforting about literature. We practice our own form of bibliotherapy when we give a copy of Mary Oliver’s American Primitive to a grieving friend, or return to The Secret Garden when life feels bleak. 
We don’t expect these books to fix anything. But they might offer company, perspective, beauty, or a few hours of relief. A century after Brett-Smith drew up his fever chart, perhaps we’re due for a new prescription: fewer hours searching for answers online, more hours in the company of a good writer. (JG)
