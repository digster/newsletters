Claire Stapleton is a writer and former tech exec. She just published a memoir, Don’t be Evil [ https://substack.com/redirect/ec02c375-311a-41bc-9eda-70a95bc41a40?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ]. Her Substack is Tech Support [ https://substack.com/redirect/5b62309d-415e-4f5a-8243-dfe6ef08f20c?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ]. Happy to have her with us this week. -Colin (CJN) 
Tell us about yourself.
I’m Claire. I worked at Google for a long time, in various writing, comms, and marketing roles, before inadvertently becoming a labor organizer when I, along with a bunch of other women, organized the Google Walkout in a moment of collective rage. This abruptly thrust me into the public sphere as a “tech worker organizer” and critic of Google and Big Tech’s ills writ large—which, naturally, proved difficult to reconcile with my day job as an enthusiastic paid corporate shill. The contradiction didn’t hold for long.
I emerged from my 12-year-long Google bubble/career in 2019, 34 years old, pregnant with baby #2, with zero steer on how to answer the question “now what?” In a sense, I’ve been answering that ever since, with the two most obvious outputs being 1/ Tech Support, my Substack, wherein I help other people grapple with existentially tinged “now what?” questions, and 2/ my memoir, Don’t Be Evil [ https://substack.com/redirect/ec02c375-311a-41bc-9eda-70a95bc41a40?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ], which was such a massive “life’s work” endeavor that I’m now asking the “now what?” question afresh. I live in Brooklyn with my husband and two sons.
Describe your media diet.
Unhinged. If there are allegations that I’m not a serious person, I’m not beating them here today. I almost never proactively visit major news websites, for better or worse (probably definitely worse). Everything comes to me via feed, group chat, meme account, Reddit, or osmosis, and yet I feel like I’m not missing much in the “discourse.” These past couple of months, putting my book out into the world has given me a bit more professional justification for being so hooked into my phone, but I’m feeling like it’s time to dust off the Brick, reinstate the screen-time limits, and stop donating my brain to Big Tech’s brain-rot experiment.
My afternoon treat, though, is YouTube. I’m drawn to non-famous people who vlog, because the delusion that we’re all the stars of our own TV shows is laid bare there (or maybe not such a delusion–plenty of them now have mansions and a massive spigot of brand deals).. I love these tiny glimpses into worlds I’d never otherwise see: moms with 8+ kids (there are a lot of these), a woman who runs a foster care house, an Orthodox Jewish woman showing life in a neighboring part of Brooklyn, a doctor on 24-hour shifts, and quite a few Mormons. 
I open X once a day, mostly to see how the MAGA factions are clashing and turning on each other or strenuously, openly arguing that immigrants, women’s autonomy, and empathy are the main things accelerating the collapse of civilization (the recent “Indian Question” debate was huge for me).
I don’t listen to podcasts very often, since I can’t find a natural place for them in my urban lifestyle. But I am spiritually devoted to Poog, Doomscroll, Celebrity Book Club with Steven & Lily, The Dig Radio, and TrueAnon, the only one I think I’ve ever paid for. Liz Franczak makes me feel strangely safe in a world where everything is connected and none of the connections are good.
What’s the last great book you read?
If I’m being honest, and why not, it’s Buford the Little Bighorn, a picture book by the wonderful author-illustrator of yesteryear Bill Peet. I read it to my six-year-old the other day and was laughing and clapping like a seal. Just a delight.
Because I’m not a total philistine/child, I’ll give an adult answer too: I reread Graham Greene’s The Quiet American while writing my book and was stunned by how relevant it felt. It’s about the very human desire to be part of something larger than ourselves, and the danger of attaching that desire to power. Pyle believes so completely in his own good intentions that he can’t see the harm he’s doing in their name. Obviously, I was thinking about Silicon Valley, and my own years of trusting in Google’s grand mission. But also about what happens when our faith in institutions begins to collapse while our longing for purpose and belonging remains intact. We still want to be part of something bigger. The question is who gets to put that impulse to work, and toward what ends.
What are you reading now?
I’m currently alternating between Meghan O’Gieblyn’s Will and Attention and Maggie O’Farrell’s Land. I read five to ten pages a night before falling asleep, and some nights I have to reread the previous night’s bit. A tidal approach to reading. Both books are gorgeous and lend themselves to it.
What’s your reading strategy when you pick up a print copy of your favorite publication?
We recently let our long-running weekend New York Times delivery lapse, not out of some principled media stance, though that would be cool; we just forgot. So now The New Yorker is the last Print Pub standing in our house (besides alumni magazines and Sports Illustrated Kids).
My strategy is to study the table of contents and contributors intensely, as if I’m about to embark on a rigorous quest of intellectual self-improvement. Then skim Tables for Two, Talk of the Town, and Shouts & Murmurs, maybe pick one whole essay to edify myself, read the first page of the fiction, and call it a day.
Most recently I read and very much enjoyed Gideon Lewis-Kraus’s essay on AI. One insight I loved was that AI has inadvertently demonstrated that another world is materially possible: it hasn’t cured cancer, but it has shown us what happens when you concentrate an almost unimaginable amount of capital, engineering talent, institutional commitment, and political will on a problem. Which raises the obvious question: why do we accept that this kind of mobilization is possible for AI infrastructure but impossible for so many things society broadly wants and needs?
(See, my commitment to edifying myself with one New Yorker essay per week is working.)
Who should everyone be reading that they’re not?
Clare Frances. I love her Substack, Famous and Beloved. I am genuinely interested in stuff like “Harry Styles sighting NYC” and “is Gracie Abrams cool or not,” and strange and stupid Internet drama. but I also want those interests lifted into broader conversation about the world: how fame is trafficked, how taste circulates, how we’re all in the business of making ourselves interesting and marketable. Clare squares this circle for me: I get celebrity gossip, sharp cultural criticism, and an intellectual justification for spending so much of my life online in the first place.
Also Leigh Claire La Berge, whose book Fake Work I loved for its articulation of how contemporary work has become an elaborate performance of productivity whose actual social value is increasingly difficult to discern (tracks with my last couple years at Google!). I just read her recent n+1 essay, “Michel and Mom,” about sifting through her mother’s journals as her mother develops dementia. It’s intellectually rich, tender, haunting. 
I gravitate toward people with Cla(i)re in their names, clearly. (Clairely? Ugh.)
What is the best non-famous app you love on your phone?
TrainingPeaks. I’m so ADHD that when I was writing my book, my developmental editor—shoutout Nancy Rawlinson, you’re my queen—became concerned enough about the chaos of my output that she had me account for my time in 30-minute increments in a personal training app. It worked, and now whenever TrainingPeaks sends me a marketing email now about training for a marathon or whatever, I reflect on the physical and emotional endurance challenge of writing a book, how far I’ve come, and how profoundly out of shape I currently feel to write another one.
Plane or train?
The A train in NYC is the central artery of my life. If something’s not on the A, I’m loath to go.
The plane, though, carries an exciting sense of possibility. Between Covid and having little kids, I haven’t traveled that much in the past decade. My older son has a growing list of places he wants to see, assembled from his own media diet and the world outside our house: Rome, Japan, Mexico. Now that my kids are a bit older and suddenly more worldly, the plane has started to seem exciting again: a route to opening the world up.
What is one place everyone should visit?
I grew up in Northern California, but my parents live in Santa Barbara now, and I remain constantly stunned by the beauty and bounty of California and, especially, how casually accessible so much of it is. Case in point: Loon Point in Summerland. You walk down beneath these huge, dramatic cliffs to a beautiful stretch of beach. There are horses around (my kids and I have taken to calling it “Horse Poop Beach,” for reasons that are obvious but only add to the charm), and the Amtrak Pacific Surfliner goes by above you. It’s somehow spectacular and completely commonplace in this part of the world. Wonder!
Tell us the story of a rabbit hole you fell deep into.
I fell deep into Subliminal Jihad’s episodes about the Grateful Dead, which connected some bizarre dots for me personally. My dad grew up in Atherton, California, and knew Lindsey Buckingham and some of the Dead, and I’d always wondered about the relationship between the very establishment Stanford/Palo Alto world and the counterculture flourishing right alongside it.
The podcast offers a darker, more conspiratorial answer, tracing the Dead’s proximity to CIA/MK-Ultra LSD research, Ken Kesey, and the Acid Tests. The provocative thesis: what if the psychedelic counterculture wasn’t so much a rebellion against power as a way of redirecting antiwar political energy into LSD, personal liberation, and extremely long jams?
And once you entertain that possibility, you start wondering what else that feels spontaneous, organic, even liberatory might actually be serving interests you can’t see. You don’t have to buy every conspiracy theory to become a little less confident in the official story of how culture happens. This rabbit hole’s deep, man….
