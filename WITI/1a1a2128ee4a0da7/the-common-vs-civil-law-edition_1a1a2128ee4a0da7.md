Steve Bryant (SB) is a Steve here. I was trying to open a bank account in Mexico City the other day when the executive ripped up my signature right in front of me. She tossed the paper into her trash can, then slid another copy of the same form across the desk. “I’m sorry, can you please sign again.” I narrowed my eyes, signed again, then slid the paper across her desk. This time she audibly sighed. “Your signature needs to be the same signature as your passport,” she said. I remembered the exact moment I’d signed that passport. It was around 10am in a Duane Reade on Flatbush Avenue. I was hungover. I had to sign on one of those tiny little electric signature pads. I explained this to her. “There’s no way I can duplicate that signature,” I said. “It was nine years ago.” The executive slid another form across the desk. “Can you please try again.” Why is this interesting? Completing paperwork in Mexico, where I live, is harder than nailing Jell-O to a tree. This doesn’t just apply to opening a bank account, but really anything that requires proving your identity: getting married, buying a house, registering a business. The process is so trying that the only way to survive it, for some, is to document their frustration in a newsletter. Why, you might ask, is this the case? The answer has little to do with the all too predictable lament of dismissive foreigners—Beautiful country, but so disorganized!— and everything to do with the legal tradition of civil law. If you’re a citizen of the UK (except Scotland), Canada (except Québec), or the United States (except Louisiana), this won’t be a tradition you’re accustomed to. In those countries, you live under common law. Common law runs on judicial precedent, and courts work by But if you’re a citizen of Continental Europe, any country in Latin America, or Japan, Korea, or Taiwan, say, you’re living under civil law. Civil law doesn’t run on precedent, it runs on codes: comprehensive statutes that try to anticipate every situation in advance. Judges apply the code, rather than construct doctrine out of accumulated decisions. The tradition is Roman, and was carried into medieval Europe through the universities, before being codified by Napoleon and exported to the Americas by Spain, which brought smallpox, Catholicism, and Roman law, in roughly that order of immediate impact. For the lowly petitioner trying to open a bank account, the big difference between the two systems comes down to this: civil law codes have to do all the work up front. The prior decisions don’t matter, so the system develops an obsession with proof. The fact of your identity must be proven at the point of any regulated transaction, regardless of how many regulated transactions you’ve made before. Common law citizens are accustomed to exactly the opposite. You show your ID, sign a form, and the transaction closes. The system doesn’t stop you from lying, it punishes you afterwards. One charming consequence is that in the United States, anybody can be you. Are we not an inventive people, up to and including identity theft? The other thing is this: common law countries tend to have privatized verification, e.g., title insurance, credit bureaus, and escrow companies. In the U.S., we outsourced the job of trust to private firms that prefer to put a price on the risk that you’re going to lie, rather than certify the truth of your identity. Mexico, instead, kept all that inside the state. All that is to say that nobody’s going to steal my identity here down below the Rio Grande. Which is good!
