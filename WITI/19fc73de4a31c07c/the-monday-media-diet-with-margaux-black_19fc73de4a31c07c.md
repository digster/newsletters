Margaux Black is a London-based creative and strategist. She writes the
Tell us about yourself. Originally from New Zealand, I’m currently based in London. By day, I’m a creative and digital strategist—a gig I’ve loved navigating for the last 13 years. A massive chunk of my career was built on brand strategy, but lately, I’ve shifted my focus deeply into user experience, specifically rooted in the concept of design ethics or human behavior study. By night (and on weekends), I’ve lived a bit of a parallel culinary life. I once moonlighted as a restaurant critic in Auckland, and for the last few years, I’ve been hosting private supper clubs out of my home. I’ve always been what standard folk call a “foodie,” though I personally think of it more as a religion than a title. I’ve also always been a bit of a writer and reader, but I lost my way with it as my corporate career ramped up. Eventually, combining my strategic brain, my love for writing, and my obsession with food culture felt like the inevitable next step. It turns out that no matter what the topic is, I always seem to be able to find a link back to hospitality or food culture in some way leading me to believe that this might actually be the meaning of life (I used to think it was 42). Enter The Set List. I started my Substack as a funnel for my opinions and a place to rescue the essays gathering dust in the depths of my Google Drive. It centers on hospitality, lifestyle, and food culture, mostly using satire, or at least a heavy sprinkle of it. As a strategist, I know that making people laugh or smirk makes ideas sticky; as a human, I just think it’s a hell of an achievement. When I’m not overthinking culture, you can find me cooking, traveling, playing music, doing yoga, or having erratic, highly philosophical conversations with friends and strangers. Describe your media diet. Lately, it’s been a heavy dose of Substack, supplemented by Instagram for pop-culture en masse. For London restaurant news, Conde Nast and The Nudge are staples, even if keeping up with this city’s opening schedule is an impossible task (my google maps would stress anyone out). My podcast rotation is a non-negotiable. I religiously follow The Curve, two fellow Kiwi women breaking down finance, and Well by Ariel Lorrie is my ultimate wellness-pod guilty pleasure. Sentimental Garbage, BBC Food productions and No Such Thing as a Fish get honorable mentions. I’m currently trying to revive my print magazine habit. While traveling in Japan, I picked up several issues of Magazine B, which profiles specific brand identities; I currently have their issues on Aesop, Acne Studios, and Staub on my coffee table. Otherwise, my ever growing book pile usually wins the battle for my attention span. What’s the last great book you read? Evenings and Weekends by Oisín McKenna. It’s set in London, tracking a messy, interconnected group of people across the exact neighborhoods I frequent. It feels incredibly indicative of modern London youth culture; or at least, what I perceive it to be, no longer being a “youth” myself. It’s a wildly addictive read that manages to provoke anger and deep sympathy all at once. What are you reading now? Until August by Gabriel García Márquez. It’s his “lost novel,” written while he was suffering from dementia and deemed by the author himself as unfit for publication. Having read it, I’m incredibly grateful his family disagreed. He had a singular ability to marry a rigorous journalistic style with magical realism, creating deeply human scenes spiked with poetic wonder. This story follows a “happily” married woman who takes a new lover once every summer on the island where her mother is buried. It’s comical, gritty, matter-of-fact, and completely scratches a literary itch. What’s your reading strategy when you pick up a print copy of your favorite publication? Because my favorite publications are equal parts visual and literary (I still mourn the discontinuation of Cereal magazine), I always start with a full visual skim. I’ll flick through the entire issue, absorbing the photography and layout first, before doubling back to the beginning to actually read the text in order of what grabbed my attention the most. This, I realise, is an erratic way to read magazines but it’s how I’ve always done it. Who should everyone be reading that they’re not? Financial culture. Hear me out. Subscribing to something like The Curve, or any publication that breaks down the economy without the jargon, is essential. It sounds incredibly dry, but finance is ultimately just a mirror for human behavior and sociology anyway. It’s worth paying attention to. Also, more fiction!!! I will die on this hill. The more widely you read across fiction, the better you actually understand culture, history, and people. Personally, I’d much rather be taught a lesson through a brilliant story than a list of raw facts. If you’re someone who claims they don’t like fiction, I simply think you haven’t found your gateway author yet. Keep trying. What is the best non-famous app you love on your phone? Artsy. I travel frequently and spend a lot of time in art galleries. I used to have thousands of random photos on my phone trying to catalog artists I liked, until I realized Artsy could do all that heavy lifting for me. It tracks artists, alerts me when pieces come up for auction or artists I follow are having exhibitions, and helps me plan for a future where I actually have the funds and wall space to house them. It’s an art lover’s essential. Plane or train? I’m going to be completely annoying and say both. As a general mode of transport, trains win hands down. If I can take a train from London to wherever I’m heading (Paris, Amsterdam, you name it) that will always be my choice. Even though the Eurostar costs a small fortune, it is just objectively better. However, I experience an overwhelming, almost miraculous sense of calm on aeroplanes that I never get on a train. Flying is probably one of the only times my brain truly switches off and I can get completely lost in a book, an album, or a film. It’s highly likely a combination of zero cell service and ambient brown noise, but regardless, I absolutely cherish that dead time. There is something beautifully liberating about being stuck in a metal tube where you literally can’t do anything else, leaving you with zero guilt about what you probably should be doing instead. What is one place everyone should visit? I really had to think about this because three places simultaneously popped into my head, but I’m going to lock in Mexico City. Art, history, culture, food, chaos, beauty—it’s all there in one place. I love Mexican music, and I think everyone on earth loves a taco (I actually wrote about the humble taco recently on The Set List). Agave-based drinks are also much more spiritual than the West’s treatment suggests, and it’s truly a city that has something for every kind of traveler. Personally, I learned just as much from the Anthropological Museum as I did from going to a Lucha Libre show at the Coliseo. Tell us the story of a rabbit hole you fell deep into. I’m still in this rabbit hole, arguably, but wine… I actually grew up in somewhat of a wine family in New Zealand. They had a string of retail stores and managed a whole lot of the wholesale and distribution for international and local brands. I always liked wine, but I got a little detached from it when I left New Zealand and started having to pay full retail price again. However, I did my WSET Level 2 at the start of this year. It covered a lot of elements I already knew, but it also completely reinvigorated my love of wine from a cultural and very place-based perspective. How the large grape varieties change based on the geography, soil, and climate of a place; how the makers and regional taste preferences can alter the end product; and how some regions have distinct grapes specifically unique to them. I am now actively searching for wines I haven’t tried before rather than just reaching for the same varieties, makers, and regions again and again. It’s a rabbit hole with endless burrows, so I may be here for quite some time. Equally, this rabbit hole led to me being able to properly articulate my deep dislike of natural wines (which you can read all about on Substack).
