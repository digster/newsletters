The Deadline Edition

On submit buttons, elections, and putting things off.

Guest Contributor
Nov 5
∙
Guest post
 
READ IN APP
 

Kelly Berold (KB) is based in Paris, where she writes and consults for luxury brands. (She’s also a longtime friend of WITI, and a true information omnivore.) She can be reached at kellyberold@gmail.com.

Kelly here. While most people’s eyes have been on America’s midterm election results, yesterday South Africans also voted in local government elections.

The race wasn’t much of an international story, but domestically, it has been utterly eclipsed by one of the more embarrassing political mishaps in recent memory. I can’t stop thinking about it with equal parts schadenfreude and all-too-knowing compassion.

Here’s the story. Every five years, South Africans vote for the municipal councils that run their cities, towns and districts, which in turn elect their mayors. The race is an early temperature check on national politics, and the first since the ruling African National Congress (ANC), the party of Nelson Mandela, lost the comfortable majority it had held for three decades, in 2024.

To be eligible, all parties had until 5pm on 28 August to submit their candidate lists to the Electoral Commission (the IEC) either in person or through an online portal. Two days out, the ANC was still finalising its online submissions, and at 4:55pm, their team was still uploading. When the deadline came and went, 181 public representative candidates across six municipalities, many of them in the party’s Eastern Cape heartland, were left idle in the system. Apparently, no one managed to press the final submit button.

Take a minute to imagine the party in power in your own country on the phone to a website help centre five minutes before a deadline that could bar their candidates from standing in their own strongholds. Then imagine how thoroughly it would be eviscerated by the public when the news broke. Which is obviously what happened. The ANC blamed a glitch. The IEC said the system was working just fine. The country’s Constitutional Court reversed the ruling two weeks before voting day, forcing the commission to print 6.6 million extra ballots.

Why is this interesting?

Beyond the potentially massive political setback had the ruling stood, or the question of whether UX design can carry the force of electoral law, I can’t think of a better public example of how badly we misjudge deadlines in which we ourselves have the most at stake.

The word “deadline” traces back to the Civil War era, specifically a Confederate prison camp in Georgia, where it marked a physical boundary that any prisoner could be shot for crossing. Printers then borrowed it to refer to the edge of the press bed, beyond which type wouldn’t print. By around 1920, newsrooms were using it as the last moment to file copy. Its meaning has continued to move from place to time, with considerably less morbid repercussions.

Nowadays, we’ve maintained the word, but we’ve crossed the line. Our deadline culture is both more elastic and more relentless, with bigger buffers, but also sometimes seemingly none at all, because now everything is typically asked for unequivocally and indiscriminately ASAP. Deadlines are increasingly arbitrary, over-allocated, and misclassified, yet missing one has become more a moral than a mortal error — doing so feels more like a reflection of your character than the precursor to any real consequences..

The ANC won by arguing evidence of intention, but rival parties jeered that a deadline that bends for whoever goes to court isn’t much of a deadline at all.

I think anyone who has been besieged by their own procrastination knows instinctively, though not always intellectually, that delays are less an issue of discipline than of proper emotional regulation. In a 2013 paper, researchers Fuschia Sirois and Timothy Pychyl note that putting things off is really an attempt to repair short-term mood: we do it to avoid how a task makes us feel, whether we’re bored, or fear judgement, or just cannot handle the uncertainty of the outcome. And we all believe, in our little overachieving minds, that the perception we’ve delivered something of quality outweighs our desire for timely completion.

The other thing is that if you miss deadlines often enough, one of two things happens. Either you become convinced you’re a person who can’t be relied on, until it’s baked into your sense of self and becomes harder to change. Or you breeze past enough “urgent” ones without real consequence that your brain decides the rules don’t really apply to you. The paradox is that deadlines carry more weight than ever as markers of performance and persona, and also mean less and less.

A political party, of course, is not subject to the same emotional calculus as a teenager with a school paper. The ANC had to contend with infighting, complex logistics and, it maintains, a real technical obstacle. But the saga is a reminder that even the most established organizations can also miss deadlines that do matter.

Personally, I’m sending this to Colin and Louis at WITI on the day of the vote, two months after deciding to write it, having vowed to start on time and promising myself I would send it by Monday. Deadlines may be too blunt an instrument to ensure we get things done (well-kept rituals and personal systems do a much better job of that). But it’s hard to argue we could do away with them altogether and still keep a sense of order, fairness and consistency. At some point, a button needs to be pushed. (KB)
