The Monday Media Diet with Alfred Tong

On The Book of Snobs, Arsenal influencers, and Conversations on Love by Natasha Lunn

May 26
 
READ IN APP
 

Alfred Tong (AT) is a writer covering fashion and luxury for Sunday Times Style, Esquire, and on his excellent Substack, Notes. -CJN

Tell us about yourself.

I write about yearning, desire and longing: for people, places and things. Which is a long-winded way of saying I write a Substack about the luxury goods business.

The foundation for what I do now was probably growing up in my dad’s restaurant on Maddox Street. Turn left and you’d hit Savile Row and Bond Street; turn right and you were in Soho. Growing up in 1990s London — and later studying at London College of Fashion in the 2000s — was where I learned most of what I know about taste, charisma and social theatre. I grew up surrounded by bold, optimistic people who were always willing to have a go. And that’s always stayed with me.

Now I try to write something atmospheric and character-led. Scenes, jokes, tragedies, stupid details. I’m very fond of the absurd. Nicky Haslam once said, “Bad taste is anything boring,” which is pretty much my working philosophy.

So I write about taste, luxury, status, vanity and pretentiousness — all the things that make human beings ridiculous and fascinating, and basically the reason I get up in the morning.

What is a book everyone should read?

The Book of Snobs by William Makepeace Thackeray.

Thackeray is my OG for this sort of thing, followed by Tom Wolfe and then Peter York. There’s a thread running through all three: baroque writing about rich people twisting themselves into pretzels over taste, style and status.

But Thackeray’s essays about the emerging consumer and luxury culture of 19th-century Britain are really the blueprint — and written in parallel with his masterpiece, Vanity Fair. There’s a lovely, probably apocryphal theory that “snob” comes from sine nobilitate — without nobility — which is almost too perfect. Either way, Thackeray is the reason the word still has such sting. It’s amazing how much he was already writing about what we’d now call hipster food culture. He would have found Anthony Bourdain earnestly eating noodles in Vietnam absolutely hilarious.

I also love that Thackeray only started writing seriously after squandering a family fortune on gambling and suddenly finding himself with a family to support. We get so precious about writing. Really, most of us do it because we have to. Because we can’t do anything else. And because we need the money.

Describe your media diet.

Pretty mad, and involving far too much time on Instagram.

Martin Amis once described America as “the moronic inferno,” and every time I open Instagram or TikTok I think: yes, exactly that.

As a fashion writer, you have to be on those platforms. You also see, in miniature, how audiences reward vanity and flexing above almost everything else. Any picture of me in a nice outfit, with a semi-famous person, somewhere rich and expensive, will outperform something remotely thoughtful every single time.

I’d love to tell you my media diet is a carefully considered rotation of Monocle, the Financial Times, beautifully annotated literary journals and judiciously chosen Substacks. And it is, partly. But in reality, much of it is consumed in the badlands.

Lately I’ve become obsessed with Arsenal influencers. Arsenal F.C. seem to be the epicentre of an intense online psychodrama. This year’s agonising tilt at the title has made everyone go mad. There’s one little boy called Roman from Bethnal Green, aged about five, who delivers post-match analysis on a par with grown men.

I also love Socks House Meeting, the king of Broadway Market. That whole corner of the internet feels like the meme-version of Peter York.

Plane or train?

Oh my God, train. A thousand times train.

Planes are vile: nasty tubes of germs and filth shot into the sky, with us inside them.

I call Ryanair the Skybarrow.

Honestly, planes put me off travelling. During my GQ years I occasionally got to fly first or business class long-haul to places like Shanghai, which completely ruins you for economy once you’re paying for your own travel. I’m from Hong Kong and desperately want to go back and see relatives, but the thought of long-haul economy is bleak.

Trains, on the other hand, are lovely. I live a 20-minute walk from St Pancras International, so Paris, Amsterdam and Brussels are always a good idea. What more do you want?

I also think we travel too much, and often to no great end. I don’t see people coming back wiser or profoundly altered. In Europe, perhaps we need to learn to stay put. Haven’t we had enough of Europeans going everywhere? Mostly, travel makes you feel important. It gives you main-character energy. That’s all.

Tell us the story of a rabbit hole you fell deep into.

I’ve recently fallen very deep into using AI to do my tarot cards and horoscopes for my love life, which is both deeply embarrassing and, at times, terrifyingly accurate. I’ve trained it to be fairly brutal and slightly masochistic. I occasionally miss the hype-man version, but it’s probably for the best.

We often talk about AI as a machine for producing slop — and obviously a lot of it is — but I’m more optimistic about its role in writing. It can’t have feelings. It doesn’t have taste, instinct or chemistry. Those things remain profoundly human, and they’re essential to good writing.

But AI is brilliant at structure and organisation. It can turn huge interview transcripts into thematic quotes in seconds. It can help sequence random ideas into a flowing narrative. It’s fantastic for rearranging the beats of a story at the click of a button. In that sense, it’s less a writer than an extremely pedantic copy editor sitting beside you at all times.

I think most people either use it far too much, in which case everything becomes dead-eyed slop, or too little, in which case they’re missing out on a genuinely useful tool. Used properly, it’s excellent for stress-testing ideas, tightening sentences and sharpening structure.

And tarot, obviously.

What’s the last great book you read?

I’m never entirely sure because I usually have a gazillion books on the go at once. But currently sitting on the empty wine crate I use as a makeshift bookstand is Jeff in Venice, Death in Varanasi by Geoff Dyer.

It’s about a journalist sent on a press trip to the Venice Biennale — basically someone very much like me. It’s absurd and poignant and captures the magic of a press trip, in which you’re made to feel very important while simultaneously doing very little except drink, eat lovely free dinners, flirt with whoever’s sitting next to you, and look at stuff.

I found my copy in a charity shop in Marylebone, and there’s a hilarious scene in which he gets his hair dyed in Marylebone when he’s supposed to be filing copy, and I just thought: is the universe taking the piss?

What are you reading now?

My life has recently been touched by death and tragedy, and I found reading Conversations on Love by Natasha Lunn genuinely helpful.

It’s about all the different kinds of love we experience, not just romantic love. The cover is bright pink, which means it has probably been marketed towards women. Fine. We shouldn’t pander to men with a blue version or whatever. But men: you will survive reading a pink book, and may even find much to cherish in this one. Honestly.

All I do, really, is work and love — and what else is there? — but I rarely stop to think about either properly.

What’s your reading strategy when you pick up a print copy of your favourite publication?

At weekends it’s normally the Financial Times, though I mostly just look at the pictures in How To Spend It.

A friend of mine has recently taken to reading the answers from HTSI’s The Aesthete out loud in an absolutely straight voice, which is one of the funniest things I’ve ever heard.

Who should everyone be reading that they’re not?

I love snooker, and Ronnie — Ronnie O’Sullivan’s autobiography, ghostwritten by Simon Hattenstone — is genuinely brilliant.

There’s a scene where Ronnie O’Sullivan and Jimmy White are at Ronnie Wood’s house, getting stoned and pissed over the snooker table. Apparently they were drinking a jar of vodka and orange per break. Wood says he’d never seen anything like it: the pair of them, completely out of their minds, knocking in 100-plus breaks toe-to-toe.

What is the best non-famous app you love on your phone?

Before my phone got stolen, I was obsessed with Yuka, which was hugely popular among the French and British chattering classes. You scanned food barcodes and it rated them out of 100. Percy Pigs got something like 4/100; Burford Brown eggs scored somewhere in the 80s.

I became mildly addicted to scanning everything: sauces, frozen dumplings, strange drinks from Asian supermarkets. The app was extremely confident about British junk food, but utterly baffled by anything foreign.

I am also in possession of a leopard-print Soho Weekly Diary from Smythson. Does that count?

What is one place everyone should visit?

Jeremy King once told me that, of all the restaurants he has looked after over the years, the one with the best luck — and the highest number of romances that actually worked out — was Le Caprice, now The Arlington.

His advice was to sit at the bar, side by side, rather than opposite each other. Face-to-face is a confrontation. Side-by-side takes the pressure off. (AT)
