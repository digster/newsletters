The Real McCoy’s Edition

On fidelity, Americana, and moving beyond reproduction.

Colin Nagy
Jun 4
 
READ IN APP
 

Colin here. Some brands chase the future. The Real McCoy’s has spent four decades chasing the past so precisely that it now arguably makes American clothing better than America ever did.

The origin story is epic. In 1978, an eighteen-year-old named Hitoshi Tsujimoto took a forty-day road trip across the United States, returning to Japan with a trunk of jeans, athletic jerseys and sweatshirts, which he sold at a swap meet in Osaka. He spent the next decade as a collector and buyer, learning the clothes thread by thread, and by the mid-1980s had opened a small vintage shop in Amerikamura, the Osaka district that became Japan’s epicenter for American fashion and youth culture.

The brand followed. On a recent trip, I wandered into the Osaka store almost by accident, pure serendipity for a longtime fan, and it reframed the whole thing. The simplest explanation for why the label exists: Tsujimoto wanted to sell the best vintage clothing in the world, and was running out of the good old stuff. So he started recreating it.

Why is this interesting?

Calling it reproduction grossly undersells what’s happening. Most repro brands squint and lovingly gesture at the past. The Real McCoy’s reverse-engineers it, all the way down to the yarn. (Tsujimoto's personal archive is one of the largest vintage collections in the world, reportedly containing more than 10,000 pairs of Levi’s, and serves as both his inspiration and core source material.) Every Real McCoy’s button and every thread is made exclusively for the brand, and the sweats are knit in Wakayama on traditional hanging loopwheel machines, a slow process we’ve covered in the past that aerates and strengthens the fabric. The brand’s patience borders on the absurd: A pair of 1930s cordovan engineer boots sat in R&D for eight years before launching, and the line produces roughly twenty pairs a month.

The distinction that separates all this from mere cosplay is that Tsujimoto isn’t a copyist. By his own account, the point was never to imitate classic styles, but to perfect and even quietly improve them. He’s subtly refined cuts, since 1940s military patterns were boxy by design, in order to carry weapons and tools across the body, resulting in a functional silhouette that can read a tad sloppy on a stylish civilian.

The goal was never to deliver a replica, but rather each style’s ideal final form: The piece the wartime factory would have made if it hadn’t been racing to meet a quota. In practice, that means materials sourced from around the world and run through machinery most manufacturers retired generations ago, and a supply chain that makes no economic sense and total spiritual sense.

The house operates several labels: the military mainline, Joe McCoy for civilian sportswear and workwear, and Buco, the leather motorcycle marque founded in 1933 that Tsujimoto acquired and revived, its 1940s and 50s horsehide jackets among the most iconic ever made. The brand is headquartered and largely produced in Kobe, historically one of the few ports where Japanese locals could encounter foreign fashion, music and food before the country fully opened to the world. The flagship there, and the handful of shops in Tokyo, London and elsewhere, function less like retail and more like small museums where the exhibits happen to be for sale.

There’s also an extended family tree worth knowing, because it’s part of the lore. An early shake-up scattered RMC alumni into their own labels: Hiroshi Okamoto left to start Toys McCoy, and Atsushi Yasui, who’d run the Joe McCoy sub-brand, left to found Freewheelers. The brand created a thoughtful diaspora of designers, equally uncompromising across their new endeavors.

In terms of garments to explore, the A-2 and the loopwheeled sweats are the brand’s gateway drugs, the items that launched the whole Japan does Americana better thesis. The grails sit a notch deeper. The N-1 deck jacket, especially the alpaca-lined version, is arguably the most coveted piece of outerwear in the main line (and the aftermarket prices hold). The B-3 flight jacket (hard to pull off unless you’re a B-17 crewman in Memphis Belle!) is built from veg-tanned horsehide and premium Australian sheepskin. Anything Buco represents another grail—and the J-series leather jackets are the spiritual center.

The through-line here is a kind of cultural anthropology in leather and loop-wheeled cotton: a Japanese reverence for mid-century America so exacting it preserved techniques America itself had abandoned. The originals were made by a young country that was, in the brand’s own framing, unburdened by tradition and totally innovative. These reproductions are made today with the opposite instincts entirely—extreme fidelity, and near-total tradition. This really is the crux of it all: It took Japanese obsession to make America’s leftover clothes immortal. (CJN)
