The Monday Media Diet with Holly Hartland

On Keiran Doddard, Madonna in a Fur Coat, and Deborah Levy

Oct 12
 
READ IN APP
 

Holly Hartland works in media and writes Sybaritic on Substack. Check her highly enjoyable NYC Martini breakdown here.

Tell us about yourself.

I’m a woman on an eternal quest for pleasure.

My life is governed primarily by the philosophy bestowed upon us by Kim Cattrall: I don’t want to be in a situation for even an hour where I’m not enjoying myself.

I document my pursuit of pleasure in my newsletter, the name of which comes from the ancient Greek city of Sybaris – a place famed for the hedonism of its citizens. My writing seeks to answer VERY IMPORTANT questions like where to find a good martini, ways to feel more sexy, and how to navigate the dating scene in London. I also write some juicy party reports, so if you are reading this and planning something spectacular, consider this my official RSVP.

During the day, I work at Condé Nast as an advisor across brands like Vogue, GQ and Vanity Fair. I’m a big lover of magazines and media, so I am of course very excited to be here. Thanks for having me!

Describe your media diet.

I read a lot for work: articles, trend forecasts, market reports, etc. It’s great actually, because I get to consume a lot of content under the guise of “research”; my excessive scrolling is what keeps me “tapped into the zeitgeist”.

Substack is a big player in my media diet too. I am constantly amazed by the level of talent and creativity on the platform. It’s become one of my favourite corners of the internet – even more so now the algorithm has finally stopped showing me those “how to grow your audience” articles that feel suspiciously like pyramid selling.

There’s also a great social scene on Substack. It’s probably overtaken Instagram for me when it comes to online community, though I do still love to keep up to date with local news via London-centric meme pages (shoutout Socks House Meeting and DalstonSuperStoned).

What’s the last great book you read?

I tend to get along well with fiction that is character-driven, slow-moving and usually a bit bleak. Some contemporary books I’ve enjoyed as of late are I See Buildings Fall Like Lightning by Keiran Doddard and Evenings and Weekends by Oisin McKenna.

On the classic front, I finally read The Stranger by Albert Camus not too long ago. I loved it (I’m sorry).

What are you reading now?

I’ve just finished Madonna in a Fur Coat by Sabahattin Ali and am now reading The Princess of 72nd Street by Elaine Kraf. It’s great so far.

What’s your reading strategy when you pick up a print copy of your favorite publication?

I’m very lucky in that I get to work across some of my favourite publications, so for me the strategy comes well before the material has gone to print.

Vogue is my number one. When it comes to reading a physical copy of the magazine, I suppose I let curiosity take the wheel – there isn’t a strategy as such, though I do like to look out for familiar advertisers and editorial initiatives I’ve worked on. It’s always fun to see those things come to life.

Who should everyone be reading that they’re not?

Deborah Levy! I’d like to live inside her brain for a day. She makes even the weirdest thoughts sound so wonderful.

What is the best non-famous app you love on your phone?

Step is good. It lets you create your own city guides by mapping out things like bars, shops, museums, cultural sites, etc. I use mine mostly as a visual list of places I want to visit (near and far), and you can also add friends and follow other users to see what they recommend.

Plane or train?

I love the train.

Actually, I recently had a great two hour train journey where I was befriended by the elderly lady sitting next to me. She asked a lot of questions, standard stuff like WHERE ARE YOU HEADED TODAY and WHAT DO YOU DO FOR WORK and ARE YOU ON “THE APPS” and ARE BOTH SETS OF YOUR GRANDPARENTS STILL ALIVE?

I answered dutifully – I felt fine sharing all of these things with her because she was being so open with me. I found out her reason for travelling was to see her daughter, who was going through a divorce and struggling with the terrible business of selling her house.

On that subject, she asked if I own the flat I live in. I shook my head and told her that I rent.

She smiled sweetly at me then, offering some words of comfort: AH, WELL THAT’S THE PROBLEM WITH HAVING BOTH SETS OF GRANDPARENTS STILL ALIVE! WHEN THEY POP IT, YOU’LL BE SORTED!

Anyway. Yes, I think train over plane.

What is one place everyone should visit?

Oxford Street in London on a weekend in December.

I can’t think of anywhere more overstimulating and overpopulated. It’s hell on earth, but I believe everyone should experience it at least once in their life, because down the line when you are having a terrible day, you can take a step back and think: ok, yes I feel Really Really Bad right now, but hey things could be worse! At least I’m not having cold sweats in a crowd outside of Oxford Circus tube station!

Tell us the story of a rabbit hole you fell deep into.

I was in Rhode Island a few weeks ago, where I visited the Newport Mansions. This sent me down a deep rabbit hole of reading about the Vanderbilt family and the Gilded Age more broadly. I love history – it’s actually what I studied at uni, so I’m always fascinated by these kinds of sites.
