Hamish Smyth [ https://substack.com/redirect/a3d0ae13-2801-40b4-b995-08d7225b91bb?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] is a talented designer, and created the new WITI identity. In this interview, we go deep on how he thinks about design, and how he thought about the project. 
Tell us about your design practice. What is the work you’re most proud of?
I’m one of the co-founders of Order [ https://substack.com/redirect/e1fcb3ea-e60c-414c-88d9-fd2873aeaa9a?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ]. My partner Jesse Reed and I started it in 2017 after working together for Michael Bierut [ https://substack.com/redirect/50b377ba-85d8-4670-9746-bafa7b241c95?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] at Pentagram for a number of years.
We set out to do the type of work Michael was doing, because that’s what we knew how to [ https://substack.com/redirect/a8dd339b-3ea4-418d-91c9-dd5e4ba13161?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] do. We were sort of apprentices of his, so, branding and identity-focused graphic design. Graphic Design with a capital G and D. Which is actually a little old-school now with so much focus on digital devices.
Order started in New York, and more recently I moved to Miami and opened an office here. So we now have two offices, Brooklyn and Miami, that Jesse and I run independently under the Order umbrella. I’ve been working on a handful of projects down here that I will be releasing soon. Miami is obviously a super distinct design environment from New York, which has been fun to get into but also challenging because of how different it is.
The last project I worked on with Michael and his partner Luke Hayman was the global rebrand of Mastercard. We did the identity part: the logo and the high-level assets. Apparently it’s one of the most reproduced logos in the world, which, if you think about it, probably makes sense. It’s on every storefront and in everyone’s wallet. If I’d negotiated a penny every time it appeared, I wouldn’t be talking to you!
Order grew out of that kind of work, but we’ve always had other interests. Jesse and I also co-founded Standards Manual [ https://substack.com/redirect/3ff97da3-0f5a-4b7c-8e44-dcc5f1769bd8?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ], which started with republishing the New York City Subway [ https://substack.com/redirect/ed40c857-e37e-404b-be61-3fc705cbfc9b?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] and NASA Graphics Standards [ https://substack.com/redirect/b500794c-43d0-4c47-88bb-967ee9d2cc58?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] Manuals. In 2020 we launched Standards [ https://substack.com/redirect/d616b810-61a7-4c2a-8006-8805c9e086ab?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ], which is basically taking that idea into the future: a digital platform for brand guidelines instead of a giant PDF nobody knows what to do with.
The project I’m probably most proud of in my Pentagram era is WalkNYC [ https://substack.com/redirect/6f81f730-88e3-4a96-8694-463cb6fbcc8b?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ], the pedestrian wayfinding system for New York City. I was responsible for the map and the graphic layout of the signs. It was a huge job. And quite daunting because you’re making this thing that’s going to be all over New York.
We decided to connect it visually to the Subway graphics, even though DOT was making these signs and MTA runs the subway. To a person walking around New York, who cares? It should feel like one city, one system.
One Order project I’m particularly proud of is Every [ https://substack.com/redirect/04a5e3da-4723-4610-89eb-9d4463bf6f34?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ], the publishing platform founded by Dan Shipper and Nathan Baschez. We built the identity around this idea of business writing (literally) and developed a custom handwritten typeface inspired by the Palmer Method. It’s a good example of the kind of work I love: there’s a simple idea at the center, but it can expand into a whole system.
You’re designing a thing, obviously, but really you’re figuring out the logic behind how everything fits together.
Who are the designers that had the most influence on you?
The biggest influence, easily, is Michael Bierut.
I studied at RMIT in Melbourne and won a scholarship that brought me to New York for two weeks. I’d never left Australia. I landed on Michael’s team at Pentagram and ended up staying for seven years. And now I’ve somehow been in America for nearly twenty.
Working for Michael was basically seven years of learning through osmosis. Obviously he’s an incredible designer, but he’s also an amazing speaker, extremely funny, and very good at explaining why an idea works to both clients and his team. Watching how he thought through a problem and then how he talked about it was probably as influential as the actual design work.
There are lots of other designers whose work I love, but in terms of someone who really shaped the way I work, it’s Michael.
What is your favorite medium to work in?
I don’t know if this is technically a medium, but I really love the refinement stage working with a logo or wordmark.
You’ve got an idea. Everyone likes it. Maybe it’s even been approved. And then you sit down and start really looking at it. You print it out, blow it up massive on screen, look at every letterform and every bit of spacing, and make all these tiny changes that absolutely nobody is ever going to notice.
But if you do it right, you make twenty or thirty or fifty of those tiny changes, and then you put the original next to the final version and the original suddenly looks terrible. The final one just feels inevitable. Like, of course it looks like that.
I love that process. You sort of lose yourself in the details. It feels less like “having an idea” and more like being a technician or specialist. People call it craft, which is a word I don’t particularly like, but I suppose that’s what it is.
So maybe my favorite medium is just doing finished logo artwork? Which is a terribly dull-sounding to normal people, but I think designers will relate!
I also love traditional materials, photography, anything where you can get your hands dirty. If you’re a graphic designer of my age, you’re legally required to be into film photography.
How did the WITI logo idea come about? Rumor has it there was a Hillstone coaster involved.
There was! Maximum designer cliche achieved. One of my favorite restaurants and places to sit  is the bar at Hillstone in Coral Gables. I go with a design problem, take a notebook, have dinner and a couple of drinks, and just sort of sit with it.
Except I’d forgotten my notebook. I do that a lot. Eventually they just started giving me paper and pens when I arrived.
So, I was sitting there writing “Why Is This Interesting?”, “Why Is This Interesting?”, and so on, over and over again. Underlining different words, changing emphasis,  capitalization. I was trying to find an angle on it. I must have written it twenty times. At one point I turned to the people next to me who’d noticed and said, “You probably think I’m insane.”
Anyway, the basic idea came out of that. Technically, it was a bartender’s notecard.
What did you arrive at? And when did things click?
“Why Is This Interesting?” is a very long name! There aren’t many longer ones you’d want to turn into a logo. People are familiar with the shorter name WITI, so we knew we had that graphic version to work with. But the thing that really clicked was the idea of emphasis using italics with the wordmark.
It’s fair to say WITI covers so many different things, and to me, the thing tying it all together is curiosity. I realized you could influence the meaning of the name just by emphasizing a different word:
Why is this interesting?
Why is this interesting?
Why is this interesting?
Why is this interesting?
It’s the same question, framed differently. The way a curious person looks at the world.
Once that idea was there, the rest started to fall into place. This is without a doubt my favorite feeling with my work. When it clicks, I say it’s done. The hard part is over. And all that now needs to happen is to get it on paper.
That is of course another story, but if you’re at a bar and reach this moment, you are allowed to put your pencils down. Just make sure you write it down first [ https://substack.com/redirect/f9d63d07-2541-45a6-9a62-b44e5bc04987?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ].
The logo?
The shorter WITI version was always going to be the more graphic mark. We took the italic capitals and stacked them, following the strokes of the letterforms until it became this almost abstract little thing.
To me it looks a bit like a watchmaker’s mark from 1780 or something. Which I like, and I know Colin likes. It has a little bit of mystery to it, and that felt right for WITI.
The word mark?
The full name is stacked over four lines, and that’s really where the emphasis idea gets to play out.
We’re using Signifier by Klim Type Foundry [ https://substack.com/redirect/af9f5329-3084-49da-94f5-18be09a0f204?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ]. It has this sort of scholarly, bookish feeling to me, but it also has an amazing italic, which obviously became very important once the idea was based around changing the emphasis of the phrase.
So the wordmark can be pretty restrained, but then one italicized word shifts the whole thing.
How did you think about this as an expandable system?
Once we had the emphasis idea, that was the system.
I like finding what I call a trick. Something simple enough that it can keep going without feeling forced. So the italics don’t just live in the logo. You can emphasize a word in a headline, an invitation, a piece of copy, whatever. Suddenly it all feels connected without needing to slap a logo on everything.
That’s usually the kind of identity system I’m interested in. You set up a few rules, and then ideally they can keep generating things.
Any tiny details you obsessed over? How did you land on the end result?
Loads! There was a lot of finessing in the main wordmark—spacing letters, getting letters to line up, cradle one another, feel harmonious, and generally making many microscopic adjustments that look like nothing on their own, but together make the whole thing feel like it was just meant to be that way.
And then the WITI mark had its own little set of problems. Following the strokes, getting the italic forms to work together, making it feel like one thing rather than four letters stacked on top of each other. Making it somewhat readable, but abstract enough to feel like a “brand mark.” That’s the stuff I really like.
There are so many things out in the world that are really well designed and nobody has any idea. And I don’t just mean graphic design. Have you ever opened an exquisite doorknob? A really good doorknob. They don’t just appear out of thin air! Somebody sweated over that doorknob for an absurd amount of time, and if they did their job properly, you never think about why it’s so interesting.
