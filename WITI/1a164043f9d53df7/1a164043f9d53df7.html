The Century Edition

On Toyota, exporting restraint, and the V12 engine.

Colin Nagy
Oct 22

Colin here. In 1967, Toyota built a car to mark the 100th birthday of its founder, Sakichi Toyoda, and named it the Century. Until then, Japan’s industrialists and ministers had been chauffeured in luxury Western cars. But quickly, the Century became shorthand for conservative success in boardrooms and ministries, and the Imperial household started to use them too.

Last October, Toyota spun it out as a standalone brand, with Akio Toyoda calling it a vehicle for the “pride of Japan.” Now, the vehicle is officially coming to the US. (In the past, Toyota imported a handful of sedans for its own executives here, but nobody has ever been able to buy one new.)

Per Automotive News, Century sales begin in 2028 through a small group of Lexus dealers, who all needed to upgrade their facilities and train their staff in order to qualify.

Why is this interesting?

The first Century ran for 30 years with only light updates, and every one of them had a V8 engine. A limousine followed in 1989, and the Imperial household got its own variant, the Century Royal, in 2006. The second generation arrived in 1997 and stayed in production, largely unchanged, until 2018. The current car is only the third generation in nearly 60 years.

The 1997 car is the one heads obsess over. Toyota built it with no assembly line, and no robots, only craftsmen. Wood paneling was cut so the grain runs continuously across the cabin, the dashboard clock was crystal glass. Under the hood sat the only V12 Toyota has ever put in a passenger car, a decision Toyota took for its smoothness.

The current generation, launched in 2018 at about 19.6 million yen, with a target of 50 sales a month, is as meticulously designed. The phoenix emblem on the grille, which replaces the Toyota oval, takes about six weeks to hand-carve. The black paint is completed with seven coats infused with black pigment to give a lacquer look, and wet-sanded by hand three times.

Every one of these choices points towards the person being driven in the back seat. The Century behaves like a ryokan that has never needed a website, or a grand hotel whose best suite never reaches the website. The product is the absence of friction. The staff are trained for years, and the guest’s status is confirmed by how little anyone remarks on it.

If it arrives stateside with the Century phoenix intact and a dealer network small enough to feel like a club, Toyota will have done something rare, which is to export restraint at a serious premium. The coupe concept shown in Tokyo suggests the bet is on Japanese craft as the differentiator.

It will be interesting to see if the current American market will be interested in paying for luxury with extreme discretion. But for those that know, being driven in the car is actually one of the biggest power moves. (CJN)
