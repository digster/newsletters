The Monday Media Diet with Marc Blazer

On How Architects Live, the Stanza, and C Thi Nguyen

Marc Blazer and Colin Nagy
Oct 19
 
READ IN APP
 

Marc Blazer is a friend of WITI and many of many talents. His new book, How Architects Live, is available here. For a deeper dive on the book, check out his excellent Substack.

Tell us about yourself.

I am a father, husband, entrepreneur, investor, sometimes writer, recovering banker and frustrated tennis player. In between all of these things, I run the vacation home brand Boutique.

I’ve had a career that is hard to pigeon hole, starting out in politics, and then leaving the virtuous world of Washington for the equally virtuous world of Wall Street. While I might have lost some of my idealism in that journey, it did give me a life philosophy that has shaped the third chapter of my work life. Namely, do things that you can explain (with pride) to your loved ones, and that enrich your life, before it enriches your bank account.

In my third chapter, I’ve had the chance to build some companies that defined the culture of dining (having co-owned and been the Executive Chairman of noma), travel (having co-founded PRIOR and acquired Boutique), and agriculture (with the Australian Agriculture Company).

I also recently released a book, How Architects Live, which follows the stories of 15 architects building homes for themselves. I learned more about life than architecture from these practitioners, in their quest to build homes to gather their loved ones, immerse themselves in nature, or find sanctuary in a turbulent world. They’re great lessons to be mindful of the choices we make in how we spend our time.

My two college age kids seem to have taken some of my decisions - and some of these lessons - to heart, and are studying fields they are deeply passionate about with a seriousness that I find very impressive. Something I can’t say I did when I was their age. It makes me immensely proud, and hopeful for their (and our) future.

Describe your media diet.

I am still very much a political junkie, although I admit that in the last decade, that has been very hard to stomach. So while Politico, The NYT, and Washington Post are still my first morning read, the inside baseball world of political score keeping has lost a lot of its appeal.

From a travel industry perspective, I turn to SKIFT and the Stanza, but I admit that I prefer building my business focused on how I like to travel, and therefore with the belief that others are likely to have the same interest. So I am a bit of a contrarian, keeping my blinders on, and not following what my competitors and the industry is doing.

From a travel inspiration, of course I am biased toward PRIOR’s newsletters, and my friend Yolanda Edward’s YOLO Journal, both of which are my travel bibles. The FT’s HTSI, and our mutual friend Maria Shollenbarger’s Travelista column are my go-to for understanding what is next. Maria has the most wonderful way with words. My wife also complains (rightly) that I never get through my subscriptions to AD, Dwell, Cabana, Monocle, and they end up in piles all around the house.

I can’t go into a bookstore without buying a design book. My coffee table and bookshelves groan under the weight of the ever increasing stack of large format books. I am just endlessly inspired by beautiful places, which perhaps comes as no surprise given my day job.

I keep up with news from the Netherlands (I grew up in Amsterdam, and still have family there), and watch the NOS Journaal almost daily. It’s a good way to understand how the rest of the world (or at least my little corner of it) views what’s going on here. I try to read the South China Morning Post regularly, as I’ve got a lot of friends and business interests in Asia. I get news clippings from Australia each day, although I find much of the media landscape there pretty provincial and depressing (thanks Mr. Murdoch).

What’s the last great book you read?

A friend recently turned me on to an article by C Thi Nguyen about the importance of play, which in turn was a reflection on the book The Grasshopper by Bernard Suits. That really sent me down the rabbit hole of thinking about why we are so obsessed with eliminating friction and optimizing every minute of our modern day lives. Yet often life’s greatest joys are as a result of friction. Play, to paraphrase Suits, is to voluntarily take on unnecessary obstacles in order to make possible the struggle to overcome them. Frankly, life’s greatest joys are often a result of its greatest inefficiencies.

What are you reading now?

I just finished Beyond Honor by my friends Bill and Karen Brodsky. It’s their debut novel, and very reminiscent of the old genre of Le Carre spy novels. It was the perfect airplane read for my recent travels to Singapore and Australia.

What’s your reading strategy when you pick up a print copy of your favorite publication?

I feel immense pressure to have to read every page, which is why so often I never get around to the stack of magazines gathering dust on my bedside table.

Who should everyone be reading that they’re not?

If you want to understand everything about our digital lives, and why we are so addicted to our devices, you have to read Nir Eyal’s 2014 book Hooked. Eyal wrote a masterful (and scary) dissertation on human behavior and how “successful” digital products exploit our innate need for reward. When he wrote it in 2014, it was a blue print for product designers and start-up founders. Now you can’t unsee what it really means for humanity at the cusp of the era of artificial general intelligence.

What is the best non-famous app you love on your phone?

Brass. It’s an app that transforms your color over-saturated, over-stimulation provoking icons into an orderly, monochromatic and minimalist grid of symbols that don’t scream “pick me up.” It’s the closest digital product I have found that feels as if Dieter Rams could have designed it. I imagine this is the direction Steve Jobs would have taken Apple into, instead of the crass icons of ios today.

Plane or train?

In Europe, definitely trains, although I prefer driving myself. It rarely makes sense to fly between European capitals given the relatively close distances, and the speed of its rail network. In the US, NEVER trains.

What is one place everyone should visit?

The National Holocaust Names Monument in Amsterdam. It is a Daniel Liebeskind designed monument with 102,000 bricks each bearing the name of a Dutch victim of the holocaust (many of my family members are sadly on it). It is a stark illustration of humanity’s depravity, and a reminder that feels particularly important today.

The post-WWII experiment of a united and centrist Europe, built on a “welfare” system anchored in human dignity, and an American led world order focused on democratic values, seems increasingly distant in the rearview mirror. The monument is a reminder of what could happen if we don’t heed the lessons of history, and turn back to a more rational and centrist society (I love the Dutch translation of society, “samenleving” which means literally living together).

Tell us the story of a rabbit hole you fell deep into.

Much to my wife’s chagrin, I have an unhealthy (for our checkbook in any case) obsession with “youngtimer” classic cars. Youngtimers are cars about 20-30 years old, that happen to coincide with my coming of age. I love Bring-a-Trailer (another app on my phone), which is an auction site for cars of all ages.

I have gone very deep into the auction history of a few of my favorite models, and often compare the auction results with listings on European marketplaces like Martplaats, and Autoscout. A few years ago I bought a 40 year old BMW in Europe (that happened to be restored by the son of a former truck driver for my father and uncle), and then a year later (on Bring-a-Trailer) another 20 year old BMW. This was shortly after I bought my son a 15 year old FIAT Abarth. I now have my eye on Alfa Romeo’s of the 1970’s, but don’t tell my wife. Like I said, the greatest joys in life are often the result of friction (I don’t mean with my wife). Buying old cars is the perfect illustration of that. (MB)
