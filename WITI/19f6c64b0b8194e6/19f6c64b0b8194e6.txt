Alex here. A recent trip to Cairo made me realise what a huge, existential challenge our world’s cities face. By population the world’s fifth largest city, Cairo is currently dealing with overcrowding and considerable pressure on its utilities, with its population said to be increasing by approximately 500,000 residents annually. If you zoom out geographically, the numbers tell the same story.
The whole of Africa is urbanising fast. In 1990, just 31% of Africans lived in cities. By 2020, that figure had reached 50%. By 2050, it’s projected to approach two-thirds. Over the next 25 years, the continent will grow from 60 to almost 160 cities of more than one million people, including 17 “megacities”—populations of over ten million people—second only to Asia.
Worldwide, figures suggest that by 2050, nearly 70% of the world’s population will live in cities. There will be nearly 70 megacities, up from 44 today. 
Around 75% of the infrastructure that will exist in 2050 to cope with it all has not yet been built.
Why is this interesting?
The growth of megacities is one of humanity’s most intriguing design challenges. The task is often framed as a technical one, of innovation and real estate, but really it’s a cultural one too. And there’s a fundamental tension between what must be built quickly, and what can only be grown slowly.
How do you design a city for today? The answer is you don’t. You can’t prescribe meaning, you can’t rush belonging. What you can design are the conditions for a city to become itself – the social anchors, the districts, the public places, and the systems that tie them all together. Success comes from cultivating culture, not imposing it.
After all, no great city was fully formed from the outset, and the best ones have let their citizens continue to influence their design. Following a devastating flood in 1957, Valencia planned to replace its diverted river with a motorway. When residents pushed back, the space instead became the 9km-long Turia Gardens, now one of Spain’s most loved public spaces.
Cities are never really finished. The Barcelona of today is also not the Barcelona of even ten years ago. Faced with growing concerns about overtourism, rising housing costs and the erosion of local identity, the city has increasingly shifted its focus from being a city to visit to a city to live in. Efforts to pedestrianise streets, reduce traffic and reclaim public space, including along parts of La Rambla, reflect a broader move towards liveability and quality of life. 
The same principle can be seen elsewhere. In New York, the High Line emerged from a grassroots effort to transform a disused railway into public space, while in Seoul the Cheonggyecheon Stream was restored after decades beneath a highway, creating a new civic heart for the city. 
London’s Canary Wharf was originally conceived as a corporate business district, and for many years felt like a ghost town at the weekends. Rather than accept this as the area’s terminal state, cultural programming was instead brought in in recent years to broaden its appeal—more public attractions, independent retail, better restaurants, nicer housing—transforming it into a destination where people increasingly choose to also live, visit, and spend regular time in.
Great cities endure because they continue adapting to the people who inhabit them. Ultimately, it’s the placemaking equivalent of Wes Jackson’s observation: ‘If your life’s work can be accomplished in your lifetime, you’re not thinking big enough’. If a city’s DNA can be constructed in a single lifetime, we’re not thinking big enough. 
Our next cities will flourish only by creating frameworks that can adapt, reflecting shared values and behaviours, and allow future generations to reinterpret the spaces they inhabit. There are no shortcuts. (AL)
