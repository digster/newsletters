A while back here, Those thoughts came rushing back recently, as I read an What I loved about her piece was how little of it concerns the music (though the music was predictably great). She catalogues the toilets, the sitting areas, the smoking area, and the little ledges around the dancefloor for your drink. She then tries to explain why the whole place felt so comfortable, drawing on some anthropology training. Along the way, she names the two best-sounding dancefloors she’s encountered in a decade. One is the aforementioned Open Ground, and the other is Klymax—the club DJ Harvey designed with Potato Head in Bali, Why is this interesting? Her central observation is that nothing at Open Ground makes you compete. There’s no queue at the door, so the night doesn’t open with a standoff with a bouncer. There’s always room to dance, so nobody defends a patch of floor. Seating is built into the building, so you never have to improvise a perch on the stairs. The lighting is minimal, so nobody is on overt display. Ledges run along the walls and stand at the edges of the floor, so you can set a slippery vodka soda down within arm’s reach anywhere. She reads all of this as proof that someone thought hard about what humans and their bodies need at 2am. (Now think about how many clubs, hotels and restaurants run on manufactured scarcity, or quieter tricks of design. Fast food chains have long been accused of making their seating deliberately uncomfortable so that you eat and leave. Fancy restaurants are often so loud you can’t focus on your conversation because of the cacophony.) Yu Su reaches for environmental psychology to explain the rest of her time at Open Ground, The acoustic treatment does social work too. In an echoey room, the Lombard effect kicks in: People speak louder, which makes the room louder, which makes everyone louder still. Open Ground’s main room rarely went above 100dB, quieter than almost any club she’s played, yet still full of textural detail. Her summary is the best line in the piece: “Nothing in that room asks you to brace.” Harvey arrived at the same place after forty years in the booth. While has also played on some of the worst sound systems imaginable and still had the time of his life, when I spoke to him for Monocle, he described Klymax, the club at Potato Head in Bali, as a machine where you “feed art in one end, and happy people come out the other.” Asked what makes a good club, he started with some good surprises: working bathrooms, friendly security and a well-run door. He also said that once people pay and come in, he feels personally responsible for their welfare. The look of the room is a byproduct of its acoustics: 2,680,135 holes perforated into wood panelling to diffuse sound across the floor. The floor itself is sprung like an old ballroom, so joints survive five or six hours of stomping. He pegged 500 people as the sweet spot, big enough for a crowd to feel brave enough to enter a state of thrall together, small enough to avoid just playing the easy hits. Behind the booth sits a toilet, a shower and a relaxation area. Put on a long track, go rinse off during a marathon set. fabric got there through operations. Co-founder Cameron Leslie studied hospitality while helping to build the club, and the lesson I took from those nights still holds: You can only lose yourself in a room once someone has made sure you feel safe and looked after. What stitches all three together is that a club sells a feeling it can never serve directly. The state everyone is chasing only arrives when a hundred small decisions about bodies, sound, sightlines, queues and toilets have been considered. Hotels call this invisible service. Clubs learn it faster, because a bad decision shows up faster. By Yu Su’s count, Open Ground spent seven years tuning its rooms. Harvey joked that you could probably buy most of Klymax’s components on Amazon Prime, and that the value lies in the collective experience of the people who assemble them. Still, a room can only nudge a party in the right direction, he told me. “But if you don’t have an intention…” He never finished the sentence. Yu Su’s notes from the train help finish it for him.
