Anita Schillhorn van Veen (ASVV) is a friend of WITI, executive director of strategy at ad agency McKinney in Los Angeles. She writes her own newsletter on marketing and culture, which you can read Anita here. After a recent injury, I’ve been spending a lot of time with doctors. To help me understand how my body works, MRIs and X-Rays have unveiled systems and networks of bones, muscles, nerves. Orthopedists, spine doctors, and physical therapists all read these like a map to see where the jams are, and then use letters and numbers - C5, C6, TFCC - to signify the locations where things have gone awry. One sprain is in a cluster of bones, tendons and ligaments so fine and tangled it’s like a complex of LA freeways snaking together, and the impact of a little jam ripples out into unexpected areas. Massage therapists dig into my muscles, stretch my fascia, and infrared therapy stimulates blood cells and healing. It’s given me new insight into the human body and how all of its networks weave together. Why is this interesting? Acupuncture has been one of the most effective ways for me to manage my pain. Acupuncturists put needles in my wrist, neck, and shoulder, where the pain is most prominent, but also into unexpected places like my knee, my foot, my ear, my skull. In Traditional Chinese Medicine, this is said to stimulate energy in a system defined by invisible meridians, or channels that run through the body. Even though these meridians haven’t been visible to the scientific eye, Western medicine made peace with them a long time ago, because this system of poking needles into unrelated body parts seemed to just… work. The practice of acupuncture made it into the pantheon of accepted medical methodologies in the West through trade, politics, immigration and fascination. But it took thousands of years for scientists to understand the exact physical system that makes it effective. As European missionaries and traders ventured to China, the West flirted with Traditional Chinese Medicine several times. It caught hold in France, where a unique form of acupuncture developed in the 19th century: The French school of acupuncture created new methodologies like ear acupuncture, and using electric currents with needles. That was until the 1970s, where a confluence of events brought it into the American mainstream, involving President Nixon and the Black Panthers. In one of the 20th century’s seminal political meetings, President Nixon visited China in 1972. James Reston, a New York Times journalist, was in China preparing for the visit, and required an emergency appendectomy. A doctor gave him acupuncture for post-surgical pain, and Reston was shocked to learn that a few thin needles alleviated his aches, and Around this time, a heroin epidemic was decimating the Bronx. Activist groups including the Black Panthers and the Young Lords created a community health clinic to address the crisis of addiction. Distrustful of white doctors then touting the benefits of methadone, one of the movements pre-eminent doctors (and Tupac Shakur’s step-father) Dr Mutulu Shakur advocated for acupuncture. He’d learned about it from a Quebecois school that had stemmed from the French lineage of acupuncture, as well as from community activists in Chinatown. Under him, the clinic developed an ear acupuncture protocol for addiction that’s still used today. Since the 1970s, acupuncture has gained legitimacy in the US and around the world as an evidence-based, effective medical practice. But Western science still didn’t have a real explanation for how it worked. Until recently. This means that after millennia of acupuncture working, this mysterious network of energy channels may finally have a physical basis, as solid as a skeleton. And a healing practice that has endured through time, and in the face of skepticism, may once and for all be validated.
