Part of what makes WITI fun is that we can do whatever we want. So, I asked one of my favorite travel writers, Maria Shollenbarger [ https://substack.com/redirect/6c126865-0a30-4479-b955-01862fef1ab8?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] (see her interview [ https://substack.com/redirect/a4c7e34a-4205-4f2a-bdd5-bbe7f071bfc2?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] + Substack [ https://substack.com/redirect/d0900ff6-fcae-434e-bee4-1b236bf2699c?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ]) to speak with Paul Theroux. And to take as much time and space as she wants.
Paul is the author of more than 70 books like the Mosquito Coast [ https://substack.com/redirect/e4503b8f-d7da-4c63-98b0-7fb5ddc2289e?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] and Dark Star Safari  [ https://substack.com/redirect/4925416c-df59-411f-965b-c18733dbc8f4?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ]and just  released his 12th travelogue: True North [ https://substack.com/redirect/f8324eb2-de96-4162-a3fe-ef6a8c8aa74e?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ], which takes him back to Canada, to his own roots.
Maria spoke to him at his home in Cape Cod from her temporary perch in Mexico City, on a Zoom call whose themes ranged from places lost in time to the nuts and bolts of writing longhand. Thanks for paying attention. -CJN
From Paul’s desk: “Rwanda 1966 on the way to Kilgali with VS Naipaul who took the photo”
So, Canada. How did this book come about? Had you been thinking about your own ancestry for some time?
I’d been traveling around the country for about six years, with the idea of writing something. My ancestral home is a small town in Quebec — my family emigrated in 1693, from Gascony — so I’d visited a number of times, just to see relatives. Then over time, I went further afield. I was collecting material, but I wasn’t entirely sure for what. Canada’s such a difficult place to write about. It’s very big; the population is very small, about the same as California’s; and nothing happens there.
And, beyond landscape, the provinces all have hugely different cultural identities. British Columbia and Quebec make a convincing case for being two separate counties.
In the book, I say Canada is like an archipelago. There’s Montreal, then you go to the next big place, Toronto; then you keep heading west and the next one is Thunder Bay, maybe? Then Winnipeg, which is like another 400 miles… it’s like traveling in the Pacific from island to island.
So the book had been gestating, but I was waiting for something to happen in my mind that would lend a notion, or a theme, to it. Then it did: the trucker protests. Protest is the most un-Canadian thing. There were some angry people. And then Trump came back into the picture, saying he wanted to make it the 51st state, and Canadians were electrified. They were traumatized, but also unified. The country changed quite quickly, in character and temperament and attitude. And there was my theme.
I’d done most of the initial travel, then I went to Newfoundland; and then drove cross country from Ontario, back and forth — on one trip I did about 7,000 miles. And I was there for the election. That was last spring, 2025.
Did you see Carney coming?
No. Furthermore, I asked Canadians, and a lot of them, neither did they. When Trudeau resigned, everyone assumed the conservative [Pierre Poilievre] would win. In fact they assumed he’d win in a landslide; but he lost.
And all of that, the timing — the election, Trump and how he affected the outcome of it, that sense of galvanizing — is what made the book cohere. I mean, Trump is the gift that keeps on giving. He’s like my publicist (laughs).
Don’t say that too loud; he’ll come for his cut. It feels like there are certain tenets of travel you adhere to: slowness, anonymity, inconvenience. Friction. That’s the exact opposite direction to where a lot of the modern travel world is going. Do you think your way affords you more authority? Does it lead you to a different, truer version of a place?
There’s a story about the Abbasid caliph Harun Al Rashid. He wanted to know what his people were thinking. He didn’t send out a retinue to canvas them; he disguised himself as a commoner, went out among them, and talked to them: what do you think of the price of rice? What do you think of our rulers? It’s an apocryphal story, maybe, but I feel like I am, I aspire to be, an embodiment of that. Look at Michael Palin; he has done various travel programmes, with huge success. He’s there on the ground, he’s talking to people, and it’s reporting, of a sort. But he’s, you know, a massive celebrity.
Years ago, he shot an episode of ‘Around the World in 80 Days’ in my mom and dad’s town in California. He was lovely and friendly with everyone, but it was the very opposite of inconspicuous; there were production assistants on the street to manage the gawkers.
Yeah. He’s famous; I’m just a guy — [laughs] I’m a bespectacled foreigner — talking to people. In parts of Africa I stand out, obviously, because I’m white, I’m a mzungu. In England, if I tell someone my name, they don’t say ‘Oh, I love your book’; they say, ‘Oh, are you related to Louis?’ And when I tell them I am, they say ‘Oh, I just love his shows! What do you do?’ Well, I say, I’ve written a few things. ‘Ah, really? Under your own name?’ Yeah, funnily enough.
But in Canada I was totally anonymous. That is a privileged position. The only way I think you can really write about something, or somewhere, is to talk to strangers about it. When people are speaking to me, it’s as an equal. I’m there at the diner or whatever, having breakfast, you’re next to me having yours, we strike up a conversation. The omelette; the weather; the upcoming election.
Also: I’m not writing anything down. A reporter is there with a pen, asking for clarifications, correct name spellings and such. I’m just talking with them. I have all these techniques for interrupting, asking for directions, ingratiating myself, buttonholing someone. And it sounds easy but it’s not; it can be really hard to talk to strangers, depending on a lot of things. Culture, weather. In Cape Cod, where I am now, if I try to just strike up a chat, they think I’m drunk. Or crazy.
But there are solutions. And it’s the only way I can write a book because what I’m exclusively interested in is the human factor. I want the human architecture of the thing. Not the church or the palace or the museum. You know, we no longer live in an age where tourism matters to travel writing. The only thing that matters is: Who are the people? What’s their historical memory?
Maybe they just arrived, and don’t even know where the hell they are. I met some Somali immigrants in Sault Ste Marie; I spoke to them in Swahili, which they understood from having been in a refugee camp in Kenya for eleven years. I want a house, they said; I want a job. Well, what can they do? They can herd sheep. They’re lost. They’re lost, in a cold foreign country, but living in a motel, which is infinitely better than a refugee camp. And their beds were being made and their toilets cleaned by another refugee, from Colombia, called Alejandra.
When you were reporting Dark Star Safari, you traveled through places which are now categorically off limits — Sudan, parts of the Horn — not just to travelers but in some cases to journalists, aid groups. I once went twice, in the same year I think, to northern Mozambique, to Ilha da Moçambique and Cabo Delgado province. I went all around, to Ibo and Pemba and through the whole Quirimbas archipelago, and it’s still one of the most haunting and magical places I’ve ever been. This was like 14 years ago, and the Rovuma Basin was a recent discovery, and all the speculation was really kicking off; Statoil [now Equinor] and Rosneft and Halliburton, so it was kind of Wild West vibes —
It’s dangerous as hell now.
The ISM [Islamic State Mozambique] is everywhere. And that place that I saw is gone, possibly forever. What does that phenomenon provoke for you, if anything? Nostalgia? Sadness?
When I was in high school I got a passport that read “Not valid for travel to Cuba and Albania.” It actually said that. I’ve never been to Cuba, though now either of us could go there easily; but I did make it to Albania. I travelled along the coast, with a guy I found — he was driving a stolen BMW, I swear; I wrote about this trip in The Pillars of Hercules — and I paid him to drive me from Tirana to Butrint, because I thought, from there I can get a ferry or a fishing boat over to Greece. And it was one of the most spectacular coastlines I’d ever seen. All these pristine Adriatic beaches; and empty, unknown, because it had been a total no-go zone. Now you have Jared and Ivanka circling.
I was in Afghanistan in 1973, I was there again in 1984. Some things from my first impressions haven’t changed: the men and teenage boys all still carry guns, and the women are still in full burka. I wouldn’t go there now if you paid me, even a large amount. But it’s likely that won’t be the case forever; it might not even be the case for very much longer. When I was in South Vietnam in 1973 the idea of going to Hanoi was so totally out of the question. And Hue — Hue was being bombed. Now it’s like one of the premier resort places in Asia.
So the answer to your question is yes, it’s a bit sad. But countries close, and countries open. And countries you don’t expect to ever open to us again, do. I know now that it’s a constant impermanence and evolving, in ways that are very hard to predict, and can be very strange.
But people forget what places were like once. So to make a record of one, at a particular time in history, is important; that’s what a travel book does. It’s an account of a place in a moment in time.
We have a lot of creative and media people in WITI-land, who would love to hear about the mechanics of your craft. What does the day-to-day business of Paul Theroux writing a book look like?
Well, with the travel books it’s very labor-intensive. I don’t travel with any electronics; I’ve been robbed numerous times, so I don’t do that anymore. Just my phone. This [waggles a general-issue yellow note pad at the camera] is how I write. In longhand. I travel with two notebooks, a small one and a big one. The small one is in my pocket all the time, with a Lamy pen, which is German and costs like $15; I have a lot of them. But I never use them when I’m actually talking to people. Writing is somehow intimidating, and will change the way they interact with you.
I wake up and I set off, hoping to meet someone. It might be an actual appointment, or me looking for an impromptu situation. In between I make notes about things that capture my attention; a dog, a restaurant, a snowfall.
As soon as each person’s out of sight and earshot, I immediately write everything down: what the person said, the way they said it, what they look like. I spend all day doing those things: Driving, walking, writing, talking. Around five pm, I’ll start looking for a hotel. It’s very, very seldom that I have actually booked anything. The first thing I do once I’m checked in is to write up everything that happened that day in the big notebook, and date the entry. It’s usually five or six pages, maybe a bit more — it comes out to between 1,200-1500 words. I might do 2,000 on a good day. In other words, I write the day while it’s still very much with me. And when I get to a real town or city, I photocopy the pages I have, and I mail them to my wife.
So analog!
I’ll end a trip with quite a few big notebooks, around half a dozen.
And at what point is Paul being layered in? Your impressions, your reactions?
I try not to put too much of that in, because I’m the observer. I might add a descriptive detail later, here and there.
When it comes time to actually write the book, I’m basically transcribing, with some additions and cuts and amplifications. I work every weekday, and often on the weekend. A book for me is a matter of months, not years. It would be rare if it took more than six months, because I’ve been working already the whole time. When I hear someone say it took them two years to write their travel book, I think, But what were you doing? Because the travel itself can be a lot of work, the reporting and recording throughout every day is a lot of work. But the actual sitting down and writing the book on your computer: it’s right there on the pages, where you wrote it all down. What are you waiting for, a MacArthur Genius Grant?
If you’re a professional writer, you have to be efficient. You have to do it now, or as soon after the fact as you can; you can’t sit around twiddling your thumbs.
And what about magazine or newspaper assignments? What kinds will you accept? Is it only if you find it terrifically intriguing as a place?
Listen, I do not turn down any reasonable offer. I’m going to Prague to do a corporate assignment. A hotel company that has a property there wants me to do something fairly involved and extensive. And as you know, corporate assignments are very well paid. And I don’t have another job, and I have no desire at all to teach creative writing. So I think it will be fun. I’ve never been to Prague. My mindset has always been, I want to earn my living as a writer, and I don’t want to do anything else, so make the writing pay.
I don’t even turn down some unreasonable offers! [Laughs] Like, “Write 500 words on your favourite piece of luggage.” Ages ago I wrote a book called The Kingdom by the Sea, about Britain, and last May the New York Times asked me to write a piece —
I saw it. The new King Charles III walking trails. It was totally charming.
Well, thank you. But I’m trying to write a short story and a novel. I gotta do this thing in Prague… Did I want to do a story like that? Well, yes. I have children and grandchildren in London. I actually like walking, and I like doing it alone, just hiking and meeting people, saying Gee, I’ll have fish and chips. And, you know, there will come a time when I won’t be able to walk, so.
Was it kind of a dream assignment? Yes; but I have things to do!  Look at all the stuff I have to do [gestures to the alarmingly cluttered desk behind him].
Also, I like returning to places.
Excellent, because this was something I wanted to get to: what are those one or two places on the map that have intrigued you sufficiently throughout your life that you have a book about them forming somewhere?
Malawi is lately at the forefront of my mind. My first job in Africa was as a teacher in the bush there, when it was still Nyasaland. That was the 1960s; I’ve been back since, you may remember I wrote about it in Dark Star Safari, I went back to that school, Soche Hills Secondary School. I haven’t visited in some time, maybe 20 years. Because of economic conditions, dictatorship, and pervasive poverty, that country has not succeeded. It’s overpopulated; it was a nation of three million, now it’s like 23 million. It’s unsustainable. It’s landlocked. There are hundreds of thousands of orphans because of how bad AIDS was.
But I would love to go back. I’d love that. To go back for a month or so, and actually teach again, and write about that. It was 60 years ago. I don’t know when, but I’m planning it in my head.
HTSI recently sent the FT’s Patti Waldmeir back to Ghana for the first time since she had her very first job as a foreign correspondent there, 50 years ago. She was gobsmacked by the changes.
Oh, it’s changed enormously. They’ve got medical schools, big universities —
Fancy safari lodges in the north.
I was in Accra in 1966. Ghanaian people are amazing. But I can only imagine how different it is.
I’d also love to go back to Mexico, where you are now. I mean, just to be in Mexico [LAUGHS].
Are you a podcast person?
The only podcasts I listen to are my son Louis’s, and another one that’s called Soundings, which is done by Jamie Brisick, for a magazine called The Surfer’s Journal.
Ah, can we give it up for The Surfer’s Journal? It’s so beautifully art-directed, and so intelligent. I feel like it’s one of the few really masterful print publications left.
It’s really great. And the podcast is too. He gets old surfers on to just tell incredible stories.
Basically I’m a reader, and I read old, paper books. Every summer I choose an author and I read everything they’ve written. Rebecca West; I read all her novels, I read her journalism, her biography. She wrote one of the greatest travel books ever written, it’s called Black Lamb and Grey Falcon, about Yugoslavia. Her novels are not good, but her journalism is beyond brilliant.
This year, it’s Patricia Highsmith. Some of her books are really bad. Like borderline unreadable. Or they start great, and then really go down. I was on a bad one, called A Dog’s Ransom. A lot was wrong with it, but for a writer even that’s interesting. I analyse the badness of it. A bad book is more revealing of a writer’s personality than a good book is. Now I’m on Strangers on a Train. That’s a fantastically good book, fantastically good. The Ripley books are next; I’ll do them in order.
How and where do you get your news?
I read the New York Times. I read the FT now and then. Then online stuff, Huffington, Drudge. I look at headlines; I’m not a news junkie. I try to keep up. The New York Times will sometimes have a story about Canada, and then there are like 100 comments or letters in response to it, people writing from Toronto, Vancouver. So I’ll really read those to see what their take is. Canadians are incredibly current on American politics.
It’s alarming to me that Americans are some of the least literate on American politics among the people I encounter in the world. When I lived in Singapore, I’d have really involved conversations with taxi drivers about the Democratic primaries.
Singaporeans were the best students I ever had. They were so conscientious. I lived there for three years in the late 60s. If Joseph Conrad had gone there then he would have recognized it. There was so much atmosphere! I lived in Bukit Timah, on College Green. We had no air conditioning. Three years, two children, one very discontented wife, no air conditioning. In my novel Saint Jack, I wanted to write about Singapore the way it looked then. Crime, prostitution, beat-up old godowns and noodles —
Now it’s 42-story skyscrapers with hydroponic gardens growing up their sides.
It’s crazy now. When I was there, there was only one of those really tall buildings. Saint Jack was made into a film; Peter Bogdanovich directed and Ben Gazzara was in it. I don’t think it was a great film, but Bogdanovich and Gazzara both loved it, and apparently it’s one of Wes Anderson’s and Tarantino’s favorites. But man, was it hard to get it made. They gave us hell; it was such an authoritarian government. The vice-chancellor of the university I was teaching at — Toh Chin Chye, he was actually deputy prime minister at one point — he told me I should get a haircut. He said I looked like a hippie. I went to the head of my department, an English guy, and told him and he said, ‘Well, it wouldn’t hurt to think about it.’ Get a fucking haircut?? Come on, man.
But I have old colleagues who hated Singapore when we were teaching, who’ve gone on to retire there, which is amusing. It’s like a giant gated community for them. It’s a strange place, you know. There is one thing I really miss.
Chicken Rice?
Close. Laksa. You really can’t get a laksa as good as the ones in Singapore anywhere. Anyway, go watch the movie, because it’s like a documentary of how it was. You saw Singapore evolved; this was something else altogether.
I will.
But read the book first. [Laughs]
