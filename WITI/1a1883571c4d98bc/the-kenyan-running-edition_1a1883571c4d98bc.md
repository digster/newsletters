Pick your hype song, set your sights, and make it happen. Colin here. I am an on-again, off-again runner. Whenever I’m trying to get more Then the muscle memory kicks in. I remember the stuff I was taught by coaches and good runners in the past; Shorten the stride a little, let my feet land closer under me. Use the balls of my feet, relax my shoulders, and the whole working machine gets quieter. Same lungs, same legs, far less energy (and pain). At the elite end, that small adjustment may well be the ballgame, though the researchers are careful to call it a hypothesis. Why is this interesting? Adharanand Finn, who lived in Iten, Kenya (the home of running champions) for six months, Another potential answer is more intriguing. Young runners in Iten train in packs, and through months and years of running with others they learn what different paces feel like, and become masters of the effort behind a specific pace. The faster runner sets the tempo and the slower one practices holding on. Think of the power of spending a season tucked behind someone relaxed and efficient. The cadence, the quiet shoulders and the short contact time get absorbed by osmosis (or survival!) along the way. Biomechanics chatter be damned. The curriculum also includes patience. One Iten coach told Finn that part of the reason for running slowly is so the body, and hence the mind, doesn’t start to dread training. Easy running is how you earn the right to be relaxed when it gets hard. The elite hand down more than technique. With little funding for up-and-coming athletes, successful runners often give their training partners shoes and gear, help with transport, and even cover their food. Which brings me back to my own stride. I have always treated form as a bug to be fixed mid-run, by thinking hard about my shoulders as I go. The Kenyan model suggests form works more like an accent, picked up from whoever you run beside. A better stride may come from spending more time next to people who already have one.
