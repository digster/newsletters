Louis here. When I was young, I remember my father sitting me down in front of a Michael Jackson music video. His reason had little to do with Jackson. 
Instead, he wanted me to pay attention to the drummers around him, all of whom were members of a Brazilian activist drumming collective called Olodum. In keeping with the group’s focus on civil rights and Afro-Brazilian pride, the Spike Lee-directed video was partly filmed in a favela of Rio de Janeiro—something state authorities tried their best to ban, fearing it would damage the prospects of Rio hosting the 2004 Olympics. 
That they managed to make it work makes the video even punchier.  (Throughout it, Jackson is wearing an Olodum T-shirt, which now hangs [ https://substack.com/redirect/161b1139-e4d8-4c61-8917-f575f75d835a?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] in the Smithsonian’s National Museum of African-American History and Culture.)
Not that my dad was focused on all of that, either. Instead, he just wanted me to admire the skills and infectious sound of the group, whose percussion gives the track a battle-like feeling.
Why is this interesting?
The only other time I’ve found percussion to be as powerful in a song is Paul Simon’s The Obvious Child, from 1990. In that song, the drums drive everything from the off, and even have a sense of humour—they cut out emphatically after the sixth verse, come back for a few fake finishes, then finally resume in full alongside some sublime whinnying from Simon through to the end. 
Attend any of Simon’s concerts, as I’ve been lucky to a few times, and you’ll always hear a few enthusiastic people get caught out and clap because they think the song has ended. (One example is below, from about the 3:20 mark.)
Simon’s song came on my shuffle the other day, and I was typically swept away by it, enough to find myself on its Wikipedia. There, in the very first paragraph, I learned that there were some familiar talents behind the song’s percussion: It was Olodum, again.
The story of how it all came together is pretty special. Per this 1990 article in The Baltimore Sun:
Simon recalled that his encounter with Grupo Cultural Olodum was “almost accidental”. He learned that the ensemble would be rehearsing within the city and traveled with some of his friends to hear them play. Upon hearing them, Simon recalled that he was “blown away by the sound” of the ensemble.
A few days after his initial encounter with Grupo Cultural Olodum, Simon brought an eight-track machine from Rio de Janeiro [ https://substack.com/redirect/4bd1e2c2-cbdb-4cdc-b72c-bcf9c7b88067?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] to the streets of Salvador to record the ensemble. He decided to record them in the streets as he felt that it would have been unfeasible to fit all ten members of Grupo Cultural Olodum in a conventional recording studio. Microphones were hung from windows or on telephone poles to capture the performances. According to Simon, “Hundreds of people gathered. It was an amazing day — an amazing recording experience.”
The following year, Simon was still so enamoured with the song that he opened his 1991 Concert in Central Park with it, even inviting Olodum to perform it alongside him [ https://substack.com/redirect/3bb34d88-5e87-4eb8-8178-53416bdcf193?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ].
Decades have passed since then. Simon has stepped back from performing. Jackson has been largely disgraced. But given that its members are constantly cycling in and aging out, Olodum endures. The group frequently plays in the same Pelourinho streets where Jackson’s video was filmed. It did so as recently as last Sunday, and earlier this year: 
And despite the authorities’ fears in the 1990s about the socially-minded group’s effect on Brazil’s public image, when the country hosted the FIFA World Cup in 2014, not only was Olodum invited to play on the official song—they also performed at the opening ceremony. 
Stars, by definition, fade out. But if we’re lucky, some of the supporting casts they lean in can be enjoyed for a little longer. (LC)
