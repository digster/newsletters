David Prior (DP) is a travel writer and runs Prior [ https://substack.com/redirect/7ee2cd1a-eb09-4669-b60a-6ae80d897750?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ]. Check out his excellent podcast here [ https://substack.com/redirect/62ebbbe4-153d-4764-bfc6-e9a06b7ad577?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ]. Happy to have him with us this week. -Colin (CJN) 
Tell us about yourself. 
I run the travel company PRIOR, and I write. I’m Australian, now living between Milan and New York, and for much of the year I’m on the road looking for the people and places that can breathe life into the kind of trips I actually want to build and share.
I started out as a cook. I studied at the University of Gastronomic Sciences, the school founded by Slow Food and Carlo Petrini, and its philosophy of protecting and celebrating biological and cultural diversity is still the thing I measure most ideas against. After that I worked for Alice Waters, which was an education in a different order of ambition. Writing for magazines began as a way to pay for more travel and turned into a career of its own: the NYT, Vogue, the FT and finally Condé Nast Traveler, where I was probably too young for my title but the scope I was given to do big destination stories in the magazine from beginning to end really gave me the idea to start PRIOR. How to make that cover worthy moment come to life, how to give an editors kind of access and create the environment where a magic can happen.
The company mixes all of my interests and experience, hospitality and editorial but in the end it is all about storytelling. Travel really is so much about that.
Describe your media diet.
My daily go-tos are the Sydney Morning Herald, The Guardian and the New York Times. Even if I try to avoid the Murdoch press I regularly fail and end up on their respective competitors too.
I lament the demise of the newspaper food section but I think the monthly COOK, from Corriere della Sera, is the last great one in print. Although a bright spot is Nigella Lawson returning to write again for the Financial Times!
On weekends I read La Repubblica and its excellent D magazine, edited by Emanuele Farneti, and FT’s HTSI.
I listen daily to The Daily from the NYT. The Rest Is Politics, the original, and particularly the Leading series. RN Breakfast on ABC Radio National to find out what is happening in Australia. Hidden Forces if you really want to get into geopolitics. William Dalrymple’s Empire is always fascinating (he is one of my favourite authors, see below).
I am new to podcasting, so I have been curious about shows that go into people’s lives from a particular angle. There is a brilliant one called Second Captains Saturday, which asks iconic, mostly Irish non-sportspeople, people about their “sporting careers”. It is often as moving as it is hilarious. In the same genre, the original and still the best of the conceit is Desert Island Discs. I find that if you get people into their sense memories, the stories and the real insight flow so I really admire shows that get to that very true place.
I am a fan of New York Magazine and I am keen to see what Jody Quon, who was Creative Director there, does at T now that she is Editor in Chief. Mark Guiducci is taking Vanity Fair in a great direction, which is to say back to its Tina Brown legacy (I also always read her gloriously unleashed Substack, Fresh Hell). Puck is in that same juicy vein, so I stay across those voicey editors.
With Cabana, Martina Mondadori created an entire aesthetic category beyond a magazine which when you really think about it is insane. She’s my friend and collaborator. I am so proud of her.
For the travel industry, Skift when there is real news. Monica Mendal’s So There’s This Place does service pieces with real energy and rigour. Alex Postman and Yolanda Edwards have built a great community with YOLO Journal. When Nadine at The Stanza leans into her independence and has a biting take on something it makes me laugh in appreciation. I was the new kid on the block colleague of Maria Shollenbarger and Sophy Roberts at CNT. They are wildly different in their topics and tastes (mine sits somewhere in the middle), but those two rarely miss and I never miss either’s stories.
What’s the last great book you read?
Nine Lives by William Dalrymple, on a reread. Nine people in India, each holding on to a form of devotion that the modern country is tugging at: a Jain nun, a temple dancer, a Sufi, a monk who took up arms. I am writing a book with Artisan at the moment and one of the chapters is on India and the idea of faith. I am headed there in a couple of weeks after several years away. PRIOR began with a trip to India, I find it an inspiring place.
What are you reading now?
I am about to start Profiles in Cowardice by Jacob Weisberg, a macabre twist on JFK’s Profiles in Courage that gives a mini biography to each of the figures who capitulated to the second Trump administration. If I need something explained clearly, cleverly and without condescension, he is who I’d ask. He also has a sense of mischief, and I cannot wait to see how the book lands, especially with the people he has profiled.
What’s your reading strategy when you pick up a print copy of your favourite publication?
I don’t read print the way I used to. The morning with a coffee and the paper was a sacred moment. Now it is just a Saturday ritual, wherever I am: London, New York, Milan or Sydney. I like the familiarity of knowing the rubrics of each publication. I usually go for the lifestyle section first and work back from there.
Who should everyone be reading that they’re not?
Sarah Wilson’s Substack, This Is Precious. Sarah is a true force of nature, a communication savant and totally fearless. As dark as it sounds, her topic is roughly: we’re fucked, the planet and society are collapsing, but there is perhaps a simpler, more connected and purposeful life to be found closer to nature, community and meaningful action. Urgent reading for all.
What is the best non-famous app you love on your phone?
Osterie d’Italia. If you want to find a local and traditional place to eat anywhere in Italy, this is excellent and means you will never waste a meal. I wish there was one for everywhere I go.
Plane or train?
I hate flying. It is the worst part of my job and moreover I think it is both boring or boorish to talk about upgrades, delays, lounges etc etc.
BUT I love trains, and my favourite building in the world may be Milano Centrale. It is a hulking rationalist pile, but it was the first place I arrived when I left Australia twenty years ago to start the life that I have now, and it still feels like the beginning of a journey or perhaps homecoming now that I live part time in Milan. I stand a little straighter walking into that hall. I am not a nostalgic person, and I would like to be a little less relentless about wanting more, but it is the one place I reliably stop and ask how any of this happened. For this reason trains and their stations are romantic to me and I always find myself a little contemplative about the momentum of life.
What is one place everyone should visit?
Varanasi. On the burning ghat you can see a snapshot of the entire human experience.
Tell us the story of a rabbit hole you fell deep into.
In my alone time I live in a rabbit hole. I have a book called Symbols. It is essentially a single volume of alphabetically organised totems. For me it works like a book of spells. I open it at random and a page becomes a little portal into whatever niche subject is sitting there: some tiny fact, a talisman’s meaning, an artwork, a language. I have had my best ideas for trips, experiences and articles through it. Diana Vreeland said something to the effect that you can see everything in clothes, even the coming revolution. Ultimately I’m an editor at my core, and I believe the same of travel.
