The Gulf Game Theory Edition

On Elmore Leonard, game theory, and the dangerous hustle for survival.

Guest Contributor
Sep 22
∙
Guest post
 
READ IN APP
 

Jonny Gannon (JG) is a distinguished fellow at the Middle East Institute and former CIA Case Officer. Check out his excellent Substack here, including an extended version of the fascinating edition you’re about to read.

Jonny here. It’s evening in downtown Abu Dhabi. At Saikindo, a Japanese izakaya crossed with a record bar, vinyls turn.

The main room is dark, cool, and carefully composed. The staff come from Thailand, Serbia, Italy, Indonesia, and several countries in Africa. The customers are almost as international: Emiratis, other Arabs, Europeans, Asians, and a lone American. It’s a modern Gulf city, compressed into one room.

I’m reading Freaky Deaky by Elmore Leonard while I sip an Old Fashioned. The novel takes place in another city, Detroit, and in another time. It is populated by former revolutionaries, a bomb-squad cop, an alcoholic millionaire, an ambitious chauffeur, and several people convinced they are smarter than everyone else.

Yashar Ali describes Leonard’s world this way: “Low-level operators who think they’re smarter than they are. Dry wit, awkward situations, and subtle power games. Realistic dialogue, often with absurd or ironic twists. A world where no one’s a mastermind, but everyone’s hustling.”

That pretty much describes every day of my career at the CIA. It’s not entirely unrecognizable in the Gulf either. This region is sometimes called a chessboard. It’s an attractive metaphor, because it makes geopolitics here look orderly, as if every piece has a defined role. The players see the same board, everyone follows the same rules, and victory means roughly the same thing to every side.

Why is this interesting?

Because the Gulf doesn’t look much like chess today. It looks a little more like an Elmore Leonard novel.

Think of it this way: everyone is in the same room, but each has arrived with a different objective. One man thinks he is negotiating, while another is preparing revenge. Someone believes he is still engaged in diplomacy, while the person across the table has decided they are now in a crime story. And everyone thinks he understands the plot.

In Leonard’s novels, the characters are like this too. The capable ones watch and listen. The dangerous ones nurse grievances. The fools talk too much, try too hard, and assume that everyone else sees the world as they do. The character who keeps his head usually sees the opening first. The one who becomes angry, frightened or overconfident starts forcing the action. Then he makes a mistake.

In Leonard’s world, as in the Gulf, keeping your cool is not a pose. It’s an advantage. It may even be the real contest that’s underway. Not who has the most missiles, money or influence, but who can keep their temperature down while everyone else gets edgy.

Every government here is playing, but they are not playing the same game. The Islamic Republic of Iran is playing for regime survival, vengeance, and the eventual exhaustion of its enemies. It does not need to defeat the United States or Israel conventionally. It needs to endure, impose costs and preserve enough coercive power to ensure that no regional order can be built without taking Tehran into account.

It defines time differently from its adversaries. American administrations come and go. Israeli governments rise and fall. Markets react by the hour. The regime, however, waits, and is prepared to accept economic suffering and human loss on a scale that would be intolerable to many other governments as it does so. (The men with the guns tell everyone else to sacrifice. They generally make sure they still eat.)

Israel is playing prevention. Its leaders view certain Iranian capabilities not as bargaining chips, but as threats that must be destroyed before they fully mature. That produces a different tolerance for risk — and for continuing violence.

Washington says it is playing deterrence: punishing aggression, protecting its forces and allies, and restoring boundaries without being pulled into another generational war. But the United States is also playing for credibility and dominance — in the Middle East, in Asia, in Europe, and before its own domestic audience. Especially before its own domestic audience.

The danger, when you’re playing for credibility, is that it can become detached from the interests you were supposed to protect. Governments start taking risks not because the prize is worth the cost, but because leaders fear that further restraint might start to communicate weakness.

The Gulf states have their own games. I’ve lived and worked all across the Gulf, but the country I’ve spent the most time in is the UAE, where I’m writing this from now. This country is playing for survival, security and optionality. Some in Washington see its dealings with China and Russia and conclude that Abu Dhabi is coldly hedging between East and West — or has already chosen Beijing and is merely stringing America along. Some of these critics genuinely find the mystery that surrounds royal courts to be confounding.

That misses something fundamental about the UAE. Here, relationships are a form of currency. It’s the opposite of being coldly transactional. The UAE favors the American technology stack. And many senior Emiratis lean toward the United States not merely because of hardware or policy, but because of relationships built over decades with American officials, military officers, intelligence professionals and business leaders.

But a relationship does not have to mean subordination. The UAE is not trying to dominate the region. It is trying to survive in it. That requires maintaining strong American ties, doing business with China, managing the Iranian threat across the water, and preserving enough freedom of action to avoid becoming completely dependent on anyone.

It must also protect Abu Dhabi’s economic vision, and the country’s emergence as a center for capital, logistics, technology and artificial intelligence. And it has to maintain confidence among the hundreds of nationalities that live and work here. None of that permits an easy calibration.

What occasionally looks like disloyalty in Washington may look like basic risk management in Abu Dhabi. The UAE is not a proxy waiting for instructions. It is a small, ambitious state in a dangerous neighborhood, buying insurance with strategic foresight rather than impulsivity or blind devotion to any people other than its own.

Saudi Arabia, then, has been playing a different game. The biggest guy on the Arab side of the Gulf, the Kingdom wants survival too. But it appears to be seeking regional primacy: greater control over the Arab political order, protection from encirclement, and recognition as an indispensable power in the Gulf.

Its infrastructure, however, has been struck. Its missile defenses are under pressure. Houthi advances in Yemen have exposed serious vulnerabilities and strengthened Iran’s potential leverage over another strategic waterway. Saudi Arabia has tried talking to nearly everyone, reaching accommodations with former adversaries, maneuvering against regional competitors, and announcing a new defense arrangement in Mecca. None of that prevented the latest Houthi assault. The Kingdom is likely reeling.

This is where something called game theory becomes useful. Game theory is not solely about games. It is about decisions made in the presence of other decision-makers. Every actor chooses, while anticipating how others may respond. Each move changes the incentives surrounding the next move. To have a good chance at predicting outcomes, you need to understand what games the other participants think they’re playing.

The trouble begins when the players value different things, interpret signals differently, or do not even agree about which game they are playing. Washington believes it is practicing deterrence and coercion. Israel believes it is practicing prevention. Iran believes it is pursuing survival, resistance, and revenge. Saudi Arabia wants security and on some level, primacy. Oman wants to preserve its independence and special culture. The UAE wants security without economic suffocation, or dependence on anyone else. Qatar, Bahrain, and Kuwait are also running angles. Russia and China want influence and advantage without assuming ownership of the chaos. One side sends a signal. Another can see preparations for war.

A limited strike intended to restore a boundary becomes evidence that no boundary exists. Restraint meant to create room for diplomacy is interpreted as weakness. An attack designed to hurt an opponent’s economy instead frightens investors and workers in countries that did not choose the war. Each player may be acting rationally, but they’re doing so within a reality the others do not recognize.

Rather than the idealized Nash equilibrium, what we really have here is a strategic trap.

And this is where Leonard becomes more useful than the idea of the chessboard. In Freaky Deaky, the trouble comes from the collision of separate stories. One person wants to recover the meaning of an earlier struggle. Another has reduced the whole thing to money. Someone wants recognition. Someone else wants control. A man who believes he is using another person does not realize he is also being used. Every plan makes sense internally, until it meets everyone else’s plan.

In Leonard’s world, the coolest character is not necessarily the strongest, the loudest or even the most virtuous. He or she is the one who sees the room clearly. They do not mistake anger for strength or movement for progress. They know what they want, but they also understand that everyone else wants something too.

This approach may determine who prevails in the Gulf.

Be cool. Don’t force it. Pay attention. Let’s see what comes next. (JG)
