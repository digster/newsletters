Ricardo Amorim (RA) is the founder of [Spine](https://substack.com/redirect/b06e9a05-35c1-471e-8eb1-de74ee48e40f?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o), a wall-mounted album-art display that connects to your streaming collection and brings music back to the wall. The title of this piece nods to Superman, but for the record: Spider-Man is, and always will be, his guy.
Ricardo here. I’m moving house this summer, and the room I’m most excited about is also, arguably, the most pointless one in the new place. I’ve started calling it the obsolete media corner: records, books, graphic novels, a SNES, and a wall of Blu-rays in an age when I already pay four different companies to stream the same films.
One night, while thinking about how many shelves we’d need, I wondered why I was so invested in a corner full of things I had technically already replaced. The answer came in three parts. I like having less to choose from. I like that a shelf says something about me without my having to say a word. And, most of all, I realized I love the act behind all of it: finding, handling, considering, and choosing the things that actually mean something to me.
Which is a long way of saying that I love to browse.
The strange thing about browsing is, nobody really designed it on purpose, and almost nobody noticed when it went away.
Record crates, video-store aisles, and bookshop tables were all machines for serendipity. What made them work was friction. They were slow, physical, and slightly annoying, and that was the engine. Friction made you slow down. Slowing down made you browse. Browsing made you stumble onto the record two bins over, or fall into conversation with the person behind the counter whose taste was nothing like yours. You went in looking for one thing and came out holding three, two of which you didn’t know existed when you walked in.
The friction was doing the work. Then, technology arrived with the kindest possible offer: let me make this easier.
First came search, which helps you find exactly the thing you’re looking for. Then came the algorithm: You’re busy, let me suggest what you’ll like next. I promise my business goals have nothing to do with it. And, to be fair, it worked. We got infinite choice and instant retrieval. We handed over the slow part without much of a fight. It’s just that somewhere in that trade, the friction disappeared, and so did the happy accidents went with it.
Why is this interesting?
It took us a while to feel the loss, but now, we’re feeling it.
People are paying real money to get browsing back. We’re buying records like there’s no tomorrow, sometimes without even owning a turntable. We’re making pilgrimages to [the last Blockbuster](https://substack.com/redirect/9b8835e2-c7d3-48bc-bd92-e23f25954c72?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o) in Bend, Oregon. We’re digging out old iPods. People are quietly rebuilding private video stores at home, with shelves of physical discs they could theoretically stream somewhere else.
The instinct underneath all of it is the same: make it physical, make it browsable, make it mine. Most people don’t really want the old format back. They want the behavior the format used to carry.
Here’s the thing, though. Buying the friction back wholesale is not the answer, and deep down I don’t think anybody wants it to be. I’m not giving up streaming, and you’re probably not driving to a video store in the rain. The more interesting question is whether we can engineer serendipity back in, while still keeping the convenience.
Some people already are. [Mr B’s Emporium of Reading Delights](https://substack.com/redirect/56c15c48-ced1-45ce-a07a-0cf7c7eea932?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o), a small bookshop in Bath, runs a subscription where you fill in a questionnaire, get assigned an actual human bibliotherapist, and receive a hand-picked book in the post every month, wrapped in brown paper and sealed with wax.
You never leave your home, but you still get the surprise: The author you would never have chosen. The thrill of opening something you didn’t pick for yourself. That is serendipity with the friction removed, and a human curator dropped into its place.
I’ve spent the last year working on the same problem in music. I’m building a wall display called Spine that turns your streaming library back into browsable album art. It keeps the convenience and access of streaming, but restores some of the curation, presence, and conversation that used to come from living with music physically.
It doesn’t replace streaming. It just makes streaming browsable again. Same trick: keep the ease, smuggle the serendipity back in. Our appetite for getting lost didn’t die with our DVD collections, it just needed a new home.
And if the answer was never going to be a mass return to the old ways, then the really hopeful part is what comes next. The interesting move is subtler: people reverse-engineering serendipity into systems that remain convenient. A bookshop here, a wall display there, and presumably a hundred more to come for film, television, art, retail, and everything else that’s been recently flattened in the name of efficiency. (RA)
