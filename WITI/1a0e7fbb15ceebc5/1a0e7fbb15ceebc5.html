The Monday Media Diet with Zaitoon

On Gondwana, PubMed, and Carl Jung

Sep 28
 
READ IN APP
 

Zaitoon writes for Arena Magazine, which is worth your time. Pleased to have her with us this week.

Tell us about yourself.

I am a physician and biotechnologist by training, currently serving as Junior Editor at Arena magazine. I live in the great state of California. Arena is a quarterly print publication championing technology, capitalism and civilization. I like dressing up.

Describe your media diet.

I watch YouTube a lot. For some reason I love watching food videos. Food tourism videos, how restaurants work, learning about different diets across the world. I have no interest in becoming a chef or opening something of my own, it just fascinates me. YouTube also has an excellent selection of movies you can watch for free. I also follow a few beauty influencers and home organization channels, it helps me calm down. I am subscribed to a few topics like neurosurgery and reproductive technologies on PubMed, I get a digest of those topics sent to my email - this is the best way to stay informed on research and development that takes time to bear fruit. For the near horizon, I embarrassingly have a very algorithmic media diet: I get my news and my thoughts informed from X. X also helps a lot with my work at Arena, it’s where I find cool companies, people, ideas to work with. I also follow a lot of meme pages on Instagram, there is just no better way to get a sense of what and where culture is right now, and where it is headed.

What’s the last great book you read?

I recently re-read Louisa May Alcott’s Little Women. I found a withered copy with a few pages nearly torn off and would read it first thing in the morning. The way the sisters in the story talk to each other is very uplifting: they are poor and without protection, there is a war and much uncertainty, yet the sisters are full of hope. They face their troubles with pride and dignity, and do not compromise on their standards or their manners. I love 19th century prose, it isn’t modern and it isn’t dated: long sentences, formal dialogue, an exploration of intense inner lives. If you read in order of Austen, Shelley, Brontë, and then Alcott, you get to talk to women across the transition from the late Georgian and Romantic eras into the Victorian age, from 1811 to 1886.

What are you reading now?

I am reading Edith Hamilton’s Mythology, and For God, Country & Coca-Cola by Mark Pendergast.

What’s your reading strategy when you pick up a print copy of your favorite publication?

My favorite print publication is Arena magazine. I read it beginning to end each quarter when I finally get my hands on what our small team has worked very hard on for the past three months. My reading strategy has always been to have a pen or pencil in hand, follow what you are reading, underline and scribble as you feel fit - reading then ceases to become a passive activity. As an editor, there is always this “we could have used another word here”, or “this could be two sentences instead of one”, but mostly I am just happy to have a physical object that showcases where the last few months have been spent. Print is forever.

Who should everyone be reading that they’re not?

Carl Jung. I recommend his writings, or writings informed by him, to everyone I get close to. Once you read Jung, you start seeing him everywhere: in the stories we tell, in the buildings we erect, in the symbols we regulate.

Jung was a disciple of the better known Sigmund Freud, and Freud considered Jung to be the person who would elevate the field of psychoanalysis to a science with authority. Jung ultimately broke away from Freud to found his own school of analytical psychology, one with an emphasis on the collective human unconscious; the fact that most cultures around the world share basic values, stories, and even Gods - e.g. concepts of love, heroism, power are universal. Jung was the first to seriously try to answer “what can we learn from this?”

So, everyone should read Jung to self-actualize and get to know themselves, and others, better.

What is the best non-famous app you love on your phone?

Beli. I love food and trying new types of foods and restaurants and then love reviewing and ranking them. It is also a closed knit social media I consider safe.

Plane or train?

Definitely train. I love looking out the window from a not-so-fast carriage, this is probably my preferred way of appreciating nature as well.

What is one place everyone should visit?

Ancestral graveyards. Get a sense of who you are and where you come from. I haven’t traveled much in my life. I felt very happy and light in the Pacific Northwest, the soil felt very familiar to me. Not sure if it would have the same effect on everyone else. It feels very calm and eternal up there.

Tell us the story of a rabbit hole you fell deep into.

I’ve recently fallen into rabbit holes about tectonic plates. Dolly Parton’s passing made me very curious about Appalachia. It is a popular meme that the Appalachian mountains are older than Saturn’s rings (whatever that means), but did you know that the same land mass - Gondwana - that created the Appalachian mountain range millions of years ago also created the Himalayan mountain range. One was Gondwana hitting North America, the other was a Gondwanan splinter hitting modern day Tibet - these two incidents were almost 250 million years apart. The tectonic plate collision in Asia is still ongoing, causing Mount Everest to grow a few millimeters each year.

It is both humbling and wondrous to realize that our Earth is an organism constantly evolving and that we only get to be here and love and fight for a very miniscule portion of Her history.
