Colin here. A detail in a recent CNN report [ https://substack.com/redirect/5b3fe133-1dd4-48ff-8681-ba52fb2cbf46?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] caught my eye. Around 15 Ukrainian drone specialists are working with rebels in northern Mali, helping them to fight Russian troops. They train local combatants, support missions, and sit down afterwards to work through what went right and wrong.
The environs are a long way from the battered front in Kyiv. The methods also sound a lot like the OSS.
Why is this interesting?
The Office of Strategic Services was America’s wartime predecessor to the CIA. During the Second World War, it worked with British and French counterparts to send small teams into occupied Europe. Known as the Jedburghs, they helped resistance fighters to get supplies, organize sabotage and work with the advancing Allied armies. Every team had a radio operator. Keeping local fighters connected to the wider effort was part of the job.
Their value was in what they helped other people do. These teams supported a much bigger conventional war, making the Allied armies’ job easier by putting pressure on the enemy behind its lines.
I’m not suggesting that Ukrainian officers are sitting around reading OSS manuals, though I imagine some certainly have. I’m saying that there is a clear resemblance in the approach: small teams, local partners, practical ingenuity, and a willingness to make trouble far beyond the front line.
In Libya, a Ukrainian naval-drone operator told CNN his unit was attacking Russian shipping targets while training local forces. Their logic is clear: Moscow should not be able to wage war in Ukraine while treating its interests elsewhere as out of bounds. 
In Russia, Ukrainian drones struck the warehouses of Wildberries, the “Russian Amazon,” hurting businesses and disrupting commerce far from the front. (Kyiv says the company sells equipment used by Russia’s military; Wildberries denies that, predictably.) Chris P wrote a great WITI about it recently [ https://substack.com/redirect/45a4c578-1c1a-4fbe-b3de-21ecf7f60dfc?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ]. 
People have been killed in these attacks. This is serious business, and there are human consequences. Still, the connection is worth examining. Research published by the Australian Army [ https://substack.com/redirect/97345bca-c143-4447-ac38-9f0f650beda5?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] describes changes in Ukraine’s drone warfare methods happening over weeks, compared with the many years Western militaries often take to develop and buy new equipment. Pilots, engineers and specialized repair crews are all part of that process. It is a tight feedback loop, and being able to change it quickly can matter more than getting it perfect in the first place.
What interests me is how these activities contribute to the wider war effort. Small teams help local forces become more capable. Experience feeds back into better equipment. Intelligence creates opportunities that brute force might not. Creativity under constraint becomes a daily practice that is hardened by battle, and dire consequences. 
The battlefield is bigger than the line on a map, and that is where the OSS comparison becomes useful: Ukraine is looking beyond the opposing army to the networks and infrastructure that help sustain Russian power. It is largely the same playbook, updated for a hyper-tech era. (CJN)
