The Shoe Leather Edition

On LVMH, Intern Pierre, and loose engagement rings.

Guest Contributor
Oct 27
∙
Guest post
 
READ IN APP
 

Alfred Tong (AT) is a London-based writer who has covered luxury for GQ, Esquire and The Sunday Times. He writes Notes by Alfred Tong, where he recently expanded on this same subject.

Alfred here. Vibes. Smells. Feel. Atmosphere. Spilled orange juice on the floor of a big, shiny shop. Something’s off.

There are some things you can only find out if you physically go to the shops. The big brains call it qualitative research. And it can be used to back up your intuition against more conventional data.

Why is this interesting?

This is how ‘Intern Pierre’ bet against LVMH and won. In 2023, Pierre, who runs a small family-office in Europe and writes anonymously as Intern Pierre, shorted the world’s biggest luxury group. “Every fund called me ‘nuts’,” he says.

His first clue about LVMH came at a dinner. His wife’s friends agreed that Tiffany’s premium was no longer worth paying. One said a Tiffany engagement ring would tell her that the guy hadn’t tried, and that he’d also overpaid.

This was the jeweller Arnault had paid almost $16 billion to acquire. At this dinner table, a Tiffany ring had become a sign of not trying very hard. Pierre’s hunch hardened after he observed, out in the real world, that other LVMH brands—Moët, Veuve Clicquot—had become the lazy, or at least the default option.

Pierre says he looks for an “ice cube melting”: a business where “the deterioration has already started” but the numbers haven’t caught up. He listens to his wife and her friends, quizzes former executives and buyers, commissions surveys and visits shops. He doesn’t mistake an anecdote for proof; he looks for the same story in different places.

In July 2025, Goldman Sachs added LVMH to its European conviction list, forecasting 69 per cent upside against 5 per cent downside. The shares rose almost 6 per cent that day. For a short seller, it’s the worst thing that can happen.

I asked whether he wavered. “Lol. I shorted more.” Goldman, he said, “clearly didn’t do much fieldwork”. “I talked to the biggest buyer of these particular products in Europe, and I can tell you they’re not buying this.”

On Rodeo Drive last month, the ground floor of Louis Vuitton’s women’s flagship was moderately busy. The two floors above it were empty. A sales associate told him foot traffic and sales were down almost 30 per cent. At Tiffany, down the road, the customer chair was stained and an engagement ring lay loose in its case.

“Unless my eyes are seeing the wrong thing,” Pierre says, “it’s pretty cut and dry. Either this store’s crowded or it’s not.”

At Dior in Singapore’s Marina Bay Sands this month, nobody greeted him at the door. In the men’s section he found rubbish in a seating area and point-of-sale terminals left out on tables. “Letting me roam freely is amazing for me (and my readers),” he wrote, “and it is generally awful for whatever store I’m allowed to walk through.”

His conviction won out. Since its April 2023 peak, LVMH stock has fallen 57 per cent. Earlier this month, Pierre covered most of his short, for a return of more than 50 per cent.

What does it all mean? That common sense, which often isn’t that common, is still the quickest way to find something out. The trick is having the nerve to trust what you’ve seen with your own eyes. (AT)
