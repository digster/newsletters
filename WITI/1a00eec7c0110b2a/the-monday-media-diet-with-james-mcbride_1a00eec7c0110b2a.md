James McBride is legendary hotelier and a longtime friend of WITI. Very happy to have him with us this week. Follow him on [Instagram](https://substack.com/redirect/cf9537d1-bd5a-4391-a898-dbdf3b76962f?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o). -Colin (CJN)
Tell us about yourself.
I’m a South African born hotelier who calls Portugal home and spends much of the year moving between Europe, Asia, Africa, and the Americas. After nearly four decades in hospitality with Ritz Carlton, Rosewood, and now as CEO and Co Founder of NIHI Hotels, I’ve become fascinated by the intersection of travel, culture, conservation, and entrepreneurship. Today my work is focused on creating extraordinary places in remote destinations while supporting the communities that surround them.
Describe your media diet.
My media diet is intentionally eclectic. Every morning starts with The Economist Espresso app and The Economist’s daily podcast. I then scan Financial Times headlines before checking markets through Moomoo.
Because I spend much of my life crossing time zones, I often begin the day watching NBC Nightly News with Tom Llamas on YouTube. Watching the previous evening’s U.S. news over breakfast in Europe or Asia is an oddly effective way of staying connected to America while living abroad.
For podcasts, I enjoy a mix of business and irreverence. Tim Dillon is wildly entertaining and often surprisingly insightful. I also listen regularly to All In, Lex Fridman, and Business Breakdowns.
On weekends, nothing replaces a proper read of the Financial Times. I also follow [Maria Shollenbarger’s Substack](https://substack.com/redirect/58bf4bfe-0b04-493e-b1f7-0887b077d24d?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o), which consistently delivers thoughtful perspectives on travel, design, and culture.
What’s the last great book you read?
Barry Diller’s Who Knew.
What fascinated me was not the success story but the honesty. Diller writes openly about ambition, insecurity, relationships, power, and reinvention. Having spent part of my career at The Carlyle in New York, where many of the personalities in the book intersected with my own world, I found myself reading not only a memoir but also a firsthand account of an era that shaped modern media, entertainment, and business.
What are you reading now?
Chris McBride’s The White Lions of Timbavati.
It’s actually a re-read. I first read it years ago and recently picked it up again as I’m preparing to celebrate Chris’s 85th birthday with him in Zambia. The story feels different today. What once seemed a fascinating tale about white lions now reads as a deeper meditation on wilderness, conservation, and our connection to the natural world.
What’s your reading strategy when you pick up a print copy of your favorite publication?
I start at the back.
The biggest stories are usually obvious. The most interesting stories are often hidden in the corners. With the Financial Times, I enjoy the features, commentary, and long form journalism as much as the headlines.
Who should everyone be reading that they’re not?
People over 80.
We spend enormous amounts of time listening to people tell us how to get rich, get fit, build companies, and improve our lives. Far fewer people listen to those who have actually lived one. Some of the most valuable lessons I’ve learned have come from conversations with people in their eighties and nineties. They tend to have a clearer understanding of what truly matters and what doesn’t.
What is the best non-famous app you love on your phone?
Flighty.
I spend much of my life crossing oceans and time zones. Flighty turns the chaos of modern air travel into something understandable and has probably saved me hundreds of hours over the years.
Plane or train?
Train.
A plane gets you somewhere. A train lets you arrive.
What is one place everyone should visit?
Mongolia.
I’ve travelled extensively around the world, yet Mongolia remains one of the few places where you can still feel the scale of the planet. Endless horizons, nomadic culture, and a profound sense of freedom make it unlike anywhere else.
Tell us the story of a rabbit hole you fell deep into.
My deepest rabbit hole began with a plane ticket to Sumba.
Chris Burch asked me to visit a remote Indonesian island that I’d never heard of. I assumed it would be a brief reconnaissance mission.
Instead, I disappeared into a rabbit hole that now resembles the Kimberley Diamond Mine.
Fourteen years later, I’ve helped build hotels, foundations, schools, equestrian programmes, wellness retreats, and lifelong friendships on an island that completely changed the trajectory of my life.
The lesson? Be very careful when someone invites you to inspect a remote island. It may consume the next decade and a half.
