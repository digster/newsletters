Colin here. For years, the Park Hyatt on Dubai Creek has been a waypoint for me: sometimes a long-haul stopover, other times a proper weeklong stay. Low, green, and quiet, with dhows still moored along the water, it feels far removed from the bustle and hyper growth of the city. Or perhaps, not removed from Dubai so much as connected to an older version of it.
As the city’s skyline grew with sci-fi forms in every direction, the creek remained its center of gravity. Turns out, that feeling goes deeper than a well-run hotel with good tennis and some lush greenery.
Why is this interesting?
The creek is not only the heart of Dubai, but is also one of the great infrastructure stories of the twentieth century.
I was reminded of this by Sultan Sooud Al Qassemi, the Emirati collector behind the Barjeel Art Foundation, who writes a recurring column for The National unpacking a single work from the collection. His latest selection is The Creek in Old Dubai [ https://substack.com/redirect/3b318d88-b1cd-45b5-9a52-0761e841aa09?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] by Hala Kouatly, painted in 1979—the same year Jebel Ali Port opened down the coast. 
He writes of the painting:
Shades of blue, brown and white dominate the canvas, with dhows lining both banks. In the foreground, traders, sailors and captains converse while others share a meal. Behind them rises the early 20th-century skyline of Bur Dubai, with coral stone, limestone and gypsum buildings suggesting Kouatly painted the scene from the Deira side of the creek.
The story the artwork is telling begins two decades earlier. In 1958, Sheikh Rashid bin Saeed, then Ruler of Dubai, borrowed around a half a million dollars from the Emir of Kuwait to dredge the creek. The loan was facilitated by the British Bank of the Middle East, and repaid in ten equal annual installments.
Dubai had no meaningful oil wealth at the time. What Sheikh Rashid had was a conviction that trade would define the city, and that the creek needed to be deep enough for modern ships. Sheikh Rashid made the bet before the future was visible, and Kouatly painted the old order just before it became recognizable as the past.
The creek project paid off gradually. Banks established headquarters along the water. The British Embassy, the Amiri Diwan, and Sheikh Rashid’s own administrative offices clustered on its banks. Trade expanded. More importantly, the project established a strategy that Dubai would repeat: building capacity before demand materialized.
Jebel Ali Port later opened down the coast, and expressed the same strategic conviction: invest in maritime infrastructure before the need for it is obvious, and let the city grow into it. Today, Jebel Ali sits at the center of a logistics network that reaches around the world. DP World, the Emirati logistics giant that emerged from it, operates terminals across dozens of countries and handles a significant share of global trade.
The even more interesting coincidence, however, is what was happening back on the creek that same year. As the container cranes went up at Jebel Ali, Kouatly stood on the Deira side and recorded the dhows, traders, and captains of Dubai’s original commercial center. She painted a port whose working world brought together Arabic, Urdu, Farsi, and Swahili; a place that still looked complete, even as the city’s center of gravity began moving elsewhere.
She was painting it while it was still fully alive, in the year it began to become the old Dubai.
The towers, the airline, the port empire came later, and get most of the PR. But long before Dubai connected itself to the world by air, the creek connected it to the region by water. 
Sitting at the Park Hyatt, I felt the history ambiently before I knew it. This painting helped everything snap together in my mind. (CJN)
