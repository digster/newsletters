Colin here. Pizza Hut played a weird and outsized role for people of a certain age in certain parts of America. Though now most associated with fast food delivery, back in the day it was actually a gathering place for everything from the post-soccer tournament pizza party to a friend’s birthday. And it was oddly cozy and convivial: There would be tablecloths, wood, lots of larger tables, and of course the iconic lamp, recently called out by the New York Times as a part of American design history [ https://substack.com/redirect/95292af0-cc1e-40d9-80d9-ebed4673cffa?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] and lore, up there with the Costco Hot Dog.
These iconic, old Pizza Huts followed all the established rules of a brand positioning textbook. They had visually distinctive red roofs, tables with white and red checkered tablecloths. I also remember large red cups where you could put every available beverage in the self-serve machine into one drink, as well as the salad bars and actual video game consoles.
Why is this interesting?
Nostalgia is increasingly a lever that marketers use (to powerful effect) when trying to put the jumper cables on in a brand. So, unsurprisingly, Pizza Hut is now keen to evoke some of these memories.
Recently, it’s been rolling out “Pizza Hut Classic” locations around the US. What is most interesting to me is this isn’t really a stunt, but something that cost some CapEx, and also likely required some serious crate digging into the archives. Just check out the entire list here. [ https://substack.com/redirect/f7a9a949-b207-44fa-9d72-396a808fb718?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ]
According to the press release from Yum Brands: “These classic elements represent more than decor, offering ‘comfort, emotional grounding, and a sense of control’ in a family-friendly environment that brings to mind happier, earlier times.”
There are reports, too, that the new locations are actually driving significant foot traffic, with some staff having to be reassigned to meet the demand.
Naturally, these openings are resonating with everyone who lived through the first era of this design, and who loved eating their personal pan pizzas under the gentle glow of those iconic lamps. But predictably, the Gen Z set can also recognize a strong aesthetic and a vibe, and in a semi-ironic twist, it is resonating with them too.
According to Fox: [ https://substack.com/redirect/cd4580f3-2bfa-4ef3-a16f-d6010e5ab45d?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ]
Older generations with fond memories of Pizza Hut aren’t the only ones drawn to the retro restaurants. Sparks said he encountered skepticism early on from those who believed the concept would primarily cater to baby boomers.
“That’s where they were dramatically wrong,” he said. “When we started to get a lot of traction with social media, these weren’t 50- and 60-year-olds coming in and doing TikToks, right? These were [people in their] teens and 20s coming in and doing them.”
It’s an interesting example of a brand leaning into some latent emotional equity, and not just turning it into a campaign, but doubling down with actual spending and immersion. More telling, there is even an aftermarket for the iconic Tiffany lamps.
