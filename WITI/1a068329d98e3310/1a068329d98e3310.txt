Louis here. The 2004 romantic comedy 50 First Dates stars Drew Barrymore as Lucy, an art teacher who was in a car accident the year before, and has been unable to make new memories ever since. Every day of her life now begins as if it was still the morning of the crash, and to spare her from the pain of her condition, her family and friends all just go along with it. 
This method works well enough, until Adam Sandler’s character Henry falls for her. Not content with having to start from scratch every day, he ultimately finds a new solution: Every night he makes a video tape, filling in all of the gaps in her recent memory. It’s the first thing she watches in the morning, and after adjusting to the shock, Lucy is able to enjoy her life in the present.
Why is this interesting?
In an obviously less extreme way, I have always felt that my own days start twice. The first comes as soon I wake up, groggy and typically with racing thoughts following a bizarre dream, or due to some latent anxiety concerning my to-do list for the coming day. 
The second beginning comes about an hour later, once I’ve showered, walked, eaten, and either talked to someone, or listened to a great song or podcast. At this point, I finally feel like myself again: Centered, happy, and no longer prey to my neurochemicals.
Given all of this, I’m forever interested in anything that might help me reduce the length of time between these two checkpoints. And while I hope many of you wake up and feel like yourself almost immediately, I’ve often joked to friends that I wouldn’t mind my own 50 First Dates style video to help me shake off the cobwebs, and remember everything that’s great and good in my world.
For now, I think forcing myself to quickly press play on a great album, or to simply turn on the radio should be progress enough. But if that doesn’t do the trick, I may well be back here in a few months, asking if any of you have recommendations for some simple video editing software. (LC)
