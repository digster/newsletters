Colin here. We covered J.Crew When I was living in New York in the 2010s, there was a great corner in Tribeca that managed to take quite a lot of my money during my walks home. It was where one of the largest clothing companies in America ran one of its best stores in a former liquor store-turned-bar, without ever putting its name on the door. J. Crew kept the watering hole’s original wooden bar and old neon sign. Inside, you’d find decanters, first editions and a stack of LPs. You came in off the street with very little information about what you were walking into, and were met with a small, low ceilinged room containing peak J.Crew’s sharpest thinking. There were Aldens made in exclusive runs, waxed Belstaff and Barbour jackets, Red Wings, and denim you could not easily find elsewhere. It didn’t feel like a store, so much as a worldview condensed into a room that had discretely agreed to sell you a few things. The staff knew their stuff, too. They’d regale you with the differences between Alden lasts, or tell you when and where to have your Belstaff Trialmaster re-waxed. The people on the floor wanted to talk about the objects, and the selection was exactly right for the moment. The backstory is that in 2008, Mickey Drexler wanted the men’s line to mean something beyond the back wall of a conventional J.Crew store. He called his friend Andy Spade, who had recently started Partners & Spade with Anthony Sperduti. Todd Snyder, then running J.Crew menswear, was involved from the beginning. Partners & Spade worked on the design with the in-house team. The store opened that summer, operated until 2019, and was eventually taken over by Snyder for his own label. Why is this interesting? The Liquor Store remains one of the cleanest examples of a large brand compressing itself into something tight and edited. It took the entire J.Crew men’s worldview—the references, fabric obsessions and specific idea of how an American man might want to dress—and reduced it to a bouillon cube. Drexler briefed a big brand to behave like a small one, and also protected the result from the normal forces that might have softened it. Taking the name off the door was the clearest signal. It also made exclusivity feel like discovery, rather than menswear-bro gatekeeping. The small-run collaborations simply were not available elsewhere. Walking into the store gave you information and access at the same time. That is a very different feeling from the tightly-controlled clothing drops and the fro-yo lines we see forming today. It was radical in its own way. Anyone with enough taste and capital can open a boutique. Compressing a 500-store brand into one unmarked room—and trusting the right people to find it—requires a kind of branding bravado that is rare. At its best, a brand is an argument about how the world should look and feel.
