Colin here. LAX has long been a disaster. For one of the US’s largest and most important airports, both arrivals and departures are chaotic.
This YouTube short [ https://substack.com/redirect/d21e97c8-4a89-4488-876d-e2bf09149cce?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] about sums it up.
A long planned Skytrain is coming, maybe. It looks no different than the monorail you might have ridden at Disneyland in the 80s, yet it still hasn’t launched. Despite being pretty plugged into the world of travel and aviation, I haven’t been able to really parse what it will do, or what value it will bring to passengers other than make them schlep further. The comms have been shoddy at best.
So I did some digging. Here’s the actual pitch: The Automated People Mover is a 2.25-mile elevated train with six stations, running from the terminal horseshoe out to two new facilities: a consolidated rental car center that holds 21,000 vehicles, and the LAX/Metro Transit Center, which opened last summer and connects to the K and C lines. Trains are supposed to run every two minutes at peak, 24 hours a day, free.
The theory of value, as far as I can reconstruct it, has three parts. First, it pulls the rental car shuttles and airport hotel buses out of the central terminal loop, which is the single biggest contributor to the horseshoe gridlock. Every Hertz and Marriott bus circling the terminals disappears. Second, it gives Los Angeles something every serious global airport already has: a rail connection. You will, for the first time, be able to ride Metro to LAX without a shuttle bus transfer. Third, it gives people being dropped off or picked up an option to do it away from the curb entirely, at a station with actual capacity.
It’s worth also noting what it does not do. The train is landside only. If you’re connecting between terminals, the airside corridors still beat it, and you’d have to exit security to ride it. And for the majority of travelers who arrive by Uber or private car, the value is indirect: a little less bus traffic in the loop, marginally faster curbs. As LA continues to grow, this might become less useful.
The people mover is really a piece of infrastructure for the rental car crowd and transit riders, wrapped in the language of fixing LAX for everyone.
Why is this interesting?
The train was supposed to open in 2023. The design and build portion was contracted in 2018 at $1.9 billion. After a $400 million settlement package in 2024, that figure now sits around $3.34 billion, which works out to roughly $1.49 billion per mile.
Wow.
Also worth noting, it completely missed the World Cup, its most visible milestone, and as of this month there is still no announced opening date. Empty trains run training laps on the guideway while the stations sit fenced off.
An L.A. County grand jury report from last year found that delays and overruns stemmed less from engineering than from a dysfunctional relationship between Los Angeles World Airports (LAWA) and its contractor, an inadequate dispute resolution process, and, notably, the contractor leveraging political pressure around the 2028 Olympics deadline to extract more money from the city. The deadline meant to discipline the project became the mechanism for inflating it. City Council has now formally demanded a full accounting from LAWA on why a project that was 96 percent complete in 2024 still isn’t carrying a single passenger.
The lesson? When a public agency signs a decades-long design-build-operate contract and then announces an immovable deadline to the world, it hands its counterparty all the leverage. Every megaproject tied to an Olympics or a World Cup should study this case study of failure and cost overruns.
The train will eventually open, and travelers will shrug and ride it. But the real cost of LAX’s fix wasn’t the building of a fairly simple piece of transport, it was a bad negotiating position. (CJN)
