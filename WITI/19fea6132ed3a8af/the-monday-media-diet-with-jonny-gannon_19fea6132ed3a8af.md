Jonny Gannon is a distinguished fellow at the Middle East Institute and former CIA Case Officer. Check out his [excellent Substack here](https://substack.com/redirect/9f148719-4f0f-4aea-a2a0-ef4b446e4db4?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o). Support the Nimsdai Foundation [here](https://substack.com/redirect/09c424ad-45c3-4d7f-90b3-cb928f2b2692?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o). Pleased to have him with us this week. -Colin (CJN)
Tell us about yourself.
I’m an adventurer. I travel for both work and pleasure. Sometimes to conventional places, sometimes off the grid. I like comfort like anyone else and will take it when it comes, but I actively seek out places and people that bring me to the edge of who I am. Anthony Bourdain once described himself as an advocate for getting up and moving. That resonates with me, though I’d add one more ingredient: doses of discomfort. I like placing or finding myself in situations that demand my full attention — whether a yurt in Mongolia, a sugar plantation in Zanzibar, a race through the mountains of West Virginia, or a high-stakes conversation in a country most people only know from the headlines.
Professionally, I work with companies in private intelligence and national security technology. I’m also a Distinguished Fellow at the Middle East Institute in Washington, D.C., and serve as a Charity Ambassador supporting mountain communities in Nepal.
Before that, I spent twenty-six years at CIA as a case officer and senior executive. Recruiting and handling sources was a core part of my career, but I also worked on covert action, liaison with allied intelligence services, hostage negotiations, diplomatic back channels (shadow diplomacy), and coordinating activities across agencies in Washington. Some people in media use the word “operative,” and that may capture the essence of the job better. But the official designation is case officer. The key thing is this: those experiences taught me early on that trust and credibility are often more powerful than authority or titles.
I write on [Substack](https://substack.com/redirect/9f148719-4f0f-4aea-a2a0-ef4b446e4db4?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o) and occasionally speak publicly.
Most importantly, though, I’m a husband and a father. Everything else comes after that.
Describe your media diet.
Most mornings begin with a customized AI briefing I’ve built in ChatGPT. It pulls together headlines and analysis from around the world, with particular attention to geopolitical hotspots and both English- and foreign-language reporting. Rather than replacing journalism, this helps me decide where I want to dig deeper.
After that, I usually check the BBC, along with a handful of regional publications depending on what’s happening that day. When I have time, I also enjoy reading long-form reporting and essays. I’m less interested in breaking news than in trying to understand why events are unfolding the way they are and how they impact people on the ground.
What’s the last great book you read?
The Hour of the Predator by Giuliano da Empoli. It’s one of the sharpest books I’ve read on how global politics is changing — while the nature of how power is accrued, maintained, and lost isn’t really changing. It’s superbly written. Enlightening, disturbing, farcical.
What are you reading now?
Two books: Martha Gellhorn’s The Face of War alongside Alex Finley’s Victor in the Rubble. Gellhorn, I think, reminds us that wars are ultimately about ordinary people caught in extraordinary circumstances. And they’re often run by people who don’t have to live with the consequences. Her reporting was at times unsentimental, at others deeply compassionate, and ultimately timeless. Finley, meanwhile, writes thrillers in which intelligence officers and terrorists grapple with bureaucracy as much as each other, and where our side sometimes succeeds despite itself. Reading the two together is an interesting contrast between exceptional journalism in dire circumstances and satire that hits awfully close to home.
What’s your reading strategy when you pick up a print copy of your favorite publication?
I scan the headlines first, looking for something that tells me what my morning briefing didn’t. If I already know the basic facts, I’m looking for context, original reporting, or an angle that changes how I think about the story.
Who should everyone be reading that they’re not?
I’d encourage people to revisit Nassim Nicholas Taleb’s Antifragile. We spend a lot of time talking about resilience, but resilience simply returns us to where we started. Antifragility asks the better question: how do we become stronger because of stress and volatility?
I’d also recommend anything by Stephen Walt, particularly his work on defensive realism. Whether or not you ultimately agree with him, he’s one of the clearest strategic thinkers writing today, and he challenges many assumptions that people take for granted.
For those interested in foreign policy but with a taste for fiction, I recommend The Ugly American. It was published in 1958 by William J. Lederer and Eugene Burdick. Ironically, the “Ugly American” in the novel is not the arrogant, culturally tone-deaf official the phrase has come to mean, but a man named Homer Atkins — a practical engineer who earns trust by living among local people, understanding their needs, and solving problems with humility rather than bureaucracy. The book’s enduring message is that lasting influence comes from building durable relationships and developing local knowledge.
What is the best non-famous app you love on your phone?
Insight Timer. It’s a meditation app.
Travel can be exhilarating, but it can also be exhausting. Spending fifteen or twenty quiet minutes each day meditating is like fluoride and floss for the mind. It helps me stay grounded, wherever I am in the world.
Plane or train?
Train.
For short trips, that is. Give me a quiet car, a good book, and a window seat over an airport lounge any day. The food is usually terrible, but I’ll generally make that trade for watching the countryside roll by. Once the itinerary stretches beyond five or six hours, I’ll take a business-class flight.
And, frankly, I’m on planes far more often than trains these days.
What is one place everyone should visit?
For anyone who is physically able, I’d say Tiger’s Nest Monastery in Bhutan.
The hike is demanding enough that you have to earn the view. When the 334-year-old monastery finally comes into sight, seemingly suspended from the side of a cliff, it’s one of those rare moments when photographs don’t quite capture the experience. That’s saying something, because it’s the first pinned image on my Instagram.
For a destination that’s accessible to almost everyone, I’d choose Sukhothai in Thailand. It’s quieter than many of the country’s better-known sites. You feel like you can breathe history. Rent a bicycle, spend a day wandering among the ancient temples, and you’ll leave with a much deeper appreciation for Thailand than if you had only visited the beaches and five star hotels.
Tell us the story of a rabbit hole you fell deep into.
Several years ago, I started wondering why some countries seem to accomplish extraordinary things through personal relationships while others rely almost entirely on institutions. Ours is a country that favors institutions in government. That has clear upsides, particularly transparency and accountability. But it can also make our engagement with the world seem transactional.
That question — why we try to institutionalize relationships in almost every context — sent me down a rabbit hole that I’m still exploring today.
It led me to write a [Substack essay that drew from ](https://substack.com/redirect/a74e321a-f0e5-4f35-8379-dd5b7313e6e4?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o)[The Godfather](https://substack.com/redirect/a74e321a-f0e5-4f35-8379-dd5b7313e6e4?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o). And to reading diplomatic history, anthropology, sociology, network theory, and eventually to conversations with people here and abroad. Along the way, I realized that trust isn’t simply a virtue. In many parts of the world, it’s infrastructure. It’s the operating system. It’s how business gets done, how diplomacy succeeds, and sometimes how conflicts are prevented.
Once you start looking at the world through that lens, you see it everywhere. And once you see it, it’s hard to unsee. It gives you a hint as to why American officials are often admired, but not always trusted.
