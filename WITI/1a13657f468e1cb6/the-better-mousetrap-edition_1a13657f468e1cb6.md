Colin here. Patrick Collison recently posted one of his [long riffs on X](https://substack.com/redirect/396d6091-5e86-4808-a78b-1ed2d4817231?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o). This one was about what could happen to the economy once personal AI agents handle a lot of our buying. The whole thing is worth reading, but one paragraph deserves an edition all of its own:
In corporate strategy, people have long debated the relative importance of distribution and product quality. Perhaps you’ve made something better, but does anyone know about it? Won’t people just continue to buy the ACME Corp Mousetraps? Personal agents will, I think, act as a kind of structural subsidy for product quality. How would markets look different if individuals always spent at least 10 hours researching their purchases? On the margin, focusing on making a product better is, I think, going to become a more effective strategy for companies. This is good!
The mousetrap is a nod to the old line, “Build a better mousetrap, and the world will beat a path to your door.” But anyone who has worked in strategy or marketing knows the world mostly doesn’t bother. The cold reality is it still walks to whatever sits at eye level at Home Depot, ranks first on Amazon, or has the biggest media budget behind it. For most of modern commercial history, distribution has won that argument much more often than quality.
Why is this interesting?
For most consumers, brand has functioned as a research shortcut. Nobody generally has ten hours to hone in on the greatest mousetrap, mattress or dishwasher, so we outsource the decision to familiarity: the name we’ve heard, the logo we trust, the thing our parents bought. “Nobody ever got fired for buying IBM” is perfect shorthand. A meaningful share of marketing spend is effectively rent paid on that shortcut.
Patrick also flags a related, but different category: business models that quietly depend on inattention. This is the subscription nobody remembers to cancel, the coupon only some people bother to clip from a long receipt, or the loyalty points that expire unused, which the loyalty world calls “breakage.”
An agent that never forgets about that thing is bad news for all of them.
We recently saw this in the market, with some stocks exposed to the Cambrian explosion of personal AI agents like Instinct. These agents read the reviews, the return data, the teardown videos and the kind of deep cut Reddit thread where someone tested twelve mousetraps in their barn. And this research doesn’t take AI agents ten hours, but instead a matter of seconds.
If he’s right, this idea of the rent on familiarity falls, and the return on actually making the better thing rises.
There are many categories where buyers currently put in the ten hours voluntarily: Handmade leather goods, Japanese denim, hi-fi systems, rare varieties of sake, independent watchmakers. A two-person amplifier company can build a serious following (likely including many of us WITI types) with almost no advertising.
These categories have forums, obsessive reviewers and collectors acting as a distributed quality control. But now, agents could extend that setup to categories nobody has ever been enthusiastic about. We’re talking insurance, dental floss and HVAC contractors.
Travel is one place I expect it to show up quickly. Hotel choice today leans heavily on distribution: loyalty programs, OTA placement, whoever paid for the top of the search page. An agent working only for the traveler, with a memory of what that person actually values, will weigh points only as far as the math justifies them. The independent property with the excellent breakfast and the genuinely well furnished rooms gets a lift it has never had.
There are two caveats, and the first is the corollary Patrick draws in another of his bullets. If agents reward quality, information about quality becomes the scarce input, and wherever value concentrates, people try to manufacture it. SEO and online reviews both went this way, and a cottage industry around “generative engine optimization,” which is mostly marketing to machines, is already forming.
Signals that are expensive to fake—return rates, warranty claims, repeat purchase and longevity data—should matter more. The five-star review should matter less.
The second is that the word “personal” in “personal agent” carries a lot of weight here. The subsidy only flows toward quality if the agent works for you. An agent built by a retailer or a booking platform has its own built-in incentives, and that sponsored slot I alluded to earlier would simply relocate inside it. In that world, distribution moves from paying to be on the most visible shelves in the store to paying to be the AI assistant’s default recommendation.
For brand people, the takeaway is uncomfortable, and mostly healthy. The power of brands that served mainly as shortcuts for tired consumers is threatened. But the meaningful things still matter: Taste, a brand’s point of view, the intangibles that make someone want one thing over its functional equivalent.
An agent can tell you which mousetrap works best. But it has much less to say about why you want a particular watch, and that will probably stay the human’s job. (CJN)
