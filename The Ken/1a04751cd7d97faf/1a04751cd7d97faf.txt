The SIP boom has a floor, and small investors just fell through it. A record time for SIPs, and a data point industry’s own reports don’t explain. Here is what may be going on 
 By Tanim Mozumder https://the-ken.com/writers/tanimthe-ken-com/

For the first time in years, the number of SIP accounts contributing Rs 1,000 or less a month actually fell https://www.business-standard.com/markets/news/small-ticket-sips-see-first-decline-after-years-of-rapid-growth-126081901181_1.html in FY26, down 1.4 million according to Sebi data, even as every bracket above it kept growing, and grew faster the bigger the ticket size got. 

The overall SIP stoppage ratio actually improved over the same stretch. So, more than SIPs slowing down, this one is a story about one specific group leaving while everyone with more money to spare puts in more of it.

It is a weird thing to happen in the middle of a boom. Monthly contributions hovered around Rs 32,000 crore in July, and SIP assets under management have already quadrupled in five years to about Rs 15 lakh crore. 

It also happens to be the same week Amfi and Crisil put out a joint factbook crediting SIPs with most of what’s gone right in Indian retail investing lately, a more diversified investor base, deeper reach beyond the metros, holding periods that keep stretching out. Some of that is genuinely true. The share of SIP assets held for more than five years has climbed from 12% in 2021 to 31% now, and among retail investors, SIPs have grown from 34% of mutual fund AUM to 45% over the same period. 

A report funded by the mutual fund industry’s own trade body has an obvious reason to lead with such numbers, especially with the Nifty still below its September 2024 peak and the “SIP sahi hai” pitch needing all the goodwill it can get. Still, worth going back to a few things this particular week’s report leaves out.

Start with who actually sticks around. Direct plans, the ones with no distributor and no commission attached, make up about 30% of SIP AUM in the Rs 500–1,000 band, compared with roughly a quarter across the bigger bands. Regular plans dominate everywhere, and they dominate more as the ticket size grows: 77% at Rs 1,000–5,000, 78% above Rs 10,000. Regular-plan SIPs also have a longer track record to point to, 34% held for more than five years against 20% for direct plans. 

The industry’s own numbers are making an argument that a distributor, even one taking a commission, tends to keep people invested longer than an app does on its own.

Jio Blackrock found that out the expensive way https://the-ken.com/story/jio-blackrock-and-zerodha-took-the-same-path-only-one-stayed-on-it/?utm_source=email&utm_medium=newslettter&utm_campaign=collection. It launched as India’s first fully direct-only fund house, no distributors anywhere in the chain, on the simple pitch that lower costs mean better returns. Within a year it was selling through distributors too, after the direct-only model didn’t hold up on its own. If a joint venture backed by Mukesh Ambani and Blackrock couldn’t make that work without help, a Rs 500 SIP opened on a discount app, with nobody checking in on it, is a much harder bet.

Value Research’s Dhirendra Kumar reads https://www.livemint.com/money/personal-finance/mutual-fund-sip-investors-small-savers-decline/amp-11787463295098.html the small-ticket dip as partly people graduating into bigger contributions as their income rises, not pure exit. But he is also clear that real attrition is happening, and it is concentrated in a specific kind of investor: someone who started a small, goal-less SIP during the 2023–24 bull run, exactly the kind that is easiest to walk away from once returns go flat. 

Which tracks, because a good chunk of the SIP pitch has always been mythology. A senior mutual fund executive told Anand Kalyanaraman flatly last year that “SIPs are a problem”. Around the same time, researcher Rajan Raju tested the SIP-as-universal-answer claim directly https://the-ken.com/kaching/are-sips-always-right-nah-says-a-new-study/?utm_source=email&utm_medium=newslettter&utm_campaign=collection and found the industry’s own messaging “oversimplifies complex trade-offs and may contribute to unrealistic expectation formation”. Kotak Institutional Equities’ Sanjeev Prasad has flagged a related symptom https://the-ken.com/story/kotaks-sanjeev-prasad-debunks-mutual-fund-hype-40-of-retail-flows-since-2021-yielded-zero-returns/?utm_source=email&utm_medium=newslettter&utm_campaign=collection: 40% of all retail inflows into the market since 2021 had, until the same time last year, yielded zero returns.

There is also the matter of what people are actually being sold. Equity schemes took Rs 2.9 lakh crore of the Rs 3.4 lakh crore that flowed through SIPs in FY26, and sectoral and thematic funds, among the riskiest categories on the shelf, now account for 8.7% of SIP AUM, up from 5.8% five years ago. A fund tied to millets or green energy can be genuinely appealing because of the idea behind it https://the-ken.com/kaching/should-you-sip-for-the-millet-or-for-the-money/?utm_source=email&utm_medium=newslettter&utm_campaign=collection. 

The cause and the investment are two separate things, though, and a good enough story makes that difference easy to stop noticing.

Then there is a cost that has nothing to do with returns. Bounced SIP transactions are rising, and banks profit from every single one https://the-ken.com/newsletter/make-india-competitive-again/your-missed-sip-could-be-making-banks-tens-of-crores-every-month/?utm_source=email&utm_medium=newslettter&utm_campaign=collection. A missed Rs 5,000 payment can turn into a Rs 5,500 debit once charges are added, and unless someone checks every line of a statement against what they expect to pay, that gap slides right past them. For someone putting in Rs 500 or Rs 1,000 a month, that’s not a small leak.

The Amfi-Crisil report is, understandably, focused on the other side of the ledger, more investors, more money, more cities, longer holding periods, and most of that is genuinely real. But that is exactly why the 1.4 million missing small-ticket accounts deserve attention. The SIP boom has clearly brought more Indians into investing. 

What happens to the ones who came in with the smallest SIPs, and whether anyone in the industry actually knows why some of them are leaving, is a different question.
