The prompt engineer is now a syntax guy, my friend Aman declared over coffee last week, splitting a croissant like it was his company’s AI budget. 

He was echoing what I’d spotted on Reddit threads and AI-enthusiast groups lately. In some organisations, prompts seem to be getting shorter—no adjectives, more abbreviations, and keeping only what’s strictly necessary. In others, prompt writers are deciding what context actually matters and structuring it clearly.

Companies are also optimising elsewhere, by caching previous computations, routing questions to different models based on their difficulty level—anything that reduces token spend. 

Behind this sits a much larger shift. The instinct has moved from “how would this be better with AI” to “that’s way too much AI for this budget.” Like every technology before it, AI has entered the midlife crisis of optimisation.

The person making the tech calls is no longer the engineer writing the prompt or the CTO lobbying the board for the newest model. It is the CFO reading the invoice.

The invoice itself is part of the problem. AI is sold in one unit and used in another. Companies spend in tokens—which until now was a number nobody outside engineering has ever cared about—but businesses measure tasks, like a sales call, an insurance claim, a support ticket, a contract review. 

That gap is exactly what tokenomics is trying to close.
