I rarely use X these days, but if you do, the hottest tech debate of the previous week was kicked off by Josh Miller, co-founder and CEO of The Browser Company. This is the company that previously made the Arc browser and now has a new AI-first browser called Dia. 

Anyway, Miller raised an interesting question, which I’ll summarise in one line: Why is nobody outside tech using AI agents?

It’s generally fashionable for AI-pilled founders, especially on platforms like X, to contort themselves with edgy questions or edgier answers to boost engagement. But I found this one to be a bit different. Miller seems genuinely perplexed why AI agents haven’t seen the consumer take-off that we’ve seen on the enterprise side (especially among coders). This is coming from an ex-Facebook product manager, who used to be head of product for the White House at the end of the Obama administration. So I think he knows a thing or two about how the general public perceives and adopts technology. 

If this was old X (aka Twitter), I could have quoted him in full, but not anymore, so here’s the key excerpt: 

“…if you can figure out the answer to “why” most people don’t care about AI agents yet (and have no enduring interest in using them)—especially since the models and harnesses are here and ready—the answer to that question will allow you to capture a lot of marketshare and make a lot of money in 2027.

Theoretically, the tech is ready for AI Agents to totally transform how we work and live our lives…but alas the general public dgaf…that’s the generational puzzle to solve for the next 12 months for anyone not working on the models themselves.”

Agents are real. They are powerful. The tech is there. People at companies are adopting them feverishly. And yet, if you look around, there’s…nothing. Among consumers, agents do not have adoption anywhere close to, say, Facebook, Airbnb, Uber, Instagram, or Tiktok. People have neither adopted agents, nor have any breakthrough startups really created agent-first consumer products. 

In the famous words of BR, the tobacco boss from Thank You for Smoking (one of our favourite movies), “We don’t sell Tic Tacs for Christ’s sake. We sell cigarettes. And they’re cool and available and addictive. The job is almost done for us!”

Agents are cool, available, and addictive. 

So why haven’t AI agents had their ChatGPT moment yet?

As I write this, Miller’s question has around 3.2 million views, and founders, leaders, influencers, and grifters (on X, these are often the same person) are all offering theories about why it hasn’t happened. I read through most of them, paying close attention to the ones from people I trust and whose views I respect, and found a pattern. 

Sidu Ponnappa, co-founder and CEO of Realfast, an AI-native service company, argued that the reason why AI agents haven’t seen widespread adoption is because they promise productivity, but demand a ridiculous amount of effort to get them up and running. And he argues, “Products that demand increased cognitive load rarely if ever take off in the mainstream.” 

Paras Chopra, founder of Lossfunk, India’s AI-research lab, and ex-CEO of Wingify, has a similar point of view, but from the other side. He wrote, “Most people don’t use AI agents daily because delegation (to another human) is a learned skill. Also, using an AI agent requires skills similar to managing a junior employee, and most of the world simply isn’t ready or equipped to do so. Using AI agents is actually a lot like doing natural language programming: specifying requirements, verifying outputs, guiding the flow, giving context.” Later in the post, he says, “In personal life, for most tasks, the benefits really don’t outweigh the costs.”

What makes these two takes interesting—beyond the fact that both Sidu and Paras were guests on the Zero Shot podcast and panelists at The Ken‘s AI events—is that both of them are describing the same thing from opposite ends: the capability is here, but the packaging asks too much of the person using it.

If you follow that chain of reasoning (hah!), then the obvious question is, who absorbs this complexity? If the barrier to diffusion and adoption is that “the work of specifying is too complex”, well, someone has to take it on. 

Enter Aaron Levie, CEO of enterprise-cloud company Box, with his theory. 

Levie’s argument is that working with an agent is closer to managing someone through a process than asking a question, and the payoff only arrives once you re-engineer your entire workflow around it, which often takes years. This is also why he thinks the tokens and the value sit in enterprises, and why consumers will encounter agents wrapped inside end-to-end services. Stuff like faster insurance, quicker bank onboarding, with nobody thinking about the AI underneath.

“This means getting the agents the right data to work with, crossing organisational boundaries, and evolving the human-in-the-loop steps for when people actually review the work. All of this has to change about today’s processes for the big upside to occur,” he writes.

So essentially, the bottleneck for AI adoption has moved from the supply side to the demand side, and the problem has to be solved there. 

And this is much more complicated and interesting. 

I think this debate assumes that adoption looks the same everywhere, but it really doesn’t. 

Which brings me to India. 

There’s been a lot of lazy (and frankly uberpatriotic slop) written for years about India being a different kind of consumer market. But the evidence suggests it’s true. Tech products diffuse and adopt in unique, strange ways in India. This is a country that skipped desktops and went straight to Rs 10,000 phones wired together on the world’s cheapest data. Then it skipped cards for UPI, and today a large share of its commerce is through Whatsapp threads and voice notes. 

This leads to strange outcomes like Kunal Shah, former CEO of Cred, becoming the global head of Whatsapp, because even Silicon Valley has thrown up its hands and recognised that India is actually a bit unusual, and the usual rules don’t apply. 

But forget Kunal Shah, and just look at e-commerce platform Meesho instead. 

A few months back, Meesho launched a product called Vaani, an AI-powered voice shopping assistant. Now Meesho is a product for India’s mass market of 500 million internet users. The bet that the company made was that shopping in India is essentially conversational because people go to stores and describe what they want, ask questions, and check options. A web interface with filters and grids doesn’t do that well, and voice is the best and most natural medium for that to happen. And now, with AI, this kind of experience may be the most obvious one that gets Meesho to mass adoption. 

It’s too early to say anything definitive, even though the company claims that users who use Vaani to transact have higher retention rates, lower returns, and lower cancellations. 

But my larger point is that this is probably the kind of end-to-end service—where an agent listens, understands, clarifies, and transacts for you—that Levie was talking about. 

I suspect this will look very different for India, and dare I say, India will be where this will happen first. It already has all the ingredients of a consumer agent-AI company to emerge. Voice is a natural mode of interaction, payments are already digitised and as seamless as they can be, and e-commerce has diffused in a massive way across the length and breadth of the country. 

The next skip is waiting to happen.

Also, India is the market where delegation to other humans (which is the point that Paras makes) already happens in a way that’s commonplace. There are actual human agents who do things on behalf of others, even for mundane things such as booking train tickets, delivering products, or managing money. There’s no market other than India where this kind of intricate delegation system already exists. 

But what is missing is the actual consumer side of the story. 

Which jobs would people hand over tomorrow if something trustworthy showed up to take them? 

What are the parts of the week they dread, or carry around in their heads, or keep putting off? 

And what are the strange, elaborate, and somewhat embarrassing systems they’ve already rigged up to manage it, like the voice notes they routinely send to their landlords after the rent is paid, the screenshots of payments they forward to others, the Whatsapp group with one member, or the three people it takes to get one packet of jeera into a kitchen?

Nobody knows what it looks like.

And that’s what we are going to build. 

We’re calling this project The Great Rewiring. 

The Great Rewiring is what happens when agents start becoming common in the Indian consumer’s life. And then, every business in India built on owning your attention, your habit, or your indecision will discover that an agent has none of the three. This means that power will shift towards new kinds of companies and drain from the old, leading to the biggest reshuffle of Indian business in a generation.

What nobody knows is how it’ll play out, from the beginning to the end. 

We will ask thousands of its subscribers, India’s sharpest consumers and operators, what they hate doing, keep postponing, or wish would just handle itself. From those signals, we’ll derive the opportunity spaces worth attacking. Then thousands of student teams across India will do what no report can: they’ll assemble real solutions on real infrastructure, combining agentic payments and authorisation, voice and logistics. 

They’ll find where India’s agentic infrastructure bends, where it breaks, and exactly what must be built next. 

Oh and the best teams defend their solutions in a live adversarial finale, and the best solutions get Rs 20 lakh in cash prizes. 

Finally, everything we learn feeds into India’s agentic map: the first definitive account of how AI agents will remake India, co-authored by those who’ll build that future, tested on the rails that will carry it.

It’s all exciting, and I can’t wait to see how it plays out.

But the first step of the Great Rewiring begins with you, with consumers across India naming what actually annoys them and why.

Because the future won’t rewire itself around your life, unless you tell it what to fix first.

What you tell us becomes the problem statement, because over the next few weeks, India’s sharpest student teams will compete to build AI assistants for annoyances like the ones you face, and you’ll get first go at testing what they make and telling them where it falls short.

It’s a short, fun, survey that’ll take you just five minutes. Perfect for a weekend, and dare I say, it’s not your usual kind of survey—we’ve tried to keep it interesting, and as you go, you’ll see what other subscribers confessed: some of it quirky, some eerily familiar.

Be a part of India’s Great Rewiring. 

It’ll be fun.
