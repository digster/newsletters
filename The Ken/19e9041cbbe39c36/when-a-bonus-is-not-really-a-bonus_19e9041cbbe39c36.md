Good Morning Ishan,
It’s ‘bonus time’ on Dalal Street.
Yesterday, on 3 June, shareholders of Life Insurance Corporation of India (LIC) received 1 bonus share for every share they already held. A 1:1 bonus in stock market parlance. Over the next few days, shareholders of Anand Rathi Wealth will also get 1 share for every share they hold, and those of Trent will receive 1 share for every 2 shares. In 2024, Reliance Industries announced India’s largest bonus issue—a 1:1 distribution of 67.6 crore bonus shares.
Bonus issues, in general, have a celebratory air around them. Companies frame them as a form of largesse—a reward to shareholders. Reliance Industries (RIL) had cast its bonus shares as “[an early Diwali gift](https://www.ril.com/sites/default/files/2024-09/Media-Release-RIL-Board-Approves-1-1-Bonus-Issue-Largest-Bonus-Issue-In-Indian-Equity-Markets-06022023.pdf) to shareholders in RIL’s all-round value creation tradition”. Touching.
Happy words like “bonanza” and “[reward](https://economictimes.indiatimes.com/markets/stocks/news/lic-11-bonus-bonanza-is-today-the-last-day-to-buy-shares-for-free-rewards/articleshow/131320421.cms?from=mdr)” are doing the rounds in the media for the LIC and [Trent](https://www.cnbctv18.com/market/trent-bonus-share-alert-nearly-5-lakh-retail-shareholders-of-this-tata-group-stock-to-be-rewarded-for-first-time-ws-el-19889328.htm) issues. You see, ‘bonus’ conjures images of free, extra, or more. Many rush in to buy such stocks before the record date to be eligible for the ‘reward’. The rally in LIC, Trent, and Anand Rathi shares since March could be, at least in part due to the bonus rush.
Just that this bonus is not really a bonus. There’s nothing free, extra, or more about it. No reward, no bonanza. It is simply shareholders’ money being given back to shareholders in the form of more shares. Money moving from the left pocket to the right. Companies, the market, and the media must spare investors the hype. And the regulator must take note.
Bonus issues do not increase the size of the pie or result in wealth addition for shareholders. They simply transfer funds belonging to the company’s owners (i.e., equity shareholders) from one accounting head (reserves and surplus) in the balance sheet to another (share capital). To the extent there is an increase in the share capital, there is a decrease in the reserves and surplus, which is essentially profits accumulated over the years.
In effect, it is a zero-sum for shareholders, as their total worth and wealth in the company remains unchanged.
The market knows this and adjusts the share price accordingly.
Consider an LIC shareholder who held 10 shares and received another 10 as a bonus. Before the bonus date, the share price was around Rs 830. After the bonus, it halved to around Rs 415. The total value of the shares before the bonus was Rs 8,300 (10 shares at Rs 830 a piece), and it remained the same Rs 8,300 after the bonus (20 shares at Rs 415 each).
The market ‘adjusted’ the share price to reflect the increase in the number of shares due to the bonus. Simply because the company’s fundamentals or its financials did not change overnight due to the bonus issue. It was just an accounting entry. The earnings per share (EPS) also adjusted, halving in LIC’s case.
Bonuses are cosmetic. Yet, companies declare bonus issues for a few reasons.
One, by increasing the number of shares and reducing their price, the stock becomes more liquid and more affordable for small investors, at least optically. RIL’s share price after its 2024 bonus fell from around Rs 2,200 to around Rs 1,100. LIC’s stock price came down from Rs 830 to Rs 415.
Higher participation from investors, combined with the feel-good factor associated with bonus issues, might push the post-bonus price above the theoretical adjusted level. But its sustainability would eventually depend on whether the company delivers on performance.
Sometimes, private companies planning to go public issue bonus shares to keep the asking price within acceptable limits for small investors.
Others use bonus shares to perk up sentiment and mask weak performance.
While the magic word ‘bonus’ contributes to the sentiment lift, what also helps is the perception that the company has the confidence to service a larger number of shares. That is, pay dividends at the same rate on more shares.
For instance, if a LIC shareholder in the above example was receiving dividends on 10 shares until last week, she will get dividends on 20 shares in the future, hopefully at the same rate per share. But this may or may not happen. The company might simply decide to halve its dividend per share to adjust for the higher number of shares. On the other hand, it might just decide to maintain the dividend at the same rate. But that would depend on the company doing well fundamentally and improving profits.
Investors should therefore bet on fundamentally strong companies with good growth potential, available at reasonable prices and valuations, not merely on companies that seemingly ‘reward’ shareholders through bonus shares.
Perhaps, market regulator Sebi needs to step in and mandate a name change. “Bonus” is a misnomer. How about “Additional shares in lieu of reserves”? A mouthful, yes, but it conveys the correct picture, doesn’t it? Or maybe simply “Internal transfer”?
