Agents changed everything about hiring except the outcome. AI was supposed to make hiring a better love marriage, instead it made it into an arranged marriage

The newest way to build a new AI product is to say, “X is broken, and we fixed it using AI”. 

Well, hiring was probably broken, but AI didn’t change the outcome. 

Instead, it made it into an arms race where candidates are sold AI tools that help them apply to companies and roles en masse in an automated way. On the other side, recruiters who have increasingly gotten tired of the same AI-generated answers, resumes, and artefacts that have been generated by AI have resorted to deploying AI systems to filter out candidates and get what they are looking for. And AI companies sit on both sides of the transaction, billing tokens while hiring outcomes come back to exactly where they were.

Almost exactly a year ago, I faced this exact frustration. And I wrote about it in The Nutgraf in an edition titled “AI sells swords at dawn and shields at dusk”.

I’ll go back to the example I originally shared—that of my Product Designer hiring. In an earlier world, I’d get 50 applicants, eliminate about 30 of them, and interview maybe 20. Today, I get 500 applicants, use AI to eliminate about 480 of them, and interview… 20 candidates. I’m back where I started, except both the candidates and I have paid AI companies for something neither of us signed up for.

The tragedy is that AI companies profit from both sides of the world they’ve created. On one hand, they’ll charge people to create huge volumes of cheap, low-quality content, and then they’ll charge humans to protect them from it. 

Net-net, we’ll end up in a world that’s not much different from earlier—except that there’s a tax paid on both sides. 

AI sells swords at dawn and shields at dusk, The Nutgraf https://the-ken.com/the-nutgraf/ai-sells-swords-at-dawn-and-shields-at-dusk/

I’m quite proud of the analogy I came up with to describe this. Hiring needs to be more like a “love marriage” with the candidate working with you as a live-in relationship. Instead, agents have made it more like an “arranged marriage”, where candidates have agents who create their profiles, and agents who evaluate other profiles, before humans even meet each other. 

Except instead of relatives and parents, the machines are doing the role, and getting paid for it. 

So in today’s edition of Two by Two, I decided to talk to two founders who are on either side of this transaction—one of them is using agents and AI exclusively to hire, and the other is “selling” AI to both candidates and companies. 

To both of them, I asked the same question: how does AI lead to better outcomes for both companies and the candidates? Aren’t we back to where we began, except with a token bill? 

My first guest is Aakash Dharmadhikari, co-founder and CPTO of Realfast.ai http://realfast.ai, a startup that’s an AI-native services company. Realfast caught my attention because they were recently hiring to scale up their team and they had a single unique requirement from all applicants—apply using an AI agent.

Meanwhile, on the candidate side, Saumil Tripathi, co-founder and CEO of Grapevine. Grapevine began as an anonymous professional network to discuss salaries, layoffs, and workplace culture. Then it swiftly grew past one million users. It has since pivoted hard into AI hiring with two products: TAL, an agent that scans job boards on the candidate’s behalf and Round1, an AI interview-simulation product. In some ways, Grapevine sits on both sides of the AI in the hiring equation.

In this episode, both of these founders defend their approaches and argue that they have, in fact, solved hiring in their own ways, with better outcomes for companies and candidates. 

I’ll let you decide if they convince you about it. 

1. The arms dealers don’t think anyone wins this war

Both founders built their companies on AI-hiring, and both spend the hour conceding the tools are degrading the thing they’re paid to repair.

Saumil: “AI is actually not solving recruitment right now. AI is in the process of destroying it. So, you know, it will completely destroy recruitment before someone can make something that solves it.”

Aakash: “If you’re going to grow fast and you don’t want to make a lot of mistakes, you have to figure out innovative ways of fighting this arms race that you’re talking about—and the arms race part is absolutely true.”

2. When the take-home can be “one-shotted”, what’s left to test?

If a model produces a cleaner solution than your best engineer, the only thing worth grading is the one part the model can’t supply.

Aakash: “We’re not looking for your coding ability. We’re actually looking for your ability to learn a completely new domain. We’re looking at your ability to drive an agent to produce certain artefacts.”

Saumil: “Beyond a point, I must say I still think some percent of people are cheating and we think they’re very smart and they deserve a job anyway.”

3. The whole industry filters out AI. One founder filters out the people who refuse it.

Recruiters are buying subscriptions to screen AI-generated applications, while these two are handing candidates more firepower, not less.

Aakash: “Rather, if you don’t use AI, don’t apply.”

Saumil: “So all we did was we said the companies have so much money, so much ammunition, let us give some to the candidates as well. Not to create more noise, but to fall in love with a job and apply very deeply to that job.”

4. The signals you’ve trusted for 20 years are the ones that died first

Resumes and cover letters became the easiest things to fake, so the real test moved to the live call and the agent log, i.e., the parts a candidate can’t pre-write.

Aakash: “Email and resumes are no longer unvarnished human work. That is the problem that we are trying to solve.”

Saumil: “The candidates I will give you through Round 1 will be closer to 90% or 95%. You will not hate the candidates I send you—I’m not saying they’ll get hired, but it vastly increases the signal side.”

5. Why the “just come work with us” dream collapses above four years’ experience 

Everyone romanticises proof-of-work hiring, but India’s notice periods and the two-way judgment of senior candidates squeeze it into a narrow band.

Saumil: “Somewhere beyond three to four years of work experience hiring, this becomes a little bit impractical because people in India will not leave a job before they get the next offer.”

Aakash: “So I love the idea, I think it’s possible within a certain Goldilocks zone—it is not going to happen beyond a certain scale, and it will not happen before a certain scale.”
