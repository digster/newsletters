Foreign universities setting up in India like to show off their memorandums. 

The signing ceremonies are carefully staged. Senior bureaucrats smile for photographs, and vice-chancellors talk about academic exchange and global classrooms. But behind the photo ops is a reality that is almost never discussed. 

That many of these universities are landing on Indian shores because things are not going especially well back home. And many of the students signing up are here because going abroad has become harder than expected.

For years, countries like Canada, the UK, and Australia built higher-education systems that depended heavily on international students, especially Indians. Students, in turn, were buying an education, mobility, residency, and, ideally, a better labour market. But the visa mattered as much as the degree. That equation is beginning to break down.

Which is what makes recent developments in India more interesting. Last month, six foreign universities that have already established campuses in India partnered with the edtech platform Eruditus to launch a Rs 1,000 crore, multi-year scholarship initiative. 

The scholarship is a distress signal sent in the language of philanthropy. 

Because foreign education in India is not easy. Not as a business or as a pedagogical framework, and certainly not as a guaranteed route to opportunity.

And that’s the running thread: of education systems that act like markets under pressure, where universities chase students across borders, students chase visas across continents, and everyone else, from consultants to edtechs and coaching operators to even medical colleges, steps in to monetise the anxiety.

My university, my interests

Last year, nearly 1.8 million Indian students went abroad for higher education. That number has climbed so quickly over the last few years that it is easy to miss the other cohort growing alongside it: students who wanted to leave, but couldn’t.

https://the-ken.com/story/who-benefits-from-the-influx-of-foreign-universities-in-india-not-students/?utm_source=email&utm_medium=newsletter&utm_campaign=collection?utm_source=ro_hdr&utm_medium=email&utm_campaign=

Sometimes it’s money. Sometimes it’s visas or family obligations, or the overwhelming complexity of the process. 

Increasingly, that uncertainty is creating a strange new market. Foreign universities inside India, aimed at students who still want the signalling value of an international degree, just without the immigration gamble attached to it.

The University Grants Commission opened the door for foreign campuses in 2023. Since then, universities from the UK and Australia have arrived with surprising speed. 

The problem is these universities are not designed to benefit the students but the ones who signed the memorandum. Some are still operating out of corporate buildings in Gift City rather than sprawling campuses.

With branded degrees crossing borders, students are now ever so confused about what to do. So they turn to a rapidly growing ecosystem of education consultants, study-abroad influencers https://the-ken.com/edsetgo/move-over-finfluencers-the-study-aboard-gurus-are-here/?utm_source=email&utm_medium=newsletter&utm_campaign=collection, and venture-backed startups that promise to decode the labyrinth of applications, tests, and visas. 

Leverage Edu, one of the bigger players in this space, recently turned Ebitda-positive after years of aggressive growth. It turned a corner and is now reportedly looking for a public listing.

Who is welcome?

Every year, thousands of students leave for medical schools in China, Ukraine, Russia, the Philippines, and elsewhere. And unlike the common trend of getting a work visa after finishing their education, this particular crop is more interested in coming back home. 

Then why do they go abroad? Because of a capacity failure at home. 

https://the-ken.com/story/india-needs-doctors-so-why-are-foreign-trained-ones-working-unpaid-or-not-at-all/?utm_source=email&utm_medium=newsletter&utm_campaign=collection?utm_source=ro_hdr&utm_medium=email&utm_campaign=

India does not have enough medical seats. It also lacks enough doctors. The allopathic doctor-patient ratio has inched forward https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=2197614&reg=3&lang=1#:~:text=The%20doctor%2Dpopulation%20ratio%20in%20India%20is%20estimated,(WHO)%20recommends%20a%20doctor%2Dto%2Dpopulation%20ratio%20of%201:1000. from 1:1,260 to 1:1,251 as of December 2025, but that’s very marginal. The recommended ratio by the World Health Organisation is 1:1,000. 

By that measure, the country is short of hundreds of thousands of doctors. And medical students go out to study to treat that ratio. Of course, that needs a lot of money. But even after going through all that, they have a hard time getting a paid position back home.
