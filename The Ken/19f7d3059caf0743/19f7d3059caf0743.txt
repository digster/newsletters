In early May, when Galaxeye launched its satellite, Mission Drishti, aboard a SpaceX rocket, it was a moment of celebration as well as political flexing. (As most space events in India have become.) A world-first, the two-in-one satellite combined electro-optical and synthetic aperture radar (SAR) sensors on a single platform, allowing it to capture images day and night, through clouds and in all weather conditions.

There was demand for that capability. Founder Suyash Singh told The Ken the satellite’s imaging capacity was already booked until April 2027. But the audacious attempt by a five-year-old startup ran into some expected challenges—stabilisation, which is common for earth-observation satellites—and unexpected, catastrophic jolts, such as a vendor failing to supply space-grade components.

The latter is a gap that the fledgling private space sector will have to solve collectively if it wants to avoid costly mistakes and derailment of its current momentum.

On 7 July, Singh took to X to officially announce the end of the mission.

He said https://x.com/thesuyashsingh/status/2074429802145325179: 

“Mission Drishti reached orbit and earned accolades that meant the world to us. For a few weeks, we watched something we had built with our own hands come alive above the planet. Then, after those early weeks of operations, Drishti stopped performing the way we had dreamed it would. I won’t dress that up. It hurts. But here is the truth we’re holding on to: what this mission taught us; we could not have learned any other way…”

So, what brought down India’s largest privately built satellite—a 190-kg bundle of cameras, antennas, sensors, computers, solar panels and power systems—had little to do with the few weeks it spent in orbit, and everything to do with the years spent building it.
