Bengaluru’s early winter has set in this week. I felt a proper chill this Tuesday morning as I headed to the gym in the wee hours of the morning. The air smelled fresh and of blooming frangipani.

By lunch, though, the weather is to die for. You can still feel the chill, especially on your fingertips. But the bright and piercing sunshine is softly warm, so you’re feeling cold and warm at the same time. Indiranagar’s inner roads are fragrant too, mostly from the thousands of tiny Honge blossoms on many of them. You can hear birds chirping. You can see BWSSB staff calmly breaking roads with JCB backhoes, piling earth on footpaths and roads, so you’re forced to engage in who-blinks-first with oncoming traffic. 

But I can’t be mad at the traffic today. Because today is also Karnataka Rajyotsava. It marks 70 years since 1 November, 1956, when the Kannada-speaking regions of Mysore, Coorg, and the Bombay, Hyderabad, and Madras provinces came together as one state. I have lived in Bengaluru for 29 of those 70 years, first from 1994 to 1998 and then since 2001. I haven’t lived anywhere else for as long. I still love the weather and people here (I ignore the traffic because you cannot have it all). Bengaluru’s people have given me more than its weather has. They made room for me when I arrived, and they have made room for millions of people like me from every part of India, often without much thanks. I built The Ken in this city, and many of the founders I invite onto First Principles built their companies here too. ಕರ್ನಾಟಕ ರಾಜ್ಯೋತ್ಸವದ ಶುಭಾಶಯಗಳು!

Coincidentally, today is also Kerala Piravi. On 1 November, 1956, Travancore-Cochin joined with the Malabar district and Kasaragod to form Kerala, now called Keralam. So, the state where I was born turns 70 on the same day as the state where I live. My family is from Kerala, and to think I’ve ended up spending 29 years a few hundred kilometres away in Bengaluru :) Kerala has sent its people out for generations, to the Gulf, to Mumbai and Chennai and Bengaluru, and to almost every country with a hospital or an oil field. I, too, am one of them. കേരളപ്പിറവി ആശംസകൾ!

“Meh, RPE is for losers”

“Meh, recovery is for losers.”

“Meh, mobility is for losers.” 

“Meh, warm-up is for losers.”

“Meh, Zone 2 is for losers.”

“Meh, rest between sets is for losers.”

“Meh, RPE is for losers.”

When you’re young, your metabolism and body let you get away with a lot. You don’t realise how much till you cross middle age. 

Once you’re on the other side, you sheepishly and humbly accept that you are now part of the cohort for which all of those matter. 

One such thing is RPE, or the Rate of Perceived Exertion. Every time you complete a set of strength exercises, you note down how much you exerted yourself, usually on a scale of 1–10. A 10 means you maxed yourself out and couldn’t do a single more rep. 8 means you think you could pump out 2 more reps with good form. 

Some people track the inverse of RPE, called RIR—Reps in Reserve. So an RPE of 7 would mean an RIR of 3, for instance. 

No, you did not accidentally open a gym-bro newsletter. This is still very much First Principles. 

RPE https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/articles/17450-rated-perceived-exertion-rpe-scale was developed by Swedish researcher Gunnar Borg in the 1960s and popularised by powerlifting coach Mike Tuchscherer. The rationale is that readiness varies day to day with sleep, stress and carried-over fatigue, so you prescribe an effort level and let the weight vary to match. This means a well-rested person’s RPE will be very different from a tired and stressed person’s. 

The main idea behind RPE is autoregulation—your ability to dynamically adjust variables (e.g. weight or volume) based on how you’re feeling on a given day, instead of blindly following a program. 

So, a program will say “5 reps at RPE 8,” and the lifter picks whatever weight gets them there that day. Thus, on a good day, 50kg could be an RPE 6, but on a different day, after a bad night’s sleep, the same 50kg could be an RPE 9.

Now let’s imagine your workplace as your gym, and your career as your training calendar. How many variations of work do you do in a week? How tough are they? How much do they contribute to your development and growth as a professional? 

Old-school management prescribes the load (ship this product, write these stories, close these deals) and treats the cost as fixed. In reality, the same task costs very different amounts depending on the person, the week and what else is going on. 

Instead, if you manage by effort band, you’d say “this quarter should feel like a 7–8 for you” and let the scope change to fit it.

Growth means a task that was a 9 a year ago is a 6 now. Your first shipped product feature, a hard edit, a tough negotiation. At 4, you’re rehearsing, not learning. At 5, you’re probably stagnating. You’re competent, but nothing is adapting.

Sustained excellence happens around 7–8, with some “reps” held in reserve for the moments that need it.

Just as training mostly happens at 7–8, not 10, similarly, learning happens around 7–9. 

The journey to truly superior performance is neither for the faint of heart nor for the impatient. The development

of genuine expertise requires struggle, sacrifice, and honest, often painful self-assessment. There are no shortcuts. It will take you at least a decade to achieve expertise, and you will need to invest that time wisely by engaging in “deliberate” practice – practice that focuses on tasks beyond your current level of competence and comfort. 

The Making of an Expert https://hbr.org/2007/07/the-making-of-an-expert by K. Anders Ericsson, Michael J. Prietula, and Edward T. Cokely (HBR)

Why is training hard, whether in the gym or at work, important? Because that’s when we stimulate growth. This isn’t new to us, readers (and, er, writer) of First Principles.

Sustained high effort is a training block, not a cost to get through. Also, when work begins to feel like play, that is roughly the 7–8 band. 

#165, “The grind is the purpose, the moat, and the playground” (20 Sep 2026) https://the-ken.com/newsletter/first-principles/the-grind-is-the-purpose-the-moat-and-the-playground/. 

There are more similarities between athletic and corporate performance than you think. Because the underlying subject in both cases is the same. It’s us. 

Panio: The importance of rest is very counter-intuitive. [But] there is a balance between how hardcore you go and how much you rest.

Steve: That’s one of the things we really want to get across. The message out there is: work really hard, put your nose to the stone, and grind away. But regardless of whether it’s athletics or life, the same principle applies: we call it “Stress + rest = growth.” To get growth and adaptation, whether physical or mental, you have to have rest. Your body adapts when it has time to come off the stresses and stimuli. For instance, we process learning when we sleep.

An Olympic Coach on How to Train Smarter, Not Harder https://nextbigideaclub.com/magazine/conversation-an-olympic-coach-on-how-to-train-smarter-not-harder/14901/

But working at RPE 10 week after week in the gym is also a sure-shot recipe for… injury. Which is why experienced lifters rarely train to failure.

At work, the equivalent is burnout. 

RPE 10 should be rare and on purpose: a launch, a crisis, a big story. Workplace cultures that treat 10 as the baseline get injuries, which at work means people quitting or making mistakes.

One reason I never bothered tracking RPE when I was younger was because I felt I didn’t need to. I could get by without it. Injuries were rarer, results were quicker. 

But as those started to appear as limiting constraints, I took to tracking RPE to make sure I was operating in the optimal range (usually around 7–8). I was sceptical at first about how accurate my own judgement about something as vague as exertion would be.

These days, I often find that I’ve noted the exact same RPE for an exercise as the week before.

The only way to discover your strengths is through feedback analysis. Whenever you make a key decision or take a key action, write down what you expect will happen. Nine or 12 months later, compare the actual results with your expectations. I have been practising this method for 15 to 20 years now, and every time I do it, I am surprised. The feedback analysis showed me, for instance—and to my great surprise—that I have an intuitive understanding of technical people, whether they are engineers or accountants or market researchers. It also showed me that I don’t really resonate with generalists.

Feedback analysis is by no means new. It was invented sometime in the 14th century by an otherwise totally obscure German theologian and picked up quite independently, some 150 years later, by John Calvin and Ignatius of Loyola, each of whom incorporated it into the practice of his followers. In fact, the steadfast focus on performance and results that this habit produces explains why the institutions these two men founded, the Calvinist church and the Jesuit order, came to dominate Europe within 30 years.

Practised consistently, this simple method will show you within a fairly short period of time, maybe two or three years, where your strengths lie—and this is the most important thing to know. The method will show you what you are doing or failing to do that deprives you of the full benefits of your strengths. It will show you where you are not particularly competent. And finally, it will show you where you have no strengths and cannot perform.

Managing Oneself https://hbr.org/2005/01/managing-oneself by Peter Drucker (HBR)

The same knowledge, but this time from a strength training coach.

Surprisingly, knowing how to manage the difficulty of hard sets is one of the greatest unsung “secrets” of successful strength training. Many people don’t even get close to muscular failure in their workouts and wonder why nothing changes, and many others meet failure far too often and wonder why everything always hurts. You now understand how to thread this needle.

Bigger Leaner Stronger by Michael Matthews

Not your chai-tea-latte

Who makes the typical cup of chai in India? From your answers so far, I can picture three people.

The first drinks from a mug. Their chai is about a quarter milk, toned, poured in once the water boils. A teaspoon of CTC goes in, along with ginger they crush rather than grate, and either no sugar or less than a spoonful. They let it boil up once, give it three to five minutes, and then strain it. This person is most likely past 45 and calls it “chai”.

The second makes something closer to what a tapri (roadside stall) sells. Half the cup or more is milk. Ginger and cardamom both go into the pot, and so does a full teaspoon of sugar or a piece of jaggery. Then they boil it up three times, or leave it to simmer until the colour turns. They pour it into a cup or a steel tumbler. Most of the people who make this cup are between 25 and 44.

The third skips milk entirely. They brew Darjeeling, loose-leaf or in a bag, drink it from a mug with nothing to sweeten it, and call it “cha” or just “tea”. More of them are in their late fifties and early sixties than in any other age group.

I doubt many of you make exactly any of these cups. But we need more variety! So far, nearly 2/3rd of the responses come from men, and Mumbai and Bengaluru make up almost 50% of the responses. Tell me your chai recipe 🙏🏾
