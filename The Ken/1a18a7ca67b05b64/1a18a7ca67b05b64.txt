If comedy and horror shared a border, you’d find last week’s eMudhra episode dancing recklessly along its edge.  

Here’s the meme-worthy gist: eMudhra is one of the few companies entrusted with issuing security certificates to websites worldwide. These digital credentials verify that a website is authentic and secure, and to issue them, the company relies on “private keys”. And when it could have chosen from more security key combinations than there are atoms in the universe—a good key would be about 116 digits long—it chose keys as simplistic as just the number four. 

Keys that weak, of course, left massive vulnerabilities in websites around the world, and the blowback was severe and immediate. eMudhra’s spectacular fall from grace was rivalled only by the subsequent fall in its stock price, which was down 36% within a week. And eventually, they were cornered into shutting down their security certificate business entirely. 

But amidst the wreckage, a few independent security researchers began flagging another, supposedly unrelated phenomenon. 

On 21 October, a digital-rights group found https://sflc.in/sflc-ins-statement-on-emudhra-private-key-vulnerability/#:~:text=Food%20Safety%20and%20Standards%20Authority%20of%20India that the Food Safety and Standards Authority of India’s (FSSAI) website was blocking some users from accessing it because its security certificate was no longer valid.

Two days later, an ethical hacker, Nemo, found FSSAI https://captnemo.in/blog/2026/10/23/emsign-revoked-gov-in/ to be only one of many such websites.
