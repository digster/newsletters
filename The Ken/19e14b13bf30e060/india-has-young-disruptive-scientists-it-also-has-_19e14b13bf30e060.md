Does appetite for disruptive work decline with age?
It is a contentious question, but certainly worth asking. Workforces across the world are ageing, and how academics in universities and research institutions shape science will determine how countries make progress.
A new study published in the 8 May [edition](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.ady8732) of Science attempts to answer it. Studying a dataset of 12.5 million scientists who published between 1960 and 2020, the researchers found that scientists tend to get better with age at generating novelty—connecting previously unrelated ideas. But their capacity for disruption, “or overturning established paradigms with fundamentally new ones,” tends to taper.
The study argues that research systems should encourage both continuity and renewal, though it’s the former that has become dominant in most developed countries. While it clarifies why earlier work on this subject has been mixed, it also links cognitive dynamics to team structure, explains why larger, hierarchical teams innovate less due to ageing leaders, and points to the social foundations of “intellectual ageing”.
The paper has some holes, including its placement of India above the US on the disruptive-publication curve, near China. But before we get to that, there’s one anecdote, dated but still relevant, that shows how deeply hierarchical Indian science is and why this study fails to account for it.
One of India’s most decorated scientists retired from the directorship of a research institution in Bengaluru but did not vacate the director’s bungalow. He stayed on and headed a few committees in the country. The incoming director, an accomplished scientist in his own right, did not get to stay in the official residence for his full five-year term because the previous director, academically his senior, neither thought it was necessary nor was asked by his successor or the administration to do so.
