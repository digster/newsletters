Good Morning Ishan,
ITC has spent decades trying to become more than a cigarette company. Yet cigarettes remain its most profitable business, and the business that keeps putting it under pressure.
This February, the government increased cigarette taxes by 20–55%, depending on the cigarette’s length. The hike was steep enough to risk pushing smokers towards illicit, untaxed cigarettes. The bourses read it as a hit to ITC’s earnings: its share price has fallen 28% so far in 2026. In comparison, the Nifty FMCG index has declined 16% over the same period.
There is another, more fundamental problem. People are increasingly aware of the harm caused by smoking. This has stopped cigarettes from being an extraordinarily good business for ITC. Every year, just before the Union Budget, wholesalers in the Gwalior market used to buy Lifebuoy and Lux soaps from HUL on credit and sell them quickly at a loss. They would use the proceeds to stock up on ITC cigarettes, knowing that a tax increase was almost guaranteed in the Budget. After the Budget, they would sell the cigarettes at a premium, more than making up for the loss on soap.
Given that cigarettes are addictive, demand holds even when prices rise.
Sudhir Sitapati, a former chief executive of Godrej Consumer Products, noted this annual phenomenon when he was working at HUL in 2000. It was just an illustration of the dominance of ITC’s cigarette business, and of why getting away from it was never going to be easy.
