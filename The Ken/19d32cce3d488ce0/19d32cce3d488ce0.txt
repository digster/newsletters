Andrew Grove was the third employee of technology company Intel and its CEO from 1987 to 1998. 

He’s seen as one of the greatest business executives of the late 20th century. Even if you don’t know much about him, chances are you’ve heard someone utter the title of one of his books, Only the Paranoid Survive, which was published in 1996. He articulated the idea that the leaders who survive manage to sense existential threats before they are blindsided, and then act aggressively to get ahead of those threats. Meanwhile, complacent leaders fall into a holding pattern until the evidence is in front of them, and then it’s too late for their company.

Grove had firsthand experience of this. In the mid-1980s, Intel was primarily a memory-chip company. Japanese competitors were making DRAM (dynamic random access memory) chips, but offered them at much lower prices. One day, Grove asked Intel co-founder Gordon Moore (who made the observation formalised in Moore’s Law) what he thought a new Intel CEO would do if the two of them were booted from the company.

Moore said their replacement would ditch the memory-chip business. So, that’s precisely what they did. Intel pivoted to making microprocessors. In the process, it became one of the most valuable companies in the world, reaching peak valuation in 2000, during the height of the dot-com boom.

I want to go back to 20 January 2025. That’s the day the world woke up and smelled the coffee after the release of the Deepseek R-1 model. 

Silicon Valley scrambled to reason why a little-known Chinese lab could rival the ChatGPTs and Geminis of the world—and do it with chip constraints. We got article after article analysing what the “Deepseek moment” meant. But those tracking Chinese tech reminded us that Deepseek wasn’t a random event. There was history, politics, policy, and a whole set of overlapping factors that led to the R-1 model. 

Cut to now. We are seeing images of thousands of people lining up to get Openclaw installed across China. And again, everyone is trying to explain the agents “mania” and “craze” there. 

On Zero Shot, we wanted to look beyond the hype and talk to someone who could help us situate what’s currently unfolding in China. And Kevin Xu, the founder of Interconnected Capital, a global technology hedge fund and writer of the Interconnected substack, did exactly that.
