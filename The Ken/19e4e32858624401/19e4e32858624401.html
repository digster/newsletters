Meanwhile, elsewhere in Indian infrastructure held together by institutional improvisation…

Vodafone Idea (Vi) recently reported a Rs 52,000-crore quarterly profit, and its stock has doubled over the past year. In the last month alone, it has rallied more than 40%. Analysts are once again discussing stabilisation, improving Arpu, slowing subscriber losses, and whether the telco may finally be turning a corner.

Which is slightly amusing, because The Ken was writing in 2023 about whether “slow demise” was actually Vodafone Idea’s most realistic future. 

Back then, one telecom analyst made an observation that, in hindsight, now reads as unusually prescient. “If I were Mr Birla,” he said, referring to Aditya Birla Group chairman Kumar Mangalam Birla, one of Vi’s key promoters, “I would wait for rules to change even further and make it favourable for myself.”

That, more or less, is what happened.

The government converted dues into equity and became Vi’s largest shareholder. AGR (adjusted gross revenue) liabilities were recalculated, and staggered payment schedules became more flexible. Birla, who had once practically offered the company to the government, returned earlier this month as non-executive chairman. The Aditya Birla Group has now committed another Rs 4,730 crore to the company while banks continue discussing a much larger funding package. 

The important thing, though, is that nobody really called this a rescue.

Even in 2023, government officials privately described the objective more narrowly: not necessarily saving Vodafone Idea, merely helping it “survive”.  There is a meaningful difference between a turnaround story and what one official in that same story essentially described as a telecom “coma vigil”.

And that may still be the best way to understand Vodafone Idea today.

Because the company still looks financially fragile. The latest profit largely came from accounting gains tied to AGR dues. Revenue grew just 3%. Vi is still seeking roughly Rs 35,000 crore in funding while planning a Rs 45,000-crore capex programme merely to remain competitive. Its rivals remain miles ahead on network quality, spectrum, subscribers, 5G rollout, and, crucially, money.

And yet, Vi survives.

At some point, Vodafone Idea stopped behaving like a normal telecom company. A normal telco is supposed to either win or die (unless it’s BSNL). Vi evolved into a company whose continued existence became strategically useful to too many parts of the system. Now, it increasingly trades less on telecom fundamentals and more on a broader assumption that India will keep finding ways to stop the story from ending.

Accidentally national

There are many ways for a government to nationalise a company. Usually, though, the process is more deliberate than this.

Vodafone Idea spent years accumulating dues it could not realistically pay, after the Supreme Court’s AGR ruling detonated the balance sheets of telecom operators. Eventually, the government converted part of those dues into equity, becoming Vi’s largest shareholder. This created a fairly unusual arrangement.

India then effectively owned a large chunk of a private telecom operator that has behaved and still behaves like a distressed company while competing against two rivals with much deeper pockets. And yet, despite becoming the largest shareholder, the government does not particularly want to run it either. It just wants it to continue existing.

That’s key because the state’s objective here was to preserve a three-player telecom market long enough for the sector to stop eating itself.

People forget how destructive the telecom wars became after Jio’s entry. Tariffs collapsed, revenues imploded, debt piled up, and Vodafone and Idea merged largely because surviving separately didn’t seem practical. Even then, the merged entity kept bleeding subscribers while Airtel and Jio pulled further ahead on network quality and capex.

Telecom had stopped being just another private industry somewhere during this period. Payments started running on it, as did streaming and enterprise software. AI ambitions, cloud services, digital governance, e-commerce, food delivery, and half the Indian internet increasingly sit on top of telecom networks that require absurdly expensive and continuous investment.

Which means policymakers became trapped in this position. A Vodafone Idea collapse might improve industry profitability in the short term. But a duopoly controlling India’s digital infrastructure market also sounds like the sort of thing governments later regret encouraging.

Traded like an opinion on Indian policy

The funny thing about Vodafone Idea’s stock rally is that the market is technically reacting to telecom metrics while really betting on something else entirely.

Yes, Arpu improved to Rs 190, subscriber losses narrowed sharply, and Ebitda rose nearly 5%. These things matter. But nobody looking seriously at Vodafone Idea believes the company has suddenly found a secret way to outspend Jio.

The more important development is that the system keeps extending Vi’s timeline.

“We are deeply engaged with the SBI-led consortium, comprising public sector banks, private banks as well as foreign banks, and we are very confident of closing that very fast”

Vi CEO Abhijit Kishore during Q4 earnings call

And why not? Telecom markets behave strangely once they consolidate aggressively. Prices go up, investment incentives weaken, regulators become more nervous, and governments lose bargaining leverage. India has already spent the better part of a decade trying to stabilise a sector that swung from hypercompetition to near-consolidation remarkably fast.

Vodafone Idea’s existence helps everybody avoid confronting what the end state actually looks like. And so the company survives in this middle ground among distressed asset, policy instrument, and infrastructure.

A business that moved on

One of the things about Vodafone Idea’s story is that the industry transformed while the company spent years managing financial emergencies.

The original Vodafone-Idea merger was fundamentally a scale story. Bigger subscriber base with larger spectrum holding and combined infrastructure. So, cost efficiencies. That should have been enough to survive telecom consolidation.

But Jio understood early that telecom was no longer merely about connectivity. It was becoming an ecosystem business tied to entertainment, cloud infrastructure, enterprise software, AI, e-commerce, payments, and data consumption itself. Airtel adapted fast enough to remain competitive. 

Vi spent much of that same period negotiating with lenders, managing AGR liabilities, cutting costs, and trying to avoid collapse.

Which is partly why many of its newer initiatives feel both sensible and slightly late. The company now talks extensively about AI-powered recharge assistants, OTT aggregation through Vi Movies & TV, enterprise services, IoT offerings, and digital products layered on top of the network. 

These are not bad ideas. They are, in fact, roughly what every telecom company now wants to become. The difficulty is that ecosystem businesses are easier to build when they are not simultaneously trying to refinance their existence.

Real business may now be time

Reading through our Vodafone Idea coverage over the years, one thing becomes consistent. Every few years, the company approaches some version of existential collapse. Then another stakeholder steps in.

The government restructures dues, banks reconsider exposure, promoters recommit, investors rediscover optionality, analysts talk about stabilisation. 

The cycle repeats.
