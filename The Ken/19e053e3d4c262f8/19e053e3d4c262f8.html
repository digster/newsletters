I’ve been (co)hosting the Two by Two podcast for nearly two years now. As long-time listeners of the podcast know, we don’t over-prepare for our conversations or come in with specific conclusions—instead, we try to hold on to a theme and go where the discussion takes us. 

My favourite episodes are the ones where we go in with a specific idea of what I think the episode is about, but then the guests surprise us and take us in a completely different direction. 

And well, last week’s episode was one of those. 

Essentially, I went in with a premise about the dilemma faced by early-career product designers. It’s never been harder to find a place to begin, especially because AI has taken the function by storm and is in danger of pushing them out. My fundamental argument was that AI has taken away all the “grunt work” that young designers did—stuff like designing the fourth iteration of the button, or the edge cases. This was the work they did religiously, day in and out, to develop the necessary skills to become a senior designer. 

So now in this new world, what are the options ahead?

In my telling, I visualised it as two paths for designers, which I articulated like this: 

“The first path is essentially what I call the new grunt work. Go and search for it, which means that you basically go and say, okay, maybe I can’t do all of these other things, or machines do these things, but let me find different kinds of work that I can do. And by doing that work, after I do them, I get them in front of humans, and I basically say, now give me this feedback, please, and tell me how I’m doing this so that I can learn better. Now the problem is that nobody knows what this looks like, and nobody’s really clear what is this room that this junior designer walks into where senior or experienced humans give them feedback. Why should they? They owe them nothing. So that’s the first part—find new forms of grunt work, essentially the same as before, but find it in a different way” 

Then, because we are living in the era of Claude Design, I defined a second path:

“The second one is something that a lot of designers now are starting to do, which is that forget this grunt work stuff, let’s just jump straight away to judgment, which means that let’s try to learn how to critique, how to prompt, rather than execute. And then accept that, look, this is the path for becoming a designer, and I don’t know how this person is going to create judgment, but they are.”

And to help figure out the ramifications of these two paths, I had two wonderful guests with me: 

Jay Datta, founder of Designup, Southeast Asia’s largest design conference, and 25 years in design at Deutsche Bank, Adobe, Flipkart, and Makemytrip.

Shreyas Satish, founder of Ownpath, a design studio. Shreyas has spent years trying to rebuild the apprenticeship model from outside the system. 

To both of them, I posed a question: 

How does a 22-year-old build judgement now?

And well, as always, the episode became about the incentives, and how this judgement can be learnt, and who pays for it. 

 

Medicine trains 22-year-olds to perform surgery. Design doesn’t train anyone for anything

Shreyas made a canny observation: the key problem in his mind is that the training system was never built, and colleges are the ones who were supposed to build it.

“A 22-year-old is leading a contingent in the Indian Army. You have 22-year-olds flying planes. You have 22-year-olds performing surgeries. What is the gap between those fields and design? The gap is you don’t have an effective simulation environment where people are getting trained in things with consequences. The projects people are doing are extremely hypothetical and conceptual to the point that it doesn’t help build judgment. Whereas in the army, pilot training, surgery—these are done in an environment that simulates consequences as well. That is the only long-term solve: institutions that adapt and create this environment where people feel less unready and intimidated.”

 

Paying to apprentice isn’t exploitation. It’s just honest

The old deal, i.e., the company pays you to do the grunt work, you learn in the process, assumed that companies had an incentive to invest in junior talent. They don’t anymore. 

Jay’s argument is that paying a senior designer for real access to real projects is simply acknowledging what that time and network are actually worth, and the only model where both sides have skin in the game.

“There was a very radical idea we were discussing in a leaders group—that people pay for apprenticeships. They just don’t pay fees to learn, but they pay fees to be actually working on real projects in real time. There might be a time where you’re not getting paid for apprenticeships, but you have to pay for them. You are essentially getting time with a top designer, you’re going to eat their head, they will take a liking to what you’re doing, and maybe recommend you to 10 other people. You’re not only getting to learn—you’re also getting him or her to maybe open doors for you. That is probably much more invaluable than just learning.”

“Taste” is an annoying word

I was so thrilled when I discovered someone who detests this word as much as I do.

Here’s Jay:

“Everyone talks about taste. I think it’s much more than taste. It is about building good judgement, it is about building intuition. The taste for one brand versus the taste for another brand are two very different things. So you will need the judgment to understand what you’re doing for Brand A versus Brand B. You may not agree with what you do for Brand B—that’s not according to your taste—but it’s client work, it’s paying you money. So you have to mould your taste to find that relevant sweet spot. Taste is not a stand-alone objective thing. It is essentially resting on judgment.”

Don’t underestimate the 22-year-old

Shreyas, ever the defender of the hustle, drive, and agency of young designers, essentially argued that they’ll find a way. 

“I don’t think we should underestimate how much initiative they do show. Like, the folks I see, they show a lot of initiative. They go out and network, they make it a point to meet people and hustle their way to their first job. They have a lot more agency than I think this question can make it seem like. It’s not like, oh, I’m this helpless person, who’s going to teach me? If they can do a Hyrox, they can definitely go build their own products—even if it doesn’t have to become a business. Something enough that gives them that first job.”

It’s a fun, (occasionally) combative, and provocative conversation. 

I’d love for you to listen to it and figure out for yourself where you land in the discussion.
