There are two ways to build a beauty brand. There’s the modern way. Launch online, pick a hero ingredient rediscovered on social media, spend aggressively on marketing, grow quickly on e-commerce, and think about profitability later. The other way is much slower.
When Mira Kulkarni started Forest Essentials two decades ago, the idea of an Indian Ayurvedic beauty brand becoming a global luxury name would have sounded optimistic at best. Beauty startups didn’t exist in swarms back then.
Yet Forest Essentials built exactly that. The company opened its first store in Delhi in 2001 and barely added a couple of stores a year for more than a decade. Today, it has about 175 stores, revenue of Rs 584 crore, and net profit margins of roughly 22%, far higher than those of many fast-growing beauty brands.
That slow-burning has just culminated in a milestone moment: Estée Lauder has now fully acquired the brand at a valuation reportedly north of $1 billion.
It’s a remarkable outcome for a company that followed what could be called an anti-growth playbook of focusing on offline retail and building discovery through unlikely channels.
But Aakriti’s [timely and fascinating story](https://the-ken.com/story/how-forest-essentials-chose-anti-growth-to-thrive-in-beauty/?utm_source=newsletter&utm_medium=email&utm_campaign=collection) today also leads to a larger question.
And found a suitor in global major Estée Lauder
In a beauty market now crowded with D2C brands, quick-commerce channels, celebrity founders, and aggressive discounting, what does it take to build a lasting beauty brand? Over the years, we’ve explored that question through many different stories.
If Forest Essentials represents the slow, patient way of building a brand, Minimalist represents another kind of disruption. The Jaipur-based skincare startup managed something few thought possible: it built a cult following in a market long dominated by global giants like L’Oréal and Estée Lauder.
Minimalist did it not with celebrity endorsements or luxury packaging, but with a simple proposition of ingredient transparency and clinical-looking products that appealed to a younger, internet-savvy audience.
In our story on how the four-year-old brand managed to do what even L’Oréal struggled with in India, we saw how Minimalist essentially rewrote the rules of skincare marketing.
For years, legacy brands have either veiled their actives-based products (like Pond's) or shied away from launching them in India (read: L’Oréal). Minimalist broke that mould
But skincare companies weren’t the only ones trying to rewrite the playbook. For decades, the beauty industry assumed that men were not serious consumers of cosmetics. That has been changing.
In one story, we looked at the challenge of selling makeup to men. Something brands have tried before with mixed results. The new generation of companies is approaching the problem differently, designing products specifically for male consumers and trying to strip away the stigma attached to cosmetics.
A slew of brands now sell serums, cleansers, and makeup for men. But how different are they from the countless products already made for women?
That shift is now becoming visible even among large FMCG companies. In another piece, we explored how brands like Godrej and Mamaearth are reviving the men’s cosmetics category, betting that younger consumers are far more comfortable experimenting with beauty products.
What recent acquisitions reveal about the shifts in India’s personal care market
Meanwhile, another battle unfolded in the beauty-products market.
For years, Nykaa dominated India’s online beauty retail market. But that position is increasingly under pressure.
Reliance launched Tira, a beauty platform meant to compete directly with Nykaa’s ecosystem of brands, stores, and logistics. In our story on Tira’s rise, we examined whether the Reliance-backed platform truly has the firepower to disrupt Nykaa’s position, or whether beauty retail is harder to crack than it looks.
Reliance Industries's right of way can’t always be taken for granted
And it’s not just traditional e-commerce platforms entering the fight.
Quick-commerce companies like Blinkit brought the “10-minute delivery” promise to cosmetics as well. That might sound like a novelty, but it fundamentally changes how beauty products are purchased, turning them from considered purchases into impulse buys.
BlinkIt's newest dark stores are meant to be a miniature Nykaas on fast-forward. But everything rests on whether it can change the habits of beauty product users
Perhaps the most telling sign of the industry’s transformation is the number of companies trying to enter it.
Retailers are launching their own beauty lines. FMCG brands are expanding into skincare. Even fast-fashion chains see an opportunity.
In one story, we looked at how Tata’s Zudio, known for selling affordable clothing at scale, made a surprisingly logical move into beauty products, highlighting just how accessible the category has become.
Mamaearth, Mcaffeine, and Zudio have something in common: their manufacturers and a strong focus on marketing
And then there is Mamaearth, arguably the most emblematic brand of the D2C boom.
The company built its reputation online, grew rapidly with performance marketing, and eventually went public amid enormous expectations.
But the journey since then has been far more complicated.
We examined how Mamaearth’s attempt to transform itself into a broader FMCG player has run into friction, because consumers and investors often want very different things from a brand.
Honasa Consumer’s switch from super-stockists to direct distributors left it with dead stock, mounting losses, and shaken investor confidence
In such a market, the Forest Essentials success story feels almost old-fashioned.
