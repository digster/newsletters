The Collection
Fri, 18 Sep 26
Multiple stories, multiple perspectives, one theme worth your time—every week.
Good Morning Ishan,
In 1965, an engineer named Gordon Moore wrote a short paper for a trade magazine predicting that the number of transistors packed onto a chip would roughly double every year. It was an observation so central to how the semiconductor industry sees itself that it went beyond just being an observation.
It became Moore’s Law.
Sixty-one years of doubling later, on his own 76th birthday yesterday, Indian Prime Minister Narendra Modi stood up at Yashobhoomi in Delhi to inaugurate Semicon India 2026 in front of 600-odd companies from 52 countries, and said that India’s semiconductor journey has “moved from policy discussions to commercial production”, with chips made in India now reaching customers at home and abroad.
It is a good line for a good day.
But keep Moore’s law in the back of your head while you listen to it, because the whole point of his prediction was that standing still, even briefly, means falling further behind than it looks like from where you are standing.
So the question is which chips? And whether those customers are the ones the rest of the world is currently fighting over.
Take the one government-owned fab the country already has, since it is the best possible test of the gap between announcement and delivery.[ ](https://the-ken.com/story/indias-only-chip-fab-is-getting-rs-4500-crore-the-shopping-list-is-clearer-than-the-mission/)
We reported last month that the Semi-Conductor Laboratory, on its umpteenth attempted revamp after decades of false starts, had been promised Rs 4,500 crore, a 100X jump in capacity, and a leap to 28-nanometre chips. What it is actually getting is a 3X capacity bump and a technology ceiling still parked at 180 nanometres—several generations behind the chips now powering frontier AI systems.
Sure, SCL’s chips serve real customers, ISRO and the DRDO among them, in categories like power management and industrial control that were never going to need anything sharper. But it shows what “commercial production” may mean here in practice.
The upgrade strengthens what SCL already does, including catering to agencies and prototyping for students, rather than the 100X expansion and 28nm chips the government had promised
The design side of the mission has outperformed the fabrication side, which somehow makes the mismatch more interesting.[ ](https://the-ken.com/newsletter/make-india-competitive-again/india-semiconductor-mission-2-0-has-a-supply-plan-the-demand-gap-remains/)
We wrote in July about the design-linked incentive scheme’s wins, 24 chip-design startups backed, EDA tool access extended to more than a hundred companies, and 23 completed tapeouts, including Netra Semiconductor’s A2000 AI processor, taped out on TSMC’s 12-nanometre node, a properly advanced result for an Indian design house.
But a director at Karnataka’s Semiconductor Fabless Accelerator Lab made a point. “There is sufficient demand, as signified by the amount of semiconductors we are importing,” he said. India wants advanced chips badly enough to pay someone else to make them. Wanting them and being able to fabricate them domestically are two entirely different achievements. And the mission has moved much faster on the first than the second.
A more integrated incentive system across the value chain can close the loop
A fabless company needs at least US$10 million to build a basic chip; that’s far above the limit usually made available for them
So, who is actually running India’s AI workloads on the most advanced silicon available right now? We reported in May that Nvidia’s most advanced AI chips go to only two companies in India, Reliance Jio and Tata Communications. The global chip giant rations its best hardware to companies with the network, the data-centre relationships, and the enterprise customers to use it at scale, and in India that happens to be a club of two.
Every genuinely frontier AI workload running in this country right now is, by definition, running on imported silicon, gatekept by a couple of companies.
Tata Communications has Nvidia’s most advanced chips, a growing AI-infrastructure stack, and enterprise demand. Its stock is still down 16%
India’s most serious attempt to close that gap is still years away from mattering to AI compute. We covered, also in May, Tata Electronics’ Rs 91,000-crore fabrication plant going up in Dholera, the single largest semiconductor bet India has ever made, running alongside a smaller, more piecemeal effort from the Murugappa Group, whose chip venture, Axiro Semiconductor, contributed just Rs 500 crore to parent CG Power’s revenue in FY26, something like 4% of the total.
Even the boldest fab plan currently under construction isn’t remotely close to touching the AI-compute conversation Modi’s speech was borrowing its credibility from.
While Tata is trying to build a self-contained chipmaking ecosystem from the ground up, Murugappa is acquiring disparate pieces and stacking them together
Back in 2021, we reported that India was, for the third time in 20 years, shopping around for a serious chip manufacturing partner, dangling deep financial incentives while the actual global fab operators, TSMC, Intel, Samsung, stayed conspicuously absent from the queue. A consultant close to those talks put it to us: everyone wanted a piece of the pie except the companies that were actually going to build anything.
foundry.gif
With 25% in grants and more, India sees a semiconductor plant as a financial play. Big chip companies see the unproven territory and tendering as a showstopper. The political leadership can pick up the phone and get a deal, but there’s also a simpler path
Five years and many announcements later, India finally has serious semiconductor manufacturing and packaging projects under construction, along with actual domestic tapeouts to point to. That is real progress, and deserves to be called that.
It just isn’t the same achievement as being ready for the specific moment the chip industry is living through right now. Yesterday’s applause asked everyone to forget the difference.
Micron is the only reality check, though. Its Sanand plant is already producing memory modules commercially, and its first shipment went to Dell for laptops made in India. So, yes, India is making chips that are reaching customers. The wafers, though, were made somewhere else.
But the government needs more deals with companies like Micron to realise its dreams of semiconductor-fabrication plants
