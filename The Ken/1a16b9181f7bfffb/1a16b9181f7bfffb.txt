‘4’ is a perfectly good number. eMudhra’s investors beg to differ. A fault in 1% of eMudhra’s business has wiped more than a third off its market value in a week. What investors fear is the other 99%

Dan Davies, an investment-bank analyst who spent years studying corporate disasters, has a name for the kind of system where everyone is responsible for making sure something gets done, right up until something goes wrong. Then responsibility disappears into the furniture. He calls it an “accountability sink” in his book The Unaccountability Machine. Independent audits are an elegant example. 

eMudhra, a listed Bengaluru company, sells trust in digital form. Legally valid digital signatures and the certificates that put the reassuring little padlock in your browser. It says its certificate authorities passed independent audits in two consecutive years. What could possibly go wrong?

Its shares are down more than a third this week, and the company has said it will withdraw from the global website-certificate business, which it says is about 1% of its revenue. That must be 100% headache, then.

eMudhra, which vouches that a site really is your bank and not an impostor, does it with a private key. That’s a secret number used to sign its vouchers; whoever learns it can sign as that company. For the kind eMudhra used, a good key is about 116 digits long, long enough that guessing it would outlast the sun. The keys are created in a formal event called a key ceremony, the point in a key’s life when you would expect to check the result. eMudhra says a flaw in its April 2025 ceremony produced a second, weaker set of keys alongside the intended ones, and that staff used the wrong set. Mozilla found all eight affected keys, six for websites and two for signing software, were below 256 bits.

One of them was 4. Not a four-digit number. Just the number 4!! Keys that small can be worked out from the public certificates in under a second.

A researcher did, and reported them on 17 October to Apple, Google, Microsoft, and Mozilla, who acted within hours. Chrome blocked the affected certificates the same day, Firefox that evening, then the others. Nobody has shown that anyone used the keys, and the damage to a certificate authority does not need an attacker. It comes from what a bad key says about everything else the company does when nobody is watching. The shares are pricing that.

Mutasim and Sakshi reported in May that AI models like Anthropic’s Mythos have cut the time an attacker needs to under 10 hours, while Indian companies take about 180 days to patch a bug. The flaw was in the system for a year and a half and was blocked in hours by four American companies that aren’t eMudhra’s customers and that eMudhra couldn’t have stopped.

They could act alone because they make the browsers, and each browser ships with a list of certificate authorities it trusts and will accept a certificate from any of them for any website in the world. One weak authority is a weakness for all of them, so the companies that maintain the list are the real regulators of the business, whatever the licensing says. Indian policy tends to run on the opposite instinct, that what the government controls is safer.

In April, Inderpal reported that Meity had moved about 1.7 million government email accounts to Zoho in a deal worth about Rs 180 crore, putting the government on a closed, proprietary stack it says is safer, in a year when the global trend is toward open source.

For email, that is arguable. For trust, it has been tried. In July 2014, Google found unauthorised certificates for several of its domains, issued through the National Informatics Centre, which held intermediate certificates (sub-authorities licensed to issue vouchers for a bigger one) under the India CCA, the Controller of Certifying Authorities, the government office that licenses certificate companies in India. Its root certificate, the top-level one browsers trust directly, is the government’s own. Google blocked the certificates and limited that root in Chrome to seven domains, so the Indian government’s root can now vouch for only those seven. In 2023, the EU proposed going the other way, and its eIDAS rules would have stopped browsers from distrusting certificates from government-approved authorities.

The browsers’ system has a cost, and it falls on the sites that can least afford it. Chrome is due to remove its trust in eMudhra’s affected root, the top-level certificate that the company’s other certificates hang from, on 27 October, and any site on one of its certificates has to replace it before then or show visitors a warning. Reuters lists Infosys, TCS, and India’s income tax portal among eMudhra’s certificate customers, though it does not say which were affected. The industry is also moving to certificates that last 47 days instead of 398, a schedule the CA/Browser Forum, where the browser makers and authorities set the rules, voted for in April 2025, partly because revoking bad certificates has stopped working. Every site has to renew roughly 8X as often, and the ones least likely to cope are small.

Soumyajit reported in 2022 that more than 70% of Indian SMEs had suffered a cyberattack in the previous year, and described managers at a Belagavi equipment plant, hit by spyware, who had no firewall or logs and asked why anything needed fixing. They won’t renew certificates by hand every 47 days.

Revocation really has stopped working. eMudhra did revoke the eight certificates on 18 October, which is the authority’s version of a product recall, and it changed very little, because browsers mostly don’t check for recalls. The check used to be slow, told the authority which sites you were visiting, and let you through anyway when the server didn’t answer, so it failed exactly when an attacker wanted it to. Browsers now push their own lists of banned certificates, Mozilla’s OneCRL and Chrome’s CRLSets, and those lists stopped eMudhra’s keys.

What has actually kept bad certificates in check is cheaper than control. Chrome has required every certificate to be recorded in public logs since 2018, and Safari since later that year, though Firefox does not, so a forged certificate either fails in the browser or leaves a public record, and reports say none from the six website authorities had appeared in the logs when Chrome blocked them. 

A public log is the opposite of an accountability sink, because nobody can assume checks. It also means the browser makers’ refusal to give governments special powers protects an Indian user from the Indian state about as well as from a foreign one. 

I doubt that was the aim. It is how it came out.

The faults in this story were all found by outsiders. A researcher found this one a year and a half after the ceremony, and after the audits, and Google found NIC’s in 2014. In 2021, Recorded Future and The New York Times reported the first warning of Chinese-linked malware in Mumbai’s power grid, and Pratap reported that IIT Kanpur’s Sandeep Shukla said Indian agencies took 17 days to block the IP addresses on the list, when 24 to 48 hours would have been right. This time, Meity formed https://theprint.in/tech/meity-sets-up-team-to-probe-emudhra-certificate-breach-says-digital-signature-services-unaffected/3078125/ an investigation team four days after the report, and CERT-In issued an advisory on 21 October, faster than 17 days but slower than every browser maker.

eMudhra says its Indian digital-signature services run on separate infrastructure and are unaffected, a claim about the machines. The doubt is about the company’s process. India does have a licence to act on it. Under the IT Act, the CCA can suspend a certifying authority’s licence for up to 10 days on reasonable cause, and revoke it after a hearing if the authority breaks the licence or the law, and the digital-signature business runs under that licence. Trust services, which include digital signatures as well as website certificates, were about 20% of revenue in the first nine months of this fiscal year, and enterprise software was 57.6%. In FY25, 62% of its Rs 528 crore revenue came from abroad https://emudhra.com/investor-assets/investors/esg-profile/fy2025/Q4_Results_Press_Release.pdf, from customers including banks, a Middle Eastern customs authority, and Fortune 100 firms, all buying from a company whose product is being trusted.

My guess is that the market is pricing in whether those customers keep believing, and whether the Indian regulator follows the foreign ones. It is a guess, but I have not seen a better explanation for why 1% of the business moved the whole share price.

The same inference may hurt other Indian companies, because trust gets judged by category as well as by company. Pratap also reported in 2019 that government procurement had long favoured global players, and that Indian cybersecurity firms “know the deck is still stacked against them”. A procurement officer choosing between an Indian vendor and a foreign one now has a safe reason to pick the foreign one, and does not need to have read anything about eMudhra to do it.

The same dependence is older than browsers. The Supreme Court’s 2018 Aadhaar ruling sucked away the fuel powering India Stack, as Rohin wrote, and in 2017 Anand Venkatanarayanan, a cybersecurity researcher who was an expert witness in the Supreme Court’s Aadhaar case and now co-founder of the Deepstrat think tank, argued that the breaches were happening at the periphery. A key ceremony sits there too: a room, a script and a checklist, run once, around a number that has to be long.
