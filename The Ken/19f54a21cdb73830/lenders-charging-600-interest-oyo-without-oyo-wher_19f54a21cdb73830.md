[At RBI-licensed NBFCs, 600% annual interest is board-approved, ‘reasonable’, and perfectly legal](https://the-ken.com/story/at-rbi-licensed-nbfcs-600-annual-interest-is-board-approved-reasonable-and-perfectly-legal/?utm_source=weekly_email&utm_medium=email&utm_campaign=weekly_recap_card)
The RBI chose not to set a ceiling on interest rates. Now, NBFCs want to see just how far they can push it
RBI-licensed NBFCs are running short-term personal loan apps at annualised rates of 600% and higher, using the regulator’s 2022 decision not to cap interest rates. Across a sample of 25 such NBFCs—most incorporated in the 1980s and 1990s—revenues rose by a median of 4.5X between FY24 and FY25. In their own board-approved policies, one of these NBFCs claims a cost of borrowing of up to 120% a year, while their audited filings show finance costs closer to 2%. Most of these NBFCs fall below the RBI ombudsman’s jurisdictional threshold; in at least one documented case, the fallback grievance cell found a violation and closed the case with only a warning. Three companies lease India its AI compute. Nvidia supplies the chips to all of them—then comes for what they sell, one software release at a time
Three companies—Yotta, E2E, and Neysa—sell GPU compute in India. They all buy chips from Nvidia. The latter also designs the blueprint for how cloud companies build, the software that runs their GPUs, and now, even a marketplace that decides who finds them. Each company bet on something different to stand out—Yotta on scale, Neysa on custom software, E2E on price—but Nvidia is slowly absorbing all of those advantages. It could lead to a scenario where the market stops being three real choices and turns into one default giant plus a race to the bottom on price.
