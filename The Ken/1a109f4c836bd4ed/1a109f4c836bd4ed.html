N Chandrasekaran, the chairman of Tata Sons, has visited Air India’s Delhi headquarters about half a dozen times this year. And he’s a busy man, what with all the boardroom drama precipitated by his running feud with Tata Trusts chief Noel Tata.

“It tells you how important Air India is, not just from a commercial or financial lens but even from a brand perspective,” an airline executive said. “It’s the show horse.”

A horse that’s proving prohibitively expensive to keep and run: Air India’s consolidated losses widened to Rs 22,000 crore in FY26 from Rs 10,900 crore the previous year, dealing another blow to a carrier battered by the Ahmedabad crash and a series of aircraft-safety and pilot-discipline incidents.

So Chandrasekaran is rightly worried. As is Singapore Airlines.

The state-backed Southeast Asian airline became a shareholder in Air India in November 2024, when it signed an agreement with Tata Sons to merge Vistara into Air India, taking a 25.1% stake. Despite being only a minority owner, it’s putting in serious cash and effort to turn the Indian airline around.

It has booked operating losses of $780 million over the two years of its investment and has now been asked to furnish a sizeable portion of the $1.5 billion Air India wants in fresh equity.

It’s inclined to commit the capital, but with strings attached…
