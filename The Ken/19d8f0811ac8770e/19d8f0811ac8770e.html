It’s a rare financial alchemy. In one stroke, the Adani Group, which won approval on 10 April to acquire the bankrupt Jaiprakash Associates’s assets, vaults itself into entirely new businesses and solidifies some old ones.

The Rs 14,535-crore ($1.6 billion) bid will catalyse the transmutation. It’ll bring in luxury hotel properties, control of India’s only Formula 1 track, and huge land parcels in the national capital region. It’ll also plonk the group into the number two position in the cement market, creating a formidable duopoly in key regions, besides adding immediate cash flow to its energy business.

Size compounds relentlessly in India. A bunch of economists in India and abroad have recently documented how, in the first two decades of this century, Indian family business groups have diversified and cemented their hold on the economy. Just 25 family groups accounted for nearly a fifth of India’s GDP, far more than is seen in advanced economies. 

But in the five years to 2025, this feature of the Indian economy—which some economists have called a bug—has become more pronounced. Parsing the corporate activities of six family groups—Reliance, Adani, Tatas, Bharti, Aditya Birla, and OP Jindal—it’s clear that each one of them expanded into newer sectors more aggressively than they did in the five years prior.

Earlier in March, McKinsey & Co identified this as “conglomerate 3.0”, one of the “breakthrough models shaping global growth”. What the consultancy calls “ecosystem-based organisations”, economists call concentration. In India, it also boils down to using the earnings of old, regulated businesses to fund new-economy ventures…
