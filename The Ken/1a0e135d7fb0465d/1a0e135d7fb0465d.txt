Good morning Ishan, I know many of us either take the magic of internet search for granted or rue its AI slopification. But I feel that we’re in a new golden era of accurate search. 

Last Sunday I was sitting and reading a book as our gardener was sweeping away all the dead leaves and detritus that had accumulated over the week. It had rained overnight too. In that moment I was hit by a fragrance that was sweeter than petrichor! What was it? 

In an instant, I had triangulated the explanation from two different sources—Google’s Gemini and Anthropic’s Claude.

It turns out three things were happening all at once. Here’s the answer (not my words).

– Geosmin, made by soil bacteria (mainly Streptomyces). It’s the earthy, beetroot-like note, and the human nose picks it up at a few parts per trillion. Wet soil is full of it, and scraping a stiff broom across the surface breaks up soil crumbs and flings up damp particles, which releases far more of it than rain alone.

– Green leaf volatiles from the bruised grass—cis-3-hexenal and cis-3-hexenol, the same compounds behind the smell of a freshly mown lawn. Rain-swollen blades rupture easily under bristles.

– Damp organic matter and the broom itself. Coir or bamboo fibres, plus rotting leaf litter, add their own musty layer.

I also found out that what I was smelling wasn’t petrichor (the smell of fresh rain). The petrichor phenomenon plays out when rain falls on dry ground. Falling raindrops trap air bubbles as they hit the ground, which in turn capture geosmin and plant oils, which explode as aerosols as they rise into the air. Our human noses can apparently detect geosmin at concentrations of parts per trillion!

But after a night of rain, most of the plant oils have been washed away, so what I was inhaling was geosmin and crushed grass and dead leaves all stirred up by a stiff broomstick. 

Then, early Friday morning, I saw a bird with bright green feathers and a long kingfisher-like beak hopping about on the nearby banyan tree. I searched for “green feathers bird Kasavanahalli” and had my guy in 30 seconds. It was a white-cheeked barbet https://ebird.org/species/whcbar1/L5557850.

Earlier this morning, I took Gabru for a walk around Kasavanahalli lake (which was full, after the rains) and spotted cormorants, waterhens, moorhens, swamphens, kingfishers, ducks, egrets, herons, and a nature photographer shooting what appeared to be a coiled and slim snake at first, but really was a bird (I realised why the Oriental darter is also called a “snakebird”).

I found a site where people log https://ebird.org/hotspot/L5557850/bird-list?rank=hc bird sightings at the lake I was at. Honestly, it felt like magic. 

I’m from an era where, first, we had no search (you browsed directories and listings to find new websites), then we did, but it sucked, then came Google, which made search ubiquitous, but then everything collapsed under ads and pages upon pages of noise.

Things have really improved once again. If you’re the curious type, it’s a great time to be alive!

Oh, and Gabru says hi, since many of you have been curious to know what the guy I write so often about looks like 🙂

Unlearning and relearning autumns

I must start by admitting that our Autumn Playlist project flummoxed me. As I write this, 102 of you have responded to the community survey. So much of what we urban, English-speaking Indians think of as autumn is just imagination.

Red and brown leaves. Trees shedding. The orange colour. The fall word. Cold weather. Sweaters. Coziness. 

We learned most of those visual cues over the years from our media diets. 

“I think of Autumn more in terms of what happens in the west, not so much in the Indian context.” — Sonali, Mumbai

“Autumn in New York… autumn always reminds me of this song though I have never been to New York in autumn.” — anonymous, Bengaluru

“Very stereotypically, I would answer like the leaves of the trees and their shapes, but I feel like the pacing of the day kind of slows down, not as slow as it gets in winter but slows down nevertheless.” — Aarav, Goa

Fortunately, I asked all of you about what you “feel” in autumn, and what you actually “see”. There was a very sharp divergence between them. Our feelings were mostly byproducts of our media diets, but our sights were both grounded and diverse. 

When asked what we saw around us in autumn, the most common answer was about the light and the sun and, specifically, 5 p.m. (so many of you said “the sky at 5 pm” verbatim and unprompted!). We notice that “it gets dark earlier”, “the sun begins to set sooner”, “early sunsets”, and “sunset hues”.

“The sky gives the first sign, the early setting sun and light grey clouds moving without any downpour” — Sam, Shillong

“The sun begins to set sooner, and the pace of life seems to slow down a bit” — Talzeem, Mumbai

“Evening sunlight” — Anshul Borde, Surat

The second most commonly cited observation was both hilarious and revealing—the heat! Venkat called it Bengaluru’s “second summer”.

Mumbaikars were near unanimous about the return of “oppressive heat” (Sonali) after the retreat of the rains. 

And yes, rains. Most of us in Bengaluru can’t decide if autumn is about the rain, heat, both or neither!

“It’s neither monsoon nor winter. Some days both” — Suyash, Bangalore

“Warm mornings, cool evenings, a hint of rain but none around, pink skies in the evening, and a gentle breeze rolling those dry leaves around” — Akshay Patel, Indore

“Overcast skies and evening rains” — GeeVee, Bengaluru

Do you see where I’m going with this? How much of what we see and experience is reflected in how we imagine autumn to be?

I did not realise flowers and fragrance play such an important role in our autumns. “Ironic to ‘fall’, a plant that flowers during autumn tells me about its arrival,” said Vidhi from Hyderabad. I assumed she was referring to night-blooming jasmine, also called parijat, harsingar, shefali, and shiuli.

“Shiuli phool early in the morning on the cars parked beside the park” — Deb, New Delhi

“The aroma of shiuli & parijat” — Sunaina, Mumbai

“Change in the morning light, smell of shefali flowers” — Santanu, Kolkata

“A fragrance of well-being” — Su Nayanæ, Kashmir

“The smell changes, wind is a little sweet on your cheeks” — Shresth Mour, Guwahati

And the beautiful white flowers of the wild sugarcane, or kash, immortalised by the late Satyajit Ray in Pather Panchali https://www.telegraphindia.com/opinion/where-the-grass-is-white/cid/1707275. Here, take a few minutes and take in the unfiltered, sweeping yet emotional beauty https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MC8waqiwSY4 of the scene. 

“Blooming of kaash phool in the plains as far as one can see” — Kaushik Debnath, Agartala

“‘Kash’ flowers, shefali flowers and arrival tidings of Durga Puja” — Santanu, Kolkata

Deeksha referred to the “scent of those white flowers that line roads,” by which I will assume she means the incredibly (perhaps even oppressively) sweet-smelling flowers of the devil’s tree, also known as blackboard tree, Saptaparni or Chhatim. 

If there is an official set of autumn medalist flowers from India, it is surely Shiuli, Chhatim, and Kash (you can decide which one deserves the gold and the bronze).

That they’re all from India’s eastern states was another revelation. Longtime reader and contributor Liby Johnson described it best.

I grew up in southern India, where the seasonal variations were much less distinct. The six seasons were something I had mostly read about in school textbooks. Only after spending many, many autumn days in Bihar, Jharkhand, West Bengal and Odisha did I begin to develop a sense of what autumn is. I began to notice the changing light, the open skies and ripening fields, the kash phool appearing across the landscape, and the anticipation of Durga Puja. Over the years, this is what autumn has come to mean to me.

Which brings me to festivals! If “fall” in the West is associated with the anticipation of melancholy, in India it is the opposite—the “beginning of our festivals”. Ganpati pandals (makeshift tents), Durga Puja, Garba, and Navratras. 

“Autumn in India is festival season” — Maragathavalli, Bengaluru

“Everyone is off to their native places, and nothing gets done till Diwali” — Pavithra Solai, Bengaluru

“Early sunsets, a little windy, festival times” — Abhishek, Udaipur

“Change in light, festivities, warmer clothes” — Namita Pasbola, Mumbai

“The beginning of Durga Puja, the ultimate reminder of autumn growing up” — T, Delhi

Among the other recurring themes were the early chill in the air (“The classic tinge in the air, slight chill after sunsets” — Deeksha, Delhi; “Needing a shawl in the early mornings and evenings, earlier sunsets” — Lakshmi Raman, Bengaluru; “Onset of light chilliness during morning and evening walks” — Dipti, Darjeeling), beautiful and clear skies and bird songs (also Akshat, Gurgaon; PK, Pune). 

Why is our experience of autumn so different from our imagination of it? Well, for one, India’s climate. September–October for many of us is a post-monsoon season. The trees are lush, there are flowers, our lakes are brimming. It’s nothing like “fall”.

The classical name for this season—sharad—celebrates clear skies, harvests, and the full moon. Fall, in many parts, takes place in February–March. It’s called patjhad, literally “leaves fall”. September isn’t fall.

Then there are the variances across India because of how and when the monsoon withdraws over the country (the same way its arrival and expansion play out differently from Keralam to West Bengal). The monsoon’s withdrawal is sharpest in the East, hence our most lush and vivid autumns start there (like how their monsoon counterparts start in Keralam). West Bengal is our New England, so to speak, but with Clearing instead of Fall. As the rains withdraw, some of us get a second summer or second monsoon (September is historically Bengaluru’s wettest month), while others get nothing to write home about.

So, if we had to construct an Indian aesthetic for autumn, it would be flowers, festivals, skies, rains, sun, and clearing.

Phew! That was a lot to take in and write about. I have no time left to do justice to the playlist. I’ll publish it the coming Sunday? 

“The Chrysanthemums” 

Hello, this is Tanim, and today I want to recommend a short story. You can read it here https://ol.scc.spokane.edu/jroth/Courses/English%20102/01%20SPRING%202015/the_chrysanthemums_by_john_steinbeck.pdf. And if you have a short-story recommendation that makes for a good Sunday read, you can share it here https://theken.typeform.com/FPBooks?typeform-source=the-ken.com. 

In case you were wondering what the namesake looks like

John Steinbeck’s “The Chrysanthemums” is set against the backdrop of the Great Depression. It captures the uncertainty and desperation of an era while offering a timeless meditation on gender inequality and the human need for purpose.

Steinbeck opens with a deliberately bleak landscape. A closed-off valley shrouded in fog, much like the early Bengaluru mornings. But here, the fog represents the confusion and hopelessness of Depression-era America, and it also serves as a metaphor for the fog of societal expectations that envelops our protagonist, Elisa Allen.

What makes “The Chrysanthemums” particularly brilliant is Steinbeck’s careful construction of a social hierarchy through seemingly minor details. The businessmen from Western Meat Company arrive in a Ford coupe, which is a symbol of corporate power and mobility. Henry Allen, Elisa’s husband, owns a ranch and orchard but drives a car of unnamed make, suggesting modest means. The Tinker, a wandering repairman, lives and works from his worn-out caravan, occupying the lowest rung of this economic ladder.

Steinbeck assigns each character a workspace that reveals their agency. The businessmen remain outside the fence, detached and anonymous. Henry commands the orchard and makes economic decisions unilaterally. Elisa is confined to her garden, enclosed by a fence that represents the restrictions patriarchy imposes on women.

Steinbeck joined the League of American Writers in 1935 and was deeply influenced by the political and social upheavals of his time. His communist leanings and sensitivity to class struggle permeate this work, though they never overwhelm the intimate, human story at its centre.

In a few pages, the author reminds us that powerful stories don’t need hundreds of pages, and that a woman’s garden, however beautiful, can also be a prison.
