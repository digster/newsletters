Good morning Ishan, tell me, what are your views on the word “apprentice”?
You see, I was grappling with the title for a new role I wanted to hire for. One directly related to First Principles, both the newsletter and the podcast.
The newsletter is now 145 editions in, going out every Sunday since May 2023. The podcast is 54 episodes deep, with close to 110 hours of long-form conversations with founders and operators building India’s most interesting companies.
I started both. I still write the lede for the newsletter every week, with my colleagues Tanim, Sakshi, and Vishnu pitching in with their sections. I still host the podcast. I plan to keep doing both for a long time.
But FP is now becoming too big for one person to run on the side of their day job, but still small enough to still feel like a project rather than a vertical. That, in my experience, is exactly the moment to bring in someone new.
The next two to three years are about turning FP from “Rohin’s thing” into something with a sharper editorial point of view, a wider footprint, more frequent and more varied formats, and probably one or two products we haven’t invented yet. I’m looking for a partner in that work.
I cycled through a bunch of names for this role. Associate, Founding Associate, Editorial & Production Associate, FP Producer, FP Producer & Editor, FP Chief of Staff, FP All-Rounder, Apprentice in Residence.
I rejected some for being inaccurate (you cannot be “founding” unless you were there at the, well, founding), some for being too narrow (producer), some for attracting title-chasers (chief of staff), and some for being vague and pompous (all-rounder).
The word that I wrestled with a lot was what I finally picked: “apprentice.”
As a journalist and a writer, I like words to convey what they mean. I wanted to hire someone who would learn, work, and grow alongside me. Over time, they’d attain mastery over the craft of building, shaping, and scaling media products.
But we, as Indians, also tend to have a dim view of apprenticeships. We think of them as things meant only for blue-collar workers or artisans, not knowledge or creative professions.
Thus, my question. We’ll know in a few weeks. (Oh, the job description is a few sections down, in today’s newsletter.)

“Of all the cities I have lived in, Bengaluru is the loneliest.
All my friends here are lonely despite the company.
I blame it on the city somehow.
I have lived in different cities. Patna, Paris, Dhanbad-Delhi-Dharamshala, Mumbai, Manali, Glasgow, Goa but none felt this lonely.”
Thus began[ a Linkedin post](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/harshsnehanshu_of-all-the-cities-i-have-lived-in-bengaluru-share-7454401197706215424-2Rp8/) written by Harsh Snehanshu, shared a few days ago by a friend.
I can’t deny there wasn’t some element of truth in it. And I say this not just as a Bengaluru resident of…<counts in my head>…29 years, but also as a diehard lover of the city. There is absolutely no other city in India I’d rather live in.
Then, another friend articulated a point of view that was hard to dismiss. He visualised Bengaluru as a “factory city” or [“monotown”](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monotown).
But once you see Bangalore as a factory city, it makes a lot more sense.
Bangalore (2010 onwards) has rapidly evolved – in fact in some sense I would not count anything that existed pre 2010 into this view – into a city entirely geared around a particular industry. The only reason to now be in Bangalore is to participate in this industry. For that reason, anything that contradicts that is deprioritized.
The second large factor is that there’s next to no public transport. In a typical city where multiple classes of people had to live together, good networks of public transport allowed cultural producers or people who didn’t participate in the primary industry to continue to afford to be a part of and participate in urban life. In BLR, if you aren’t part of tech, you’re being priced out. You do not have a community of any kind of cultural producers, as they’re completely priced out by the city.
As it turns out, I came across these views as I was midway through Stanford psychologist and professor Jamil Zaki’s wonderful book, Hope for Cynics.
Now, I don’t count myself as a cynical person. I tend to see the glass as half-full most times. Thus, many parts of Zaki’s book were preaching to my choir.
But I liked his explanation of why so many people find cynicism appealing, albeit for the wrong reasons!
And that cynics usually engage in social comparison and attempts to pull others down, stemming from their own lack of beliefs and self-worth.
(As you can guess, today’s edition is largely based on the book and my highlights from it.)
If you’re wondering where I’m going with this, here’s where it got interesting. Zaki references research from two fishing communities just 30 miles apart in northeastern Brazil to explain the social roots of cynicism and trust.
One of the villages was set along a lake, and the other one next to the ocean.
The fishermen by the lake end up working alone and competing with each other for the best spots each day. They see each day as “zero-sum” and everyone else as a rival, thus becoming more suspicious, greedy, and distrustful of others over time.
In comparison, the fishermen living next to the ocean have to cooperate with each other to work larger boats and survive at sea. Trust and collaboration become their defaults.
Zaki argues that our environments, neighbourhoods, workplaces, or even countries, can turn us into cynical or trusting people.
This is when I was reminded of a literal sentence related to an ocean city from Harsh Snehanshu’s Linkedin post.
“The city provides no inspiration for idling, for doing nothing. Like having chuski at India Gate at midnight or sitting by the sea by oneself that Marine Drive offers.
The infinity of the sea in Mumbai makes you travel in space, and the historical monuments of Delhi makes you travel in time, offering a temporary escape—the precipice for rumination.”
Completely different connotations, but I couldn’t help notice the parallels.
Now, isn’t it doubly interesting when you think of Bengaluru as a literal “lake town”? I mean, this is the [“city of lakes”](https://reasonstobecheerful.world/bengaluru-india-city-of-lakes-revival/)!
I am not trying to read too much into this, but I just found the analogies intersecting on various planes.
Zaki goes on to explain how cynicism feeds off our perceptions of others, which are often completely at odds with their reality.
A key theme running across the book is how skepticism is a superior approach to cynicism. This is a key distinction. I’ve had dozens of people over the years accuse journalists of being cynics, and I always tell them, “Not cynics, but skeptics.”
Skepticism teaches you to ask questions and be prepared to change your mind if the answers are contrary to what you expected.
I’ll end with this bit towards the end of the book.
Perhaps it was the[ Baader-Meinhof phenomenon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frequency_illusion) that was making me notice all sorts of patterns around lake towns and zero-sum thinking, but another professor—Stefanie Stantcheva, an economics professor at Harvard—also had a column about the zero-sum mindset in the [latest edition](https://www.economist.com/by-invitation/2025/07/07/to-understand-america-today-study-the-zero-sum-mindset-writes-stefanie-stantcheva) of The Economist. It made me think we were all slip-sliding towards living in a lake world.
Some groups are more likely than others to see the world in zero-sum terms. People in cities, for example, tend to think this way more than those in rural areas, perhaps because urban life involves intense competition for housing and jobs. People with more formal education are less likely to see the world as zero-sum but the pattern flips among the highly educated: those with PhDs often show the strongest zero-sum beliefs. This could be because competitive graduate programmes attract people who already think this way or because they encourage this mindset through intense competition. Perhaps the most striking finding is that younger generations today are much more zero-sum than older ones.
[…]
To understand the zero-sum mindset—and why it varies across groups—it is necessary to look at where it comes from. This way of thinking doesn’t just appear out of nowhere; it stems from people’s economic environment and experiences—not only their own, but also those of their families and even earlier generations. Economic mobility and growth play a big role. People who have done better than their parents, or whose families have experienced upward mobility over time, are less likely to think in zero-sum terms. This also explains why younger generations in America are more zero-sum: they have grown up during times of slower economic growth and lower mobility. Other rich countries exhibit a similar generational pattern. In contrast, in many poorer countries where younger generations have experienced more growth than earlier ones, the pattern flips.

Come build FP with me!
Hi, I’m Rohin Dharmakumar. I’m the co-founder and CEO of The Ken, and the person who started both First Principles, the podcast, and First Principles, the newsletter.
I’m hiring my first full-time FP colleague.
This is the only job description I’ll write for it. No third-party recruiter, no HR template, no buzzword bingo. I’d rather just tell you what this job actually is, who I think it’s for, and what you’d be walking into.
What you’d actually do
In a single week, you might commission and produce a podcast episode; convince a CEO’s PR team into a recording slot; do the prep so well that the guest opens up in ways they didn’t plan to; sit in on the recording; work with our editors to ship a clean cut; pull our distribution numbers and tell me what they actually mean (and what we should do about them); pitch me a new format you’ve been thinking about; argue with me when I’m pursuing the wrong one; and bring back two or three perspectives from people you met that week that I would never have heard otherwise.
You’d own the FP calendar, which means special editions, themed episodes, quarter-ahead planning, and be the nucleus that everyone else (writers, editors, sound engineers, designers, product managers, occasionally me) orbits around when they need to know what’s coming.
You’d be the person who walks in on Monday morning and says: have you seen this?
You won’t be great at all of these on day one. Nobody is. The job is to be willing, and to compound at each of them faster than you’ve ever compounded at anything before.
What I’m looking for
I don’t care which college you went to, what your GPA was, or whether you’ve ever set foot inside a media company. I do care, deeply, about a small set of things.
You read like an addict. You finish books. You have opinions about Substack writers and podcast hosts. You’ve gone down rabbit holes nobody asked you to.
You have taste. You can tell the difference between a great podcast episode and a merely competent one, and you can tell me why in a sentence.
You’re the most organised person in your friend group. The one who actually books the table, sends the calendar invite (I love calendar invites!), and follows up on the boring thing nobody else wants to chase down.
You’ve shipped something. A newsletter, a podcast, a college magazine, a club, an event, a Notion site, a Youtube channel that lasted longer than three videos. Anything where you were the nucleus rather than a passenger. I’m looking for evidence of agency, not casual enthusiasm.
You write well. Not perfectly, but well. Voice, rhythm, tightness. You’d rather cut three lines than write three more.
You’re easy to work with, but not a pushover. You’ll need to push back on me, on guests, and on people much older than you. Often.
If you’re reading this list and quietly checking most of it off, consider applying.
What this isn’t
If your dream is to write a column with your name on it from day one, this is not that job.
If you want defined hours, a tidy role description, and predictable weeks, this is not that job either.
If you’ve already decided journalism is dying and you’d rather “do content”, please go do that. I’d rather hire someone who’s a little obsessed with this craft than someone who’s settling for it.
What you’d get out of it
This is the closest thing to a media apprenticeship that exists in India.
In two or three years here, you will have reported, produced, and shipped real work to a real audience that pays for it. You’ll have built relationships with founders, operators, and investors who don’t take random meetings. You’ll have learned how a media product works as a business, not just as a craft. You’ll have developed taste by working next to someone who has very strong opinions about it. And you’ll have a public body of work you can point to.
Wherever you go after this—running your own thing, leading editorial somewhere, building a media product inside a company, or staying on at FP and helping me build whatever it becomes next—you’ll have a head start that’s hard to acquire any other way.
The salary will be competitive for someone under two years in. But it won’t be the reason you take this job.
Experience and location
Ideally under two years of full-time work experience. Three years is a hard line. I want potential and ambition, not experience and polish.
The role is going to be based in Bengaluru.

If you have an English degree, you’ve probably come across [The Yellow Wallpaper](https://www.nlm.nih.gov/exhibition/theliteratureofprescription/exhibitionAssets/digitalDocs/The-Yellow-Wall-Paper.pdf) by Charlotte Perkins Stetson. Knowing that many of you may not, I’d encourage you to set aside half an hour for this quiet, unsettling story about the many ways love can be a form of control, and what happens when both society and the woman herself fail to understand where things have gone wrong.
After The God of Small Things, this is one of the most powerful reads that slips into my consciousness when I think about perspective and gender. When I realised that it’s drawn from the author’s own experiences, every word felt a little heavier. You never quite know where the line falls between fiction and reality.
The story follows a woman deemed “hysterical” who has been prescribed “rest”, which includes being forbidden from writing, painting, or socialising. And like it has been proven many times, rest and sleep aren’t always the best ways to solve problems that are rooted in your interactions with society. In my first year of undergraduate study at an all-girls college, I used to wonder why we talked so much about gender.
We clearly already knew about it, we were living it. That was before I understood that patriarchy causes harm across the board, to varying degrees.
Even now, every year I learn something new about how gender is experienced. Not only by people in general. But also by me.
It can be a sensitive subject, and you may feel there are more pressing things to discuss, or that it isn’t suited for a particular moment or audience. But there’s never a perfect time to talk about something difficult. We just have to do it.
This short story centres on what we now call postpartum depression, written nearly a decade before the term was even formalised—and published in 1891, so the em-dashes are very much the author’s own. Definitely not AI-generated.
I’ll stop here before I start sounding preachy. Go read it. It’s 10 pages long. Nearly every other line makes you feel something.
