Good Morning Ishan,
For Ranjan, coming home and doing chores after his nine-to-five is part of the deal.
He arrives home in Noida by 6.30 pm, makes coffee, takes a shower, and gets down to business. He straps a phone to his forehead and starts recording.
He moves differently once recording begins—slower, more deliberate, careful not to let his hands stray outside the camera frame. That’s because documenting the endless rhythm of a 20-something’s everyday life—washing and folding laundry, cooking, doing dishes—is gig work for Ranjan.
He earns Rs 250–300 per task. Most days, he manages three, earning up to Rs 1,000 on a good evening.
The recording must satisfy a strict set of instructions, however, or he doesn’t get paid.
Ranjan is what’s known as a physical AI trainer, a freelancer hired by data-collection firms such as Micro1, Egodata, Humyn Labs, Neocambrian, XP Robotics, Human Archive, and Cynlr to help train robots in human behaviour.
This new class of gig workers came to wider public attention in India after Pronto, the Bengaluru-based home services startup, courted controversy in May by allegedly sending camera-equipped workers into customers’ homes to collect footage for physical AI training, prompting competitors Urban Company and Snabbit to parrot reassurances.
But Pronto didn’t create the market. It merely exposed one that already existed. And that was growing at a fair clip.
