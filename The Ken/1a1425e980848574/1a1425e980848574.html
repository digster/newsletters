“If you had to pick, out of all these patterns and themes that we spoke about, which one are you the most sure about for the next season?”

That’s just one of several difficult questions Snigdha posed to me in the latest episode of Two by Two, which is also, incidentally, our season finale. You probably know Snigdha from Daybreak, The Ken’s daily news podcast. And in her trademark journalistic persona, she went back through both seasons of the show, pulled out the themes it keeps returning to, and put me (gulp!) in the guest’s chair.

It’s a bit strange to look back at the past to see what the future holds, but this is exactly what Snigdha forced me to do. I’m glad we did it, because I was able to see patterns I couldn’t spot in isolation—like how duopolies across quick-commerce, ride-sharing, and food delivery will face pressures and continue to innovate to hold out. Or how legacy companies like HUL and Bajaj Finance tell us about our understanding of assets, and how that’ll play out. And of course, about what exactly the new urban Indian is, and how they will continue to shape culture, cities, and much more.

How duopolies break

Praveen Gopal Krishnan

Both apps look exactly the same, both services look exactly the same, and the convenience fee seems to rise at the same time. So it feels like innovation kind of goes away because, at the end of the day, when it’s just 2 of you, you’re fighting between 55% market share versus 45%. I mean, who’s really going to push yourself beyond a certain point?

So the only reason, typically in the past, duopolies break is that they require a technological change, right? A new technological wave comes in and this thing— but in this case it doesn’t, right? And I feel like there’s something to say about why duopolies are now really being challenged, and it’s not for technological reasons. It is for business model reasons, which I find really interesting. […] I’ve realised that, do you know, zero commission is just a wedge. Just because you have zero commission doesn’t mean your success is guaranteed, number one. And number two: it only wins you the first fight. It doesn’t win you subsequent fights.

Another counterexample is Meesho. Meesho started with zero commission, but it didn’t win just because of zero commission. We’ve done an episode on this, where it won on something called Valmo, a logistics network it built. It won on data science, where it used its recommendation algorithm to suggest things and create a feed. It won on other things.

Like, imagine you’re part of a duopoly, and you’re like, you know what, I think the best way— and in fact, I had a guest on that, Jignan Shukla, who’s the director at Bernstein Research, and he basically characterised it as it’s like amputating to live, right? It’s ready to cut off its arm to say we will win the long game.

What going public does to a company

Praveen Gopal Krishnan

The analogy that I always give about Zepto is that it’s a little bit like the Joker in Gotham City. It’s a little unpredictable; you cannot really tell. It kind of bends the rules. Sometimes it’ll be like, “Oh, we will do this, a Rs 99 convenience store,” or “We’ll remove it,” or “We’ll start doing meat,” or “We’ll take on Licious.” And it seems very arbitrary. Some people say there is a grand plan, but it still looks arbitrary and chaotic, as they say. But effective, right?

For a long time, both Zomato and Swiggy kept saying this: boss, all of this is fine; you are a private company. In fact, they said this on their earnings calls whenever they were asked about Zepto, and Zepto kept touting these numbers. Oh, this is our market share. Yeah. And both companies kept saying, “Look, do not look at and measure a private company and what it says with the disclosures of a public company.” Correct. Implicitly saying, “Wait for these guys to go public.” […] And they decided that we will go public. And I was like, “Okay, this is so fascinating. I want to see how this happens. And they started going public, and everything was leading up to it, and literally just at the finish line, they stopped. They absolutely couldn’t. And they got blocked—not by VCs, but by mutual funds, which are a completely different player.

But I feel like what ends up happening is that when you go public, there is some pressure on you as a company to go to the other extreme. Right? So if you’re Ather and you say we are premium, they’re like, okay, you’re premium. But then you have to tell that story: we are going to go mass. It’s not about, oh, we are going to be premium; we’re going to add on some things. I don’t think investors understand that. Similarly, Nykaa goes and says, “Oh, we are mass, like, okay, but we’re going to be premium.” Oh, okay. So I feel like any story you tell that is the exact opposite, an aspirational story, is something investors tend to go for.

I feel like the story of making money from mass is so seductive and appealing that investors may be willing to understand it, fund it, and have patience with it. The story that I’m going to make money from premium and double down on it is also so seductive that people will back you. But if you tell a story, “I’m going to make money from the middle,” maybe you will, but I don’t think investors, private or public, can look at it and say, “Oh, this is such a great story.” I think I’d like to back this. I don’t think that resonance exists.

Spotting the real asset

Praveen Gopal Krishnan

I think the fundamental question with legacy companies is: what is your asset? Right? And I think their understanding of what an asset is changes. If they understand it and grasp it well enough, they succeed. But if they don’t, they kind of like persist with it. […] You may think, oh, my house is an asset. And my car is an asset. But your car is like— it’s very nice, it’s a wonderful car, it’s a wonderful house, and maybe it means something to you. But over time you sort of— because you’re kind of attached to it, you don’t see that it’s not really much of an asset. People don’t see it as much of an asset. It’s really not giving you the returns it should. But on the other hand, there is this thing lying in the garage that you thought was just, you know, a sewing machine. Then, after some time, you suddenly realise, wait a minute, that sewing machine I have […] is worth more than my car now. I think that is the fundamental thing with legacy companies. Can you spot that? Can you not?

Because those ads and those products are in our heads. It’s there in our heads, in the heads of you, me, our parents, our relatives, everybody around us. It feels like that is our asset, that is why we are great, and we just have to keep farming that asset. Well, not anymore. Okay, I don’t think people care about brands as much as they used to. […] It’s not cool anymore to drive the stuff that your parents drive. It’s not cool anymore to use the perfumes and the deodorants that your parents use. It’s not cool anymore to use the same products- soap, shampoo, and everything else- that your parents use. So that’s one reason something you thought was an asset is no longer an asset. And that is part of, at least what I argue in that episode, part of the reason why HUL is past its peak.

It is about the fact that, in their databases, in their company, they have recordings of all these calls over the years. Now think about it. Now you are in an era where voice AI suddenly comes. Voice AI is essentially saying, “Hey, we can call people to sell loans, collect loans, or do whatever else financial services do.” Bajaj Finance is now looking at its data and saying, “This is the biggest asset we’ve ever had.” Nobody has anything close to this, and only we have it. And we understand these workflows; we understand this so well.

When it became evident that open-source models were starting to compete with Anthropic and OpenAI, especially after the Fable ban, you start to realise people will want somebody to do some kind of orchestration. You can imagine these big insurance companies saying, look, we are going to de-risk ourselves. We’re not going to get tied to the hip of Anthropic or OpenAI. So you come, you set up the system in such a way that it’s protected and we don’t lose some access— what stuff we send to frontier models, what stuff we send to open source models, what stuff we send to, like, you know, closed models that is within our own premises. Yes. And I feel like Indian IT is tailor-made to solve problems like that. So sometimes I start to think, maybe it’s not so bad.

Who holds the money now?

Praveen Gopal Krishnan

So Rs 31,000 crores a month is just coming into the market from ordinary people. Who do nothing. Like, it’s just— it just comes like clockwork. Right? It just comes boom, boom, boom. Thirty-one thousand goes pumping into the market, right? It’s like a heartbeat every day.

So now, SIPs send money. Because they send money, they basically go into mutual funds. Now mutual funds have become the quote-unquote kingmakers. They’ve gotten much more aggressive in, like, earnings calls with companies. And you’re right: they are the main reason Zepto didn’t go public, because now the power has shifted from VCs to ordinary people. In a sense, you could say it’s funded by ordinary people, but the power still rests with big mutual funds. This shift has never happened before.

VCs used to be all-powerful; you’d go to them and have to get money. People used to have stories about how, as founders, they had to beg and grovel to get money from VCs. […] In fact, Indian VCs are trying to put money into international AI companies in the Valley. You’re an Indian VC; why are you sitting in San Francisco? But some of these very strange things have started happening. VCs are under a lot of pressure, and that pressure is eroding them. Public markets and money managers are becoming more powerful.

VCs are the most boring people in the world, okay, generally. They’re not just boring; they’re just so annoying. They just talk so much, and it’s just like— and most of it is all predictable, and it’s nothing interesting. In what they have to say. So I really don’t find them very interesting. But for some reason, they have filled that space on Twitter and LinkedIn and are giving gyan for things that are very obvious. And just like, oh my God, things are going to be awesome, things are going to be great, things are going to be awesome.

Regulators who want to play

Praveen Gopal Krishnan

Regulators have always been around, right? I feel like, in general, regulators are getting more involved in the business model of things, shall we say, more directly.

It’s like when you go to a when you go to a wedding, right? And someone plays the music, and it’s time to dance, right? Nobody’s going to go on the dance floor first, right? So one person has to go on the dance floor first, right? And Beam was the one who went on the dance floor first, right? And started dancing, and everybody was like, oh, okay, I can do better than this, better than this, I can dance here too. Now imagine if everybody else comes and starts dancing, and there’s a dance going on, the dance party going on, everything is fine. And this person who started the dance was probably the bride or something; must have left, right? Because I don’t know, dancing. Now suddenly at 3 in the morning, this person comes running back in. And it’s just dancing; you’ll be wondering why you are back here. But no, the NPCI is like, “We want to send a person in”.

But the thing that I was always puzzled by was: what was really broken here? Like, what is the problem here? I mean, you could say that perhaps, you know, these apps were essentially like, oh, we have been basically doing this for such a long time; we need to get some money out of it. Fine, fine, fine, I get it, all that. But I don’t know, it just seemed like even the subsidy was— they said, oh, it was a subsidy that was given by the government. But how much was that subsidy? It was nothing. It wasn’t a big amount. So it seems very strange that you had to change something that, in my view, did seem to be broken fundamentally. Who was really complaining here?

India’s California users

Praveen Gopal Krishnan

I feel like it’s getting weirder, but that’s me. Maybe I’m getting older, but I feel like it’s getting weirder. Maybe it was always weird in some ways, but it’s getting weirder. And I was always very interested in, without much judgment— okay, maybe little judgment— why is it getting weirder? […] desire for what I call— I called it ambient belonging, which is that I want to be around people, but I don’t want to be with people. […] Now, if you look at companies, companies are essentially looking at this and saying, okay, we want to do something, we want to grab the attention of people, right? But what do we do? Oh, we can’t; we can’t put up a billboard; we can’t do this. You know what, let’s do something. We’ll do a peptide party; we’ll do a hospital rave; we’ll do it. People will hate us, but that’s the whole point. It’s okay. Combined with a set of young people who are also experimental to the point where, yeah, we’ll do anything, it’s cool.

People think the market is so big, so huge. It’s not. And the whole thing that I wrote back then was that the entire market was run and created that runs the Indian consumer tech economy is run by this really, really small set of people, which is, I think, the size of Switzerland or London or Finland or something, one of the countries, okay, which is like just 10 million people. […] this set of people has such a disproportionate effect on everything. They have a disproportionate effect on companies; they have a disproportionate effect on AI and startups; they have a disproportionate effect on culture; they have a disproportionate effect in the way we see things and in the future, and the tactics and the business models of companies like even Swiggy and Zomato and Duopolis and zero commission. All of these things, I feel like this is the— if I really had to pick one thing, it is India’s California users,

I can’t think of a single company, maybe Meesho to some extent, that is a little immune from it. But the rest of them, they’re all a part of it. That’s the— India’s California users continue to exert power, influence, and effect on everything we do. 

If you look at the entire journey of Two By Two, almost every theme we pick is a function of India’s California users and their disproportionate power.
