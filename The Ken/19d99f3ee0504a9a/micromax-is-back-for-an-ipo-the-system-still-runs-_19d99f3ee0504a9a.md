Fri, 17 Apr 26
Multiple stories, multiple perspectives, one theme worth your time—every week.
Good Morning Ishan,
Bhagwati Products is not a well-known company. It used to be the assembly arm of Micromax Informatics, a once-famous smartphone brand. Now it’s reportedly planning [an IPO](https://www.moneycontrol.com/news/business/ipo/micromax-arm-and-vivo-oppo-smartphone-maker-bhagwati-products-kicks-off-ipo-targets-over-2bn-valuation-13889983.html) at a $2 billion valuation.
Not long ago, a couple of numbers stood out. The company said its revenue would jump from around Rs 650 crore in FY24 to Rs 15,000 crore by FY26. While the figure isn’t out yet, that would be a 20–25X jump in a smartphone market that hasn’t moved much. Normally, when a company’s growth graph looks like that, it has discovered something new. New tech, or some new source of demand.
Bhagwati didn’t discover something so much as [connect to something](https://the-ken.com/story/micromax-co-founder-rahul-sharmas-comeback-is-made-in-india-and-powered-by-china/?utm_source=email&utm_medium=newsletter&utm_campaign=collection), as The Ken reported a few months ago.
In 2024, Shanghai-based Huaqin bought a 49% stake. Huaqin is one of the largest original design manufacturers behind many of the world’s phones. After that, orders from Oppo and Vivo followed. Bhagwati’s output rose to about 8% of India’s smartphone manufacturing. It crossed 2 million devices. It hired 10,000 people. In effect, it was attached to an existing system.
There was another small data point this week. Apple’s vendors in India [exported](https://economictimes.indiatimes.com/industry/cons-products/electronics/indias-apple-component-exports-to-china-surge-to-record-2-5-billion-under-ecms-scheme/articleshow/130292464.cms?from=mdr) $2.5 billion worth of components, not to the US or Europe, but back to China.
From a distance, this looks like a success. India now exports over $17 billion worth of smartphones. The PLI scheme, worth roughly Rs 2 lakh crore, has done what it was meant to do, which is to bring assembly into the country quickly.
Zoom in, and it’s less triumphant. Domestic value addition is still less than a fifth. China sits closer to 40%. Indian manufacturers spend under 1% of revenue on R&D; companies like Huaqin spend ~5%. It’s a structural issue, still.
So the story is slightly different from how it is often told. India is making more phones, exporting more phones, and employing more people to do it. But the design, the key components, the coordination, and all the parts that matter most, are still elsewhere.
In other words, the supply chain hasn’t really left China; only stretched. India has gained factories, some components, and a lot of jobs. Not quite the expected decoupling.
The easiest place to see this is in the companies that are supposed to represent India’s manufacturing moment.
Take Dixon Technologies. Over five years, its revenue has grown nearly fivefold to about Rs 18,000 crore, helped along by the PLI scheme and by assembling for companies like Xiaomi and Google. The market now values it so generously that Foxconn looks almost restrained by comparison.
And yet, for all that scale, the economics remain surprisingly thin. Profit margins hover around 2%. A meaningful chunk of profits comes from government incentives. More tellingly, even at this scale, Dixon still depends on imported components, external designs, and global clients.
India may now assemble a significant share of the world’s smartphones, but much of the value still sits outside the factory.
A chunk of the contract manufacturer’s revenue comes from making smartphones, but its exports are scanty. If government incentives were to dry up, it doesn’t leave much for Dixon to stay afloat
Why investors give the US$3.8 billion-worth producer of phones and TVs the kind of premium global peers can only dream of
Which is why even the next step up the ladder is familiar.
Indkal, for instance, has figured out how to build an electronics business without owning factories at all. It licenses brands like Acer, designs products, and outsources manufacturing to the likes of Dixon. In two years, its revenue jumped from Rs 60 crore to over Rs 260 crore, with plans to keep expanding.
But look at what actually changed. Indkal swapped capex for contracts. It still leans on global brands for credibility, on contract manufacturers for execution, and on a supply chain that runs well beyond India.
If anything, this is where things seem to be heading. The system becomes spliced up. One company designs, another assembles, a third owns the brand, and each of them captures a slice. Almost no one controls the whole.
Six-year-old Indkal Technologies scaled without factories, piggybacking on a couple of global giants. Now, it has to show that the low-capex, high-licensing model can thrive in India’s high-octane electronics market
That modularity becomes harder to ignore the moment you move away from finished products and into inputs.
Consider pharmaceuticals, where India is often described as the “pharmacy of the world”. Even here, the dependence runs deep. China still accounts for roughly 20% of global API production and supplies to over 180 countries. When Chinese manufacturers cut API prices by 40–50% earlier this year, they undercut Indian producers to the point where some were selling below cost.
This is control over the terms of competition.
China is targeting the exact 41 molecules that India’s PLI scheme wants to make the country self-reliant in. Indian pharma knows better than to abandon local API makers, but deep challenges remain
The same dynamic plays out in newer industries. Electric vehicles, for instance. At one level, they are about batteries and software and clean-energy narratives. At another level, they run on things like rare-earth magnets. Which are uncool, hard to substitute, and, most importantly, almost entirely controlled by China. Over 90% of those magnets come from there. When export restrictions tightened, Indian automakers–who could’ve looked at clever redesigns—instead, cut output, pushed back launches, and scrambled for alternate supply.
Unless China clears shipments soon, Indian EV production could stall by mid-July
There was at least one attempt to go around this. A startup built a magnet-free motor. It worked, in the sense that it functioned. But it was harder to integrate and was ultimately ignored by large automakers. Mainly because it was trying to replace something the rest of the system was already organised around.
India’s EV players have been talking about decoupling from the Chinese supply chain ever since Beijing restricted rare-earth exports. They aren’t adopting homemade alternatives, though
Which is the broader point.
Moving assembly is relatively easy. Easy to subsidise and easy to scale. Moving the layers underneath—the materials, the IP, the supplier networks—that’s a different project entirely. And so far, that project hasn’t really started.
And when those layers don’t move, companies find ways to work around them rather than replace them. Sometimes that means routing supply chains through intermediaries. Sometimes it means building factories elsewhere. Indian manufacturers are increasingly doing both.
Take Jebel Ali Free Zone in Dubai. It has become a staging ground for Indian firms looking to plug into global trade with fewer frictions. More than 2,300 Indian companies now operate there, drawn by easier capital and fewer geopolitical complications. Globalisation with a few detours, if you will.
As trade between India and the UAE crosses $100 billion, the Gulf country could well overtake China to become India’s second-largest trade partner
Seen this way, China has already moved up the stack into design and components and now extends outward, letting others take on the parts it no longer needs to own directly. India is becoming important to that system. It adds scale, labour, and increasingly some capability; but not control.
Bhagwati’s IPO will be read as proof that the strategy is working, and in a narrow sense, it may. But India isn’t replacing China in global supply chains so much as being plugged into them.
