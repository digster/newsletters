In Indore, India’s cleanest city, Everenviro’s plant converts 500 tonnes of the city’s organic waste into 17 tonnes of compressed biogas (CBG) every day.
The City Gas Distribution Authority buys this and uses it to fuel the city’s 400-bus fleet and blend it into pipelines for domestic use. As Asia’s largest municipal solid waste (MSW)- to-biomethane plant, it serves as a model for urban biogas plants across the country.
Its success, however, has been difficult for other municipal solid waste (MSW)-to-CBG plants to replicate, despite government policies to support private investment.
On 1 October 2026, the Ministry of Petroleum and Natural Gas launched the Galvanising Organic Bio-Agro Resources Dhan (Gobardhan) scheme with an outlay of Rs 23,731 crore for FY 2026–35 to scale India’s biogas capacity tenfold by targeting the sector’s economic problems that its 2018 scheme called the Sustainable Alternative Towards Affordable Transportation (Satat) missed—like offering capital assistance and guaranteed buyers of CBG at a fixed procurement rate.
Satat failed to meet its goals. It had big dreams of reaching 5,000 commercial-scale CBG plants with a total production capacity of around 40,000 tonnes a day by 2023–24. But as of February 2026, there were only [134](https://x.com/HardeepSPuri/status/2023376739188298021?s=20) functional plants.
While most of these plants run on agricultural waste such as crop residue or press mud from sugar mills, animal waste, or napier grass, municipal solid waste offers a highly underutilised feedstock for urban India.
