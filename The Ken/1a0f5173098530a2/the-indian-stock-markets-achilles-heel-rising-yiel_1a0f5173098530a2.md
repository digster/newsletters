For an investor who put money in the Nifty 50 five years ago, she is now earning 6.6 per cent a year, including dividends. In July 2020, just after the Covid crash, that figure was 6.7 per cent.
Clearly, September 2026 has been one of the worst months for Indian markets in recent years. It’s the [worst](https://www.business-standard.com/amp/markets/news/nifty-50-logs-worst-september-performance-in-8-years-as-40-stocks-slide-126093000095_1.html) September since 2018. The NSE benchmark has fallen seven weeks in a row, and red candles have plagued the Indian retail investor. It closed at 22,620, roughly 14% below its 52-week high of 26,373 on 5 January, down about 5.67% over the month.
In lockstep, India’s 10-year bond yield has spiked to 7.15%, up from 6.6% at the start of the year.
Bonds are loans issued by a government or a company. You lend money to the government at a fixed interest rate, and the yield is the return you earn at the price the bond trades at.
The inverse relationship between bond prices and yields is one reason the NSE benchmark is suffering. Expensive crude oil imports, inflation, and a falling [rupee](https://economictimes.indiatimes.com/markets/stocks/news/foreign-outflows-from-indian-stocks-hit-6-month-high-in-september-on-higher-oil-yields/articleshow/134580784.cms?from=mdr), aside. All valid reasons that are also making foreign investors pull out.
