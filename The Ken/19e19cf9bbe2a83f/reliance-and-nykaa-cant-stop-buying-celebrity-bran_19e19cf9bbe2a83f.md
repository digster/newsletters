Trade Tricks
Tue, 12 May 26
A weekly newsletter about the biggest changes in commerce—focusing on shifts that matter to you.
Good Morning Ishan,
“We’re on edit 43 now,” an art director working on a Kay Beauty campaign told me recently. The team was sending the edits directly to Katrina Kaif over Whatsapp. At some point, exhausted, they accidentally resent an older version.
Kaif caught it instantly.
“This isn’t the 43rd edit,” she replied. “You’ve sent me the 13th one again.”
Kaif has built a reputation for being unusually hands-on, fronting everything from product swatches to marketing reels herself. But she isn’t the first celebrity founder to claim deep involvement.
Back in 2017, Anushka Sharma launched Nush claiming she had personally shaped the collection. But after plagiarism allegations surfaced, her business partner later [clarified](https://www.ndtv.com/lifestyle/anushka-sharma-s-nush-plagiarised-and-she-didn-t-design-apparently-1761480) that she was never involved in the design process. The brand never really recovered.
That’s old news. But ten days ago, there was a development in the celebrity-backed space that wasn’t out of the ordinary but has become a pattern: an acquisition.
Reliance Retail bought Anomaly, the haircare label founded by Priyanka Chopra Jonas. The deal included ownership of trademarks and digital assets, with plans to scale the brand through Reliance’s omnichannel ecosystem and Tira.
The recent acquisition joins a growing portfolio of celebrity-led labels Reliance has assembled over the years.
In 2023, it bought a 51% stake in Alia Bhatt’s Ed-a-Mamma at a reported valuation of Rs 300–350 crore. Before that came a 40% stake in Manish Malhotra’s MM Styles in 2021, a majority stake in Ritu Kumar’s label the same year, and earlier acquisitions like Satya Paul in 2018.
Reliance Retail isn’t alone. A few weeks ago, my colleague Aakriti [wrote](https://the-ken.com/tradetricks/what-does-nykaa-see-in-deepika-padukones-withering-beauty-brand/) about Nykaa’s talks to acquire 82°E, or Deepika Padukone’s “withering” skincare label, as she put it.
Nykaa already has skin in the game through Kay Beauty, a joint venture where ownership is split between Nykaa (50.99%), Katrina Kaif (41.5%), and Matrix, Kaif’s talent management agency (7.5%).
Reliance Retail and Nykaa did not respond to queries sent by The Ken.
Others are following a similar playbook. Aditya Birla Fashion and Retail owns the Virat Kohli-backed Wrogn, while Myntra operates Hrithik Roshan’s HRX.
These are large retailers. They have an established name in the markets. What do they see in buying celebrity brands which continue to lose money?
In beauty and personal care, the market has become intensely crowded, with legacy FMCG giants, digital-first disruptors, and influencer-led labels all trying to woo the same consumer, making differentiation harder and customer acquisition brutally expensive.
Celebrity brands offer a structural advantage: consumers already know the face, trust the person, and therefore discover the product faster with far lower marketing friction. In an overcrowded market, familiarity itself becomes distribution.
That is precisely why platforms like Reliance Retail and Nykaa want ownership, not just shelf space. By controlling the brand instead of merely selling it, they gain higher margins, pricing power, customer data, and if the product works: repeat purchase loops.
It is the same logic that pushed Amazon, Sephora, and Ulta globally toward private labels and exclusive partnerships.
Celebrity beauty has only become bigger in the West. Rhode, founded by Hailey Bieber, was scooped up by E.l.f. Beauty in a deal valued at up to $1 billion.
Hollywood has a slew of these: Rihanna’s Fenty Beauty, Selena Gomez’s Rare Beauty, Kylie Jenner’s Kylie Cosmetics, Hailey Bieber’s Rhode, Victoria Beckham’s Victoria Beckham Beauty, and Gwyneth Paltrow’s Goop, to name just a few.
My colleague Snigdha even [spoke about this](https://the-ken.com/podcasts/daybreak/rihanna-said-shed-never-be-a-sellout-then-reliance-bought-sephora/) global-local crossover and how it made Rihanna’s Ambani appearance feel almost inevitable after Reliance’s acquisition of Sephora India.
All these brands cracked something Indian celebrity labels are still figuring out: narrative.
Being a Black woman, Rihanna built Fenty Beauty around inclusivity and expansive shade ranges, while Selena Gomez, who has spoken openly about struggling with lupus, an autoimmune disability, positioned Rare Beauty around mental health, accessibility and community.
Hailey Bieber’s Rhode, meanwhile, [leaned](https://coulture.org/fashion-and-beauty-glossier-branding/#:~:text=This%20branding%20shares%20similarities%20with,communication%20and%20social%20media%20virality) heavily into the “clean girl” aesthetic: dewy skin, neutral packaging and a “your skin but better” philosophy borrowed from the Glossier playbook, piggybacking almost entirely on social media virality and consumer-led marketing.
Kay Beauty may be one of the few Indian brands beginning to figure that out.
“It’s positioned for the modern Indian woman,” said an industry executive who works with Nykaa and Katrina Kaif.
“The products sit in that mid-range sweet spot, borrowing some stylistic cues and packaging language from aspirational global brands like Rare Beauty and Charlotte Tilbury—premium enough to feel aspirational, but still within reach for a splurge. And Kaif, along with her husband, Vicky Kaushal, understands social media narratives extremely well,” she added.
Something others have completely missed or perhaps forgotten.
“There’s often limited long-term involvement from celebrities in actually building or marketing the brand consistently. But consumers won’t keep coming back just because they like a celebrity, the product eventually has to stand on its own,” he added.
If we go back in time to 2007, there was Salman Khan’s Being Human which was India’s first celebrity-led retail brand. It played a big role in cleaning his image (cue: Mumbai hit-and-run case) but after that we haven’t seen any that was able to match it’s scale. Afterall it even got other Bollywood actresses to model for it’s early collections.
That pattern has largely persisted. Sharma’s Nush struggled after plagiarism allegations soon after launch. Her husband, Virat Kohli’s Wrogn saw FY25 losses widen 32% year-on-year to Rs 75 crore, while Padukone’s 82°E reported a 30% decline in FY25 revenue to Rs 14.7 crore, with losses at Rs 12.26 crore.
Even Reliance-backed labels continue to post mixed numbers. Alia Bhatt’s Ed-a-Mamma reported FY24 losses of Rs 19 crore, while Ritu Kumar’s label saw FY25 revenue fall 5% which widened losses to Rs 49 crore. MM Styles, backed by Manish Malhotra reported profit of Rs 23.7 crore, down from Rs 34.3 crore a year earlier.
Kaif’s involvement is probably why Kay Beauty’s numbers look different than other celebrity-backed brands. The joint venture crossed Rs 133 crore in FY25 revenue and touched nearly Rs 500 crore in gross merchandise value (GMV) or the total value of products sold before discounts, returns, and other adjustments.
Reliance Retail posted FY26 net profit was just shy of Rs 14,000 crore, up 11.7% year-on-year, while Nykaa reported FY25 profit of Rs 72 crore on Rs 7,950 crore revenue, with FY26 numbers still awaited.
Despite the hype, India still hasn’t produced a true breakout celebrity-led consumer brand on the scale of Fenty Beauty or Rhode.
For all the celebrity sheen, many of these brands remain tiny relative to the balance sheets of the companies acquiring them.
Kay Beauty’s reported GMV is only about 6.3% of Nykaa’s FY25 revenue, while 82°E’s FY25 revenue is just 0.18% of Nykaa’s. Even Anomaly’s Rs 500 crore annual revenue run rate is only about 3.6% of Reliance Retail’s FY26 net profit, back-of-the-envelope calculation shows.
But if even one eventually cracks product, narrative and retention the way Rihanna or Hailey Bieber did, the payoff could be disproportionately large. Until then, the question remains: are these acquisitions strategic bets or simply expensive brand-adjacency plays?
