Good Morning Dear Reader,
Since May, the National e-Governance Division (NeGD), the IT ministry’s arm that runs Digilocker, has been keeping score. It tracks which ministries, states, and union territories have set up Data Governance Committees. The spreadsheet’s cells have finally started turning green.
October was a particularly productive month. The ministries of culture, MSME (micro, small, and medium enterprises), and even IT itself set up their committees in quick succession.
“About 38% of central ministries and departments, and 40% of states and union territories (UT) so far have got such committees,” said a person familiar with the tracker, who declined to be named.
The committees are part of a framework of the Committee of Secretaries (CoS), a group of senior bureaucrats tasked with resolving differences between ministries. Each committee will decide how its ministry classifies data, what it publishes, and who gets access to it.
This may sound like bureaucratic housekeeping. But government data has become a fairly busy business. Every time someone in India opens a bank account, takes out a loan, or buys a SIM card, a company may check their details against government records. Protean eGov Technologies, which runs a PAN-verification service under an arrangement with the Income Tax Department, processed 3.2 billion PAN checks in 2024–25 alone.
Much of that traffic moves through side doors. One-off deals between a department and its vendors, each with its own terms and its own record of who took what. In September, the Internet Freedom Foundation reported that a Telegram service was selling vehicle owners’ names, addresses, and phone numbers from Vahan, the government’s vehicle database, for as little as Rs 5 a record. There was no hack. A company with legitimate access had allegedly let someone else in.
The stakes are about to get higher. On 16 October, the IT ministry hired Corover, a GenAI company, to build agents that will fetch government records on citizens’ behalf.
