Good Morning Ishan,
Rent in Bengaluru has stopped tracking salaries, sense, or shame.
“I wanted to move from a 3BHK in BTM Layout, where I was paying Rs 16,000 a month, to a 2BHK in Koramangala. I was looking for a house with basic amenities for Rs 50,000 a month,” said Ekata Singh, a 30-year-old startup founder. “But brokers unanimously cited a budget of at least Rs 80,000.”
She is not an outlier. In The Ken’s survey of hundreds of Bengaluru tenants and landlords, nearly two-thirds of tenants, most of them strongly, want a hard limit on how much rent can rise each year. Tellingly, so does a meaningful share of landlords. Bengaluru wouldn’t be alone in doing something about it, either.
On 4 August, real-estate consultancy Anarock confirmed what tenants feel in their bank balances: Bengaluru and Hyderabad saw the sharpest rental yield growth of any major Indian city between 2019 and Q2 2026, and at 4.6%, Bengaluru’s yield (annual rent as a share of property value) remains the highest in the country. That’s stacked on top of home prices nearly doubling in six years.
The stakes go well past any one tenant’s budget. The Bengaluru Metropolitan Region (BMR), the engine room of India’s IT and startup economy, is a $149-billion economy. It accounts for more than 43% of Karnataka’s GDP and is home to 3.8 million jobs.
And Bengaluru wouldn’t be making a radical leap by capping rents. In fact, twenty-three of the OECD’s 38 member countries regulate it in some form.
New York has just approved a freeze on a million rent-stabilised apartments. Berlin runs a calibrated cap; Scotland is designing one pegged to inflation. Even Paris offers a more interesting clue: a recent study found that rent controls can bring rents down without the supply collapse critics usually warn about.
The nuance is in which version Bengaluru chooses.
