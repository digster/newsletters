An abridged, narrative version of the latest episode of Two by Two, The Ken’s premium weekly business podcast.
Good Morning Ishan,
Today’s episode is about UPI, but before I go there, I want to share a strange behaviour that someone shared with me. And yes, it involves UPI and payments:
“I maintain a separate bank account for Google Pay so that my salary account, which generally has a larger sum of money, is not exposed on UPI (with all the scams going on). So I send 15,000–20,000 at a time, but that gets over soon, in which case I have to open netbanking and do a transfer. Sometimes the money gets over without me knowing, and I don’t even have money to pay the vegetable vendor.”
That’s from Respondent #045, who’s a 32-year-old from Mumbai.
I got that from a reader as part of The Great Rewiring—a project that I’m currently running at The Ken. Essentially, The Great Rewiring is what happens when agents start becoming common in the Indian consumer’s life. And then, every business in India built on owning your attention, your habit, or your indecision will discover that an agent has none of the three. This means that power will shift towards new kinds of companies and drain from the old, leading to the biggest reshuffle of Indian business in a generation.
So to understand that better, I asked readers and subscribers of The Ken to tell me all the strange and unusual and quintessentially Indian tasks they put off, do mindlessly, or get frustrated with; plus all the weird workarounds they employ to address them (the most popular one right now is the single-user Whatsapp group).
And starting from next week, we’ll throw those problems to thousands of students all over India, and challenge them to build solutions on top of it using AI agents. Who knows, you may even get to test what they’ve built for you.
Hundreds of subscribers have already taken the fun survey. In case you haven’t yet, you can do it now—it’ll take you just five minutes, and you’ll see all the fun stuff that others have said.
You can tell us your annoyances and workarounds here: [the-ken.com/the-great-rewiring/](http://the-ken.com/the-great-rewiring/)
I got the idea for this episode after the Lok Sabha passed a bill last week to amend the Payment and Settlement Systems Act. This sounds momentous, but the import of it is quite boring—all it does is authorise the government to permit banks and other service providers to introduce charges on payments made through UPI.
There’s no word yet about what kind of charges will be levied, and how it’ll be rolled out, but the one thing everyone seems to agree on is that consumers won’t be the ones paying—at least not directly.
So who will? And who gains?
That’s really what I wanted to understand.
The answer to this question is not that straightforward. On one hand, there’s the old world, when UPI was free—where the rules were established, the players knew what they were supposed to do, how they made money, who they paid, and what they were supposed to be doing. This is the world we’re all familiar with, including several companies worth billions of dollars.
It wasn’t perfect, but everyone knew their place and their role.
And then, last week, well, we entered a new reality.
Let me stress once again that nobody knows what exactly is going to happen. But we can guess.
So I decided to bring in two smart people who know more about UPI than I do—people who’ve spent most of their careers trying to figure out this complicated net of incentives and products. And together, we spent 75 minutes trying to work out the maths of how things may pan out.
Bhavik Kaul, ex-CPO, Supermoney by Flipkart, India’s 5th-largest UPI app, and earlier at Payu Finance, running Lazypay and Paysense; and Pranav Gundlapalle, director & senior research analyst, Bernstein Research, join me in this episode of Two of Two.
And here are some of the most interesting themes we explore together.
The strangest thing I learnt from the discussion was how UPI actually helped banks instead of hurting them. Early in the conversation, Pranav set the context of how unusual this was, because usually banks are the ones who oppose any kind of payment system changes.
In his words, “It was a bold experiment from the government. You’ve seen this play out in many countries where such an experiment failed because the banks simply said, hey, we lose our debit card fees, and therefore we’ll never do this.”
So why is India different?
Well, because of ATMs.
India did approximately 10 billion ATM transactions in 2019. Today that number is close to 5 billion. Each ATM transaction costs the bank roughly Rs 20. Halving that alone saves about Rs 10,000 crore a year. Despite the fact that the population and account count both grew across the same period, India’s ATM network has been flat for the last eight years. Also, the debit card revenue that UPI cannibalised was relatively small, simply because card acceptance infrastructure was weak and debit had barely scaled.
Essentially, the timing couldn’t have been better.
But Bhavik has a different point of view. He argued that, sure, banks may have saved money on ATM transactions, but they invested a significant amount in the underlying infrastructure to run UPI, and that wasn’t trivial. He said, “The existing infra, the core banking systems at the banks, were never actually designed to bear such a heavy load. That is the one cost element that nobody had thought about, and which is also the reason why UPI, for a very long time, had seen really bad performance. Every third transaction used to go into a progress state.”
Until recently, banks never had an incentive to upgrade, but now they do.
All because UPI is no longer free.
One of the arguments that’s often made is that the government gives a subsidy of roughly Rs 2,000 crore every year to keep UPI free. Some players have argued that this is a fraction of the actual cost of running UPI (the numbers range from Rs 10,000–20,000 crore)
Pranav, though, argues that this is not really a subsidy—in fact, one could argue that it’s a rebate on a much larger saving from lower cost of printing currency. In his words, “Rs 2,000 crore is a subsidy given, but I’ve already captured Rs 7,000 crore of cost savings from lower currency printing costs. And on top of that, I also get better tax compliance, because it becomes more transparent. So tax efficiency goes up. It’s a great win for the government.”
This was the heart of the discussion and where we spent most of the time arguing.
We worked out that out of Rs 100 of UPI value, nearly Rs 70 was P2P, i.e., money sent from one person to another, and Rs 30 was money sent to merchants (P2M). However, by count, it’s the reverse—roughly 70% of transactions are P2M. Thus, of the Rs 30 P2M, generously Rs 15 would qualify for the Merchant Discount Rate (MDR), assuming these are going to be transactions above Rs 2,000.
This may not sound like a lot, but it introduces a significant amount of value in the system.
Bhavik argued that this was a net-negative for apps and detrimental to everyone except the incumbents: “The biggest cost difference that comes into play is the fact that apps have to acquire, retain, and engage the customers. For a two-paise cost, the apps are spending almost 13–15 paise. Acquisition out of that is about two paise—but just retention, the rewards, the re-engagement, that Rs 2 that you get on a Rs 500 spend—that is where this business is so heavily skewed to the incumbents.”
Pranav has a different point of view. He believes that it was well-established that the apps were never going to monetise payments, but that just gave them the ability to monetise other things.
“When they came in, they knew that this was a zero-margin business. The payments itself was the hook to get consumers on board. And then the platforms tried selling them a lot of things—mutual funds and credit and whatnot. On the consumer side, they’ve had limited success in cross-selling. On the merchant side, they’ve had a much better range of outcomes.”
This is what the episode is about. We go from stakeholder to stakeholder, starting with banks, payment apps, consumers, governments, and others and evaluate their comparative positions.
Who is in a better position as a result of this new legislation? By how much? And what happens when this plays out?
Unsurprisingly, when the calculations are done, we have a clear picture of the winners and losers.
