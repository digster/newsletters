The Collection
Fri, 24 Apr 26
Multiple stories, multiple perspectives, one theme worth your time—every week.
Good Morning Ishan,
Infosys reported its Q4 numbers yesterday, and at first glance, they look steady. Revenue grew about 13% year-on-year, and profit jumped roughly 20%. The markets weren’t convinced. The stock had already slipped ahead of the results, and is today hovering near its 52-week low.
All of that is the headline version of it. The more useful version is everything around it.
The quarter itself was expected to be soft, and it was. Sequential revenue dipped, and there were fewer billing days. Besides, growth for the year is still modest, margins are holding but not expanding, and the outlook remains cautious, with FY27 estimates in the 2–5% range.
Essentially, the odd combination of strong numbers + no momentum is visible in how the business is evolving. Demand hasn’t disappeared; deal pipelines remain healthy, especially in financial services and energy. Yet spending is more selective. Discretionary work is slower.
More of what’s coming through is tied to efficiency, cost optimisation, and increasingly, AI. Which is exactly where Infosys is leaning in.
Earlier this week, it tied up with OpenAI, a few weeks after doing the same with Anthropic, plugging tools like Codex into its Topaz platform. The pitch is the familiar IT-services playbook. Enterprises want AI; Infosys will help them deploy it. What’s more interesting is the other direction.
OpenAI needs Infosys, too. It needs someone to get its models into large organisations, places full of legacy systems and approvals. Infosys knows how to do that. It has the relationships, the access, and, most importantly, the patience.
So, this is an arrangement where Infosys is selling AI to clients, while also becoming part of how AI companies sell themselves.
That tension shows up in small ways in the results too. Headcount has dipped to about 328,000 from over 337,000 last quarter. Utilisation is steady, attrition is down, and free cash flow remains healthy. All signs of a system that is becoming more efficient. Also signs of one that doesn’t need to grow the way it used to.
Which works, up to a point. Because Infosys is helping clients use AI to need fewer people, while being a business that grew by supplying them. And that is the thread running through everything here.
The results, the OpenAI deal, even the leadership question. Salil Parekh, whose term is nearing another decision point, hasn’t misstepped (yet). But the next version of Infosys may not look like the one he’s been running.
Infosys’ old model was simple enough. It hired a lot of freshers, trained them, pushed work downwards, and saw growth for decades.
But, as we reported earlier, onboarding cycles first stretched and then tightened over the last couple of years. Freshers from earlier hiring batches were delayed, then pushed through faster pipelines. The time it takes for a new hire to become billable has dropped meaningfully across the industry. Expectations, meanwhile, have moved in the opposite direction. Entry-level engineers are now expected to perform at a level closer to that of someone with a couple of years of experience.
More skills, same pay, and barely any job guarantee—the IT services giant is raising the bar for freshers
At the same time, firms like Infosys, along with TCS and Cognizant, are pushing AI deeper into their workflows, often faster than the technology itself can comfortably handle. Copilot, internal tools, whatever the latest thing is, everyone has access.
Developers, however, told us about what might be called a verification tax. AI-generated code has to be checked and rechecked, and sometimes rewritten entirely. It behaves, as one engineer put it, like an intern who is very confident and occasionally very wrong. The workflow changes accordingly. What used to be a write-review cycle becomes generate-validate-rewrite-review.
And yet, adoption continues to be pushed from the top.
Enterprise-wide contracts and vendor bundling are hardwiring AI coding tools into India’s largest tech firms—whether engineers want them or not
This tension shows up most clearly in how IT services are being sold.
Earlier, it was all about promising cheaper services. Everyone understood the consolidated contracts and the 15–20% out of IT budgets. Now, cost savings are assumed, sometimes up to 30–40%. Clients expect these companies to be using AI already. So the new word is “transformation”.
But that’s a harder sell. It means smaller teams and more specialised work. In many cases, clients themselves are still figuring out what they want. Everyone is talking about GenAI. Not everyone is actually using it in production.
Even sales has changed. Tools now analyse prospective clients, generate pitch strategies, and personalise outreach in minutes. What used to take hours of preparation can now be done in a fraction of the time. But then, everyone has access to the same shortcuts.
So a 24-person startup says psychology can fix it.
The self-described “buyer-first” intelligence company wants to help sellers crack the buyer code, and with McKinsey and Amazon Web Services already signed up, IT services is Humantic AI’s next big target
While most of the industry is treading cautiously as the ChatGPT era enters its second year, one sector—human capital management—is eagerly embracing it
Then there’s the buyback. A few months ago, Infosys announced another one of Rs 18,000 crore. Its fourth in a few years. The official story is of excess cash, disciplined capital allocation, and “confidence” in the business. The other version is that it is not entirely clear where that money should go.
If AI is really the next big thing, one would expect bigger bets. Acquisitions, platforms, something that looks like a clear push. Instead, most of the AI effort still looks like layering. Partnerships, training, and adding tools on top of existing services. Infosys is talking about AI more than ever (nearly a third of its recent content and positioning is tied to it), but hiring patterns and capability building don’t quite match the urgency of that messaging.
A massive buyback usually signals confidence. But a deeper look reveals a company struggling to balance shareholder returns with the urgent need to invest in its AI future
It’s not just hiring or tools. In places like Kochi, you can see the shift physically.
At Infopark, Infosys and Wipro run the same old model of large campuses, thousands of engineers, work broken down and pushed through delivery pyramids. Migrations, testing, maintenance… scale, as always, is still the point.
Walk 10 minutes, and IBM looks different. Smaller teams with more senior engineers and work closer to AI systems and enterprise decision-making. Fewer people are needed to produce more output.
There are already two ways of organising in the same industry. For a long time, cities like Kochi existed for the first model. That’s still there. What’s new is the second one showing up right next to it.
IBM’s focus on AI-related products shows that tier-2 cities can go from low-cost delivery hubs to global software frontiers
There’s another change that’s harder to pin down. People inside companies like Infosys don’t seem to approach work the same way. You can hear it in small things. There is less urgency around routine work and fewer people volunteering for things outside their role. Tasks move but not with the same energy.
Some of this has the obvious reasons of slower growth and fewer raises. Some of it is something else. If the old system promised a fairly predictable path, the new one is less clear. That tends to change how people—“ambitionless”—behave.
And no, it’s not a Gen Z thing
IBM went through something similar decades ago. Built for one kind of world, adjusting to another as the economics changed underneath it. Then, it was forced to become something else. Infosys isn’t there, but it’s not entirely somewhere else either.
Indian IT giants are returning billions to shareholders while AI automates their business model. This part of the story sounds familiar
Infosys is, in many ways, doing things right. It is winning deals, partnering with AI companies, and presenting itself as the sensible bridge between new tech and the old systems.
What makes this interesting is that the same set of actions also describes a company in transition. It is helping clients use AI to simplify their operations, even as it works through what that simplification does to a business built on managing that very complexity.
It just seems to be optimising for a world that is gradually asking less of what made it work in the first place.
