If you are an IT-services company in India, there are many ways to make your stock go up. One less obvious way, as of yesterday, is for the US government to cut you off from something you are known for.

On Thursday, it suspended TCS, Infosys, Wipro, HCL, Cognizant, and Capgemini, along with Microsoft and Adobe, from the process that starts a green-card application for their foreign employees. 

On Friday, Nifty IT rose more than 1,000 points at one stage. TCS was up about 5%, with Wipro and HCLTech not far behind. Sure, investors cheered TCS’ growth, which is fair, since its annualised AI revenue crossed $3 billion.

But a rally like this needs another explanation.

Part of it is in our Intermission episode on TCS, published a week ago. Engineers in India work while the US sleeps, but a small team from here goes to sit at the client’s office. That small team needs visas. The episode discussed how new H-1B surcharges are pushing up hiring costs, and how global capability centres (GCCs), the offshore offices multinationals run for themselves, hired nearly 2X as many people as IT services in FY26. 

So, in my head, this suspension is like a restraining order against a person who has already moved cities. 

The National Foundation for American Policy counted 4,644 new H-1B approvals for Amazon in FY25 and 4,573 for the top seven India-based IT companies combined, 70% lower than in 2015. TCS fell from 1,452 to 846 in a year, HCL America from 1,248 to 379, and Wipro isn’t in the top 25. Amazon, for some reason, is not on the suspension list. On the new curb, TCS said its certification filings were in the single digits for two years, and it will hire 15,000 more people in the US; Infosys filed 781 in 2023 and none since. I couldn’t find counts for the rest.

The suspension is also the second time this year that the US has reached into Indian IT’s business. In July we asked whether India is the subject or the object of its AI story, after TCS became Anthropic’s partner for enterprise AI in India and a US export order cut off foreign access to Anthropic’s newest models.

A few weeks earlier we had argued that Indian IT’s best shot was to be the non-aligned layer, a neutral place where multi-vendor AI gets integrated and governed. Neutrality is harder to sell when the tools can be switched off from abroad. The same goes for the people who build with them, although most of those people still work for somebody else.

Who? Look at what the American Vice President attacked. (The certification programme is the step where the employer tells the labour department it looked for an American to do the job and couldn’t find one.) Vance said no company had abused the system more than Microsoft, which laid off 6,000 Americans in 2025 and filed 3,682 PERM applications, nearly a thousand for the same positions the laid-off workers held. Microsoft says about 80% of its H-1B filings were extensions or changes of status for existing employees, which answers a question about H-1Bs and not obviously the one about the certification programme. 

Either way, the crackdown targeted Indian IT, but its star exhibit is from Redmond. Labour Secretary Keith Sonderling’s own tally is that the named companies have collected over 230,000 H-1B approvals and 100,000 labour certifications since 2009. That is 17 years!!

Cognizant has the best story on the list, though. In January we asked why it was so keen to list in India at the worst possible time. 

Its idea was that a Nasdaq-listed company that sells like an Indian IT firm should be valued like one. Nine months later, the US government agreed and treated it like one, cutting it off after a fraud probe. (Cognizant’s new H-1B approvals fell from 2,873 to 743 in a year, while TCS added 4,258 people in a single quarter. So, few of them were using that door anyway.)

If the on-site team keeps shrinking, the work goes to a GCC, which doesn’t need a visa for its India team. When the $100,000 fee on fresh H-1B visas was rolled out last September, many GCC employees assumed their moment had arrived, and we found it hadn’t. Over 1,700 GCCs already existed, mostly in Bengaluru, and a former GCC employee told us, “The power will stay across the ocean, even now.” 

The services firms are restructuring from the top anyway. Mid last year, TCS announced it would cut about 12,000 jobs, about 2% of its workforce, mostly middle- and senior-level roles, and Praveen wrote that it would be seen as “the first stone that kicked off the avalanche”. 

We sketched the contest between the two models in a Two by Two on GCCs versus Indian IT companies in July 2025: roughly 1,800 GCCs, around 2 million people, and only about 35% of their parents’ tech talent in India, with a plausible path to 50 or 60%. 

Even banks, never engineers’ dream employers, are now serious competitors for that talent through their GCCs.

Microsoft and Adobe are on the suspended list as American employers, but both run big operations in India, and a freeze at home makes Bengaluru look better.

Not everyone gets off lightly. Immigration lawyers say workers nearing the six-year H-1B limit often lean on a pending labour certification to extend their stay, so for an engineer in year five, the freeze is a deadline with no mechanism. For the companies, analysts expect higher hiring and compliance costs in a sector already down 30–35% this year. Yet, Infosys’ US-listed shares dipped as much as 4% overnight, then closed 1.1% higher.

Washington keeps making the on-site option costlier, and Indian IT keeps needing it less.
