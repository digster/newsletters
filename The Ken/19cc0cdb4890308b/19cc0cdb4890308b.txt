Did Gen Z hand consumer brands a blueprint to beat the giants? For the first time, small brands are beating giants. Here’s how

“Gen Z certainly don’t search as much as scroll.”

When you first read that, it sounds like something a disappointed teacher would put in a report card or a parent forwarding a screen time article. But for a multi-billion-dollar consumer brand, that one sentence is either its biggest opportunity or the thing quietly ending it.

Because something strange is happening in consumer markets. Smaller brands are suddenly outperforming giants that had long dominated their categories for decades. 

Is Gen Z the reason? Or are we mistaking a deeper shift for a generational one?

This week on Two by Two, Rahel Philipose and Praveen Gopal Krishnan sat down with Adarsh Menon https://www.linkedin.com/in/adarsh-menon-8a35652/, partner at Fireside Ventures, and Ajai Thandi https://www.linkedin.com/in/ajai-thandi-b200a732/, co-founder of Sleepy Owl Coffee, to talk about something that sounds like a marketing trend but is actually a business strategy: Gen Z as the entry point of consumer brands.

Their silent strategy

The argument goes like this. In category after category, a new wave of startups is not trying to steal customers from the big guys head-on. They are going after Gen Z first, specifically because these legacy brands do not know how to talk to them. And once you have Gen Z, the rest of the market follows.

Look at what Snabbit is doing to Urban Company. Same thing with Zepto against Blinkit and Instamart. Same thing with Zudio versus Zara and H&M.

You hook them early, you build the habit, and then you can use them to crack the rest of the market wide open.

Adarsh Menon, Partner at Fireside Ventures

My bathroom shelf explains this better than any report

Dove, Clinic Plus, Sunsilk. Shampoo brands that have been bathroom staples in Indian households for decades. But they are just invisible to me now. Not because I have anything against them, but because I can get a conditioner for curly hair specifically, like from the brand Fix My Curls, or a shampoo without sulphates and parabens from a brand like Bare Anatomy, which I discovered on Instagram two weeks ago, made by people who actually look like they use it. The slow-motion hair flip ad with a Bollywood actress does not talk to me. It stopped talking to me a while ago, and I did not even notice until the newer options showed up.

Adarsh has a name for what happened to those legacy brands. He calls it losing the algorithm. “Distribution excellence has shifted from retail presence and availability to algorithm availability, to feed availability.” 

The brands that figured this out are the ones showing up in my scroll. 

But here is the part that PGK pushes back on. Is this actually about Gen Z, or, as always, is it the younger generation refusing to use the same brands as their parents? Ajai’s answer surprised me: “The difference between Gen Z and millennials is honestly they just dress differently. That is all.”

He is not wrong. But he is also not entirely right, and the hosts do not let him off easy.

Looking sideways

What actually changed is not the generation but the information. Ajai explains what went wrong with legacy brands better than anyone: “Go back to the founder who built and created the product. Was the ingredient first? Or was it ten years later when the corporate CEO said, ‘Let’s replace sugar with corn syrup?’” The product slowly changed, and nobody noticed for years because there were no alternatives. Now there are, and many.

I remember being 11 or 12 and buying Boost because MS Dhoni was in the advertisement. I genuinely believed that was a good reason. But I would not do that today because I have reviews, ingredient lists, and people who look like me telling me what actually works.

Adarsh calls this the sideward shift: “For Gen Z, a lot of role models are actually sideways as compared to upwards.” Which also explains why a celebrity face alone does not close the deal anymore. 

The bottom line PGK lands on after spending most of the episode as the sceptic in the room: “Is Gen Z the tip of the spear? Yes, it is. But you can even make Gen Z your shield if you want.”
