Thu, 30 Jul 26
A weekly newsletter about how finance is getting supercharged by tech in India, and how you can make money work for you.
Good Morning Ishan,
Zepto, Acko Insurance, Flipkart, Phonepe, Cars24, and more are lined up for IPOs in the upcoming months, and they have one thing in common. No, I am not talking about their startup backgrounds. It’s that all of them have been consistently loss-making for years.
Nearly 70% of startups getting listed on the main board between 2021 and 2025 had never turned a profit before going public. Profitability no longer seems to be the primary criterion it once was for companies looking to list.
But history, by and large, shows that not all loss-making companies that went into an IPO continued to show losses for those who bet on them.
Back in 2021, something shifted in Indian capital markets.
Market regulator Sebi [rolled out](https://www.thehindu.com/business/Industry/sebi-eases-norms-to-encourage-listing-of-start-ups/article34161319.ece) a more accommodating framework for new-age companies. Six startups rushed to list: Eternal (then Zomato), Paytm, Policybazaar, Nykaa, Cartrade, and Rategain. Four of them were loss-making.
The debate was quite [fierce](https://www.forbesindia.com/article/take-one-big-story-of-the-day/ipo-frenzy-lossmaking-indian-tech-startups-are-emulating-their-us-counterparts-but-thats-not-enough/69229/1). Should retail investors bear the brunt of venture capital firms’ inflated valuation models without profitability?
Five years later, we have our answer.
All six are now profitable. Eternal, Rategain, Nykaa (adjusted issue price based on bonus share issuance), and Cartrade have seen their stock prices surge 2–3X since listing. While it cannot be generalised for investment purposes, investors interested in startups have a slew of signals to consider beyond headline profit and loss, ranging from IPs, market potential to unit economics.
“Thirty years ago, when companies built factories, those costs were capitalised, and profitability looked far healthier,” Santosh N, managing partner of D&P Advisory. “Today’s intangible investments make balance sheets look weaker than the underlying economics.”
With the upcoming IPOs of Flipkart and Zepto—both of which reported losses of nearly Rs 6,000 crore in their latest financial years—the debate has resurfaced. Flipkart alone has reported more than Rs 60,000 crore in losses since its founding in 2007.
Zepto’s current [IPO filing](https://the-ken.com/story/zepto-wants-rs-8100-crore-and-investors-to-trust-the-numbers-it-wont-disclose/) reads like a paradox.
The pure-play quick-commerce challenger, not yet six years old, generated more than Rs 23,000 crore in revenue in FY26, almost matching competitor Swiggy’s topline.
Impressive on paper. But the filing also suggests the company remains firmly in its growth phase. Zepto’s draft red herring prospectus (DRHP) also acknowledges that the Rs 8,100 crore it plans to raise may not be sufficient and that it could require “alternative forms of funding” after the IPO.
And this uncertainty shows in the valuation debate as well. Zepto raised $450 million in October 2025 at a $7 billion valuation. Nine months later, it was reported to be [negotiating](https://www.financialexpress.com/market/ipo-news-zepto-halves-ipo-valuation-amid-investor-pushback-4303942/) with investors at around $3 billion, less than half of its previous valuation.
Domestic mutual funds pushed back hard, arguing that benchmarking against listed peers like Eternal and Swiggy was misplaced. Unlike its rivals, Zepto doesn’t have a food delivery business to cushion its quick-commerce bets.
Take Eternal for instance. It has Blinkit for quick commerce, Zomato for food delivery, and [District](https://the-ken.com/podcasts/daybreak/district-isnt-even-2-percent-of-eternals-business-but-its-enough-to-rattle-bookmyshow/?searchTerm=District%20isn%E2%80%99t%20even%202%25%20of%20Eternal%E2%80%99s%20business.%20But%20it%E2%80%99s%20enough%20to%20rattle%20Bookmyshow&utm_source=search&utm_medium=page) for “going out” tickets. The consolidated net profit for the parent company stood at Rs 92 crore in Q1 FY27, up nearly fourfold year-on-year. Its market capitalisation is nearly Rs 2.8 lakh crore, while the stock has comfortably outperformed the Nifty 50, which is down 8.2% this year.
But Zepto is not Eternal. It’s not Swiggy either. While [Swiggy](https://the-ken.com/story/is-swiggy-snatching-defeat-from-the-jaws-of-victory/) has not been doing well in comparison, its chase of contribution margin is an investor-friendly practice. The company has already shown positive numbers on two accounts and is now aiming to be so in quick commerce as well.
Zepto does not seem to have this leverage, at least for now, seeing how its quick food delivery and e-pharma are still in their early phases.
The market is signalling something clear: IP, market potential, diversification and, if not profitability, a path to profitability matter.
“Headline losses in new-age IPOs tell only half the story,” said Santosh. “Many of these companies are investing heavily in technology and customer acquisition, assets that accounting rules force them to expense, not capitalise.”
The critical question, he added, is whether these losses are funding a defensible, scalable business or simply burning cash.
The market is already reacting to that distinction.
Consider Ather Energy and Ola Electric. Both companies have been loss-making. Yet, one is beloved by investors, while the other is not. Ather Energy has consistently brought down its losses, halving them to about Rs 517 crore in FY26 from over Rs 1,060 crore in FY24. Ola Electric’s losses, meanwhile, widened from Rs 1,584 crore in FY24 to Rs 2,276 crore in FY25, before reducing to Rs 1,833 crore in FY26. And despite its negative numbers, Ather Energy managed to raise Rs 1,300 crore via the
Ather has just [raised](https://the-ken.com/long_and_short/what-ather-got-right-about-asking-investors-for-more-money/) Rs 1300 crore via the Qualified Institutional Placement route, where listed companies can issue new shares, but only to qualified institutional investors.
The Ken also analysed more than 30 startups that launched mainboard IPOs between 2021 and 2025. In 2025 alone, around 70% of the startups that went public were loss-making. But more than 70% of them have since turned profitable.
Within less than a year of their IPO filings, the situation has shifted significantly.
Just a few years ago, investors and founders would privately tell us about the great lengths they went to to delay certain payouts and purchases to make their IPO-bound companies “profitable” or “nearly profitable.” Soon after listing, many would slip back into losses.
Today, there seems to be greater financial discipline. At the same time, retail investors have become more careful in how they [evaluate](https://the-ken.com/story/the-indian-ipo-wave-throwing-the-rulebook-out-the-window/) startup IPOs.
His four-question framework is worth borrowing.
First, are the losses from a growth investment like deliberately buying market share or is something wrong structurally, like faulty unit economics that can’t scale? Contribution margin per order, per customer, per city, which is what’s left from an order after paying the direct costs of fulfilling it, tells you this faster than the P&L.
Second, how much of the IPO is an offer for sale (OFS)? If most of the money is going to selling shareholders rather than the business, the IPO is an exit event, rather than a capital raise. That said, several OFS-heavy IPOs by companies such as Indiamart and Happiest Minds have performed well despite such structures.
Third, what is the company’s cash runway? How many quarters can it operate before the company must return to raise more capital?
And fourth, what does the valuation imply? At the issue price, what revenue and margin must the company deliver in five years to justify the valuation? And are those assumptions reasonable or heroic?
Still, it would do well to invest with caution as the stock market shifts in how it treats startup IPOs. Five years ago, the debate was whether loss-making startups should be allowed to list at all. Today, the question seems to be around their intentions.
