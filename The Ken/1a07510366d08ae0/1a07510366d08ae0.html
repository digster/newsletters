Problems are equations. Equations are systems. The RHS is a dependent variable

Though I often joke about it with my colleagues, the truth is that writing First Principles each week forces me to “mine” my life for “content”.

It’s true. Writing to you—writing for you—each week is not something I take lightly. Like a writer or reporter who’s jotting down everything interesting they come across, because you never know when you might stumble upon the perfect opportunity to use it, I too have my FP radar on all the time.

About a month ago, it started beeping in the middle of a Friday coaching session with my colleagues. Somehow, we landed on the topic of learning to frame problems better before setting out to solve them.

“I have a hack. I think of them as literal equations to be solved,” I said. “And what do equations have?” I asked them.

Constants. Variables. Conditions. And of course, goals or results.

I did not want to stress the math metaphor too much because one of my recurring nightmares is somehow going back in time and landing in a “final exam”, except with the math-atrophied brain of my adult self, and not having a clue about the questions on the paper. A variant of this is waking up towards the end of a term to realise I’ve somehow missed all my classes and haven’t a clue what’s being taught.

Anyhoo, back to that Friday last month, I was thankful I could use the metaphor of the equation without having to solve any real ones.

“Can you solve an equation if I leave out some of the constants or variables from it?” I asked again.

Nope. Of course.

And that’s how it is with the thorniest and hairiest problems. We set out to achieve the result on the “RHS” of the equation without thinking too much about the
“LHS”.

Scratch that. Many times we don’t even define what an objective result or goal will be. What value will convince not just us, but all stakeholders (ugh, did I just say “stakeholders”?), that the problem has truly been “solved”.

Constants could be specific resources or assets that are available to us. Variables could be the things whose availability or levels are undecided and depend on us or others. Conditions are, well, constraints we have to operate within.

Instead, we dive into the RHS. We create prototypes, write proposals, and argue approaches. A special dose of anger and irritation is reserved for those who ask us, “But what are you trying to achieve?”

That should be obvious, d-uh! The person must be dumb. Or mean.

Those would include the late Charles Kettering and John Dewey too.

Kettering was the head of research at GM from 1920–1947, and Dewey was a philosopher and educational reformer. Kettering said, “A problem well stated is a problem half solved,” and Dewey, “A problem well-put is half-solved.” 

But in our workplaces, spending time on defining the problem is tricky. There is a bias for action. The clock is ticking. Who cares for restating the problem? 

Memories from school classrooms come to mind. The fellow student who asked a teacher to re-explain the problem because they couldn’t understand it, even as the “topper” had already jumped ahead to solving it.

Well, we’re the topper now. We’re already thinking of potential solutions, ha!

At the heart of these predictable errors is a fascinating mental “auto-complete” feature known as attribute substitution. When we are confronted with a highly complex or difficult question, our brains often bypass the heavy mental lifting by unconsciously swapping the hard question out for a related, highly accessible, and much easier question. Understanding this invisible swap is the key to unlocking how our intuition works, why we fall prey to stereotyping, and how our brains constantly strive to balance computational speed with real-world accuracy.

Attribute Substitution: How the Brain Swaps Hard Questions for Easy Ones, T. Franklin Murphy

Once we go down the path of solutions, we get invested in them. The first half-decent one that comes along looks good. We ask others for feedback on our solution, because we truly don’t know if it is the best one. 

Because we did not define our problem equation well enough. Sure, we got an RHS result, but is it the correct one? Someone else will have to tell us.

We shuffle through mostly similar variations of solutions, modifying one thing here, adding one thing there, so that a solution can be accepted. 

Given my love for all things “Systems”, here’s a hack: imagine that the RHS of your problem equation is not something that you control, but emerges from the interaction of the elements on the LHS. 

The equation is a system. 

“Winners and losers have the same goals,” said James Clear in Atomic Habits.

What differentiates the two are the systems they build or harness, which eventually deliver the goals. 

So, if the problem that you’re solving is an equation, and that equation is a system, where can you find most leverage?

This article by the late Donella Meadows, whose thinking and work are foundational to a lot of modern Systems Thinking, is a brilliant and often counterintuitive answer to that question.

Ultimately, if we had to reduce all of these arguments into a single line, it would be, “the RHS is a dependent variable.”

You don’t get to decide it. Instead, you construct the LHS in a way that forces the RHS to be closest to the solution you want.

Where photos tell many stories

“It is the population-dense societies versus the population-sparse communities.”

Hello, hello, this is Tanim for the books section. 

Consider the above quote in the context of language. There was no word for uranium in Khasi so the translators had to coin a new one—moranian —by combining existing words from the language to fit their context and convey a meaning they could understand. 

That line is from Em No Nahi, a posthumous photobook by Tarun Bhartiya. Though he did not live to see it published, his spirit lives on in all the photos and words he left behind.

Bhartiya is proof that people belong to people, and not places. Although he was always known as the dkhar, or the outsider, he managed to win many hearts, so much so that even the Khasi Students Union started calling him Bah (brother) Tarun. For him, identity and belonging were defined by the connections he had with people, not by where he was born or where his ancestors came from. 

The book, recently published by Yaarbal last December, pulls you in with its very first photographs. As I flipped through the pages, familiar with a geography similar to the area photographed, the experience was both nostalgic and melancholic. 

There is one image of a bus, slightly tilted like the Leaning Tower of Pisa, traversing an undeveloped area, that immediately made me think of the bumpy rides to my grandmother’s home.

Unlike that image, though, most of the photos resist a clear “subject”. People appear at the corners and edges, including the matriarch of Domiasiat, Kong Spillity Lyngdoh Langrin herself, who was the main character, so to speak. 

Accompanying the photographs is rich prose and poetry by both Bhartiya and his partner Angela Rangad. 

It’s a good time to pick this book up now against the backdrop of the 26 August resolution passed by the Meghalaya government to ban uranium mining. 

Rangad’s reaction to this was rather lukewarm. She said it was a distraction meant to obscure the ongoing exploitation caused by cement factories and coal-mining operations. 

The tug of war between population-dense and population-sparse is a never-ending loop for a developing country like India. There would be great benefit in understanding First Nations people, instead of trying to determine a single right way to live.

Long live women!

Hey there! This is Vishnu with the movie picks for the week.

A few days ago, a filmmaker friend of mine and I were having a conversation about movies with female leads and a socially relevant message. 

Of course, the starting point of this was Liar’s Dice, directed by Geetu Mohandas, whose Toxic is in theatres now.

A lot of movies came up, of which I’ve picked three for your consideration this week.
Hope you enjoy them as much as I did.

Aattam (Malayalam, 2023)

Translating to “Play”, the movie is a social drama that will remind you of 12 Angry Men—but this one will hit you harder. 

The writing is extremely good, and the acting supports the tense, raw nature of the story, especially the almost flawless performance by Zarin Shihab.

The accolades it received, including Best Film, Best Editing, and Best Screenplay at the 70th National Film Awards, are a testament to how well-made this movie is. 

Aattam is streaming on Prime Video.

Aruvi (Tamil, 2016)

 Unconventional, bold, funny, tragic—this movie is a rollercoaster of emotions, built on a strong backbone of messaging.

The dialogues are extremely well-written and land hard, thanks to the solid performance from Aditi Balan. There’s enough to think about, even after the credits roll.

Streaming on Prime Video.

Gargi (Tamil, 2022)

I remember watching this in an almost-empty theatre and being disappointed that more people weren’t watching it. 

Gargi is one of those movies that really hit me during and after watching it. Again, exceptionally written and staged—the climax is not for the faint of heart. You have been warned.

The best performance by Sai Pallavi, in my opinion, and Govind Vasantha’s violin is something you can’t miss.

Streaming on Sonyliv.
