Good Morning Ishan,
For years, Mangesh, a former engineer at Maharashtra’s renewable-energy agency, worked on a fairly modern Indian proposition: how to monetise drought.
Droughts plague the state’s farming belts in Vidarbha and Marathwada, where the ground is cracked more often than green. Farmers now lease their parched fields, sometimes sell them to power companies, where rows of solar panels stand as precisely as crop lines once did. “The same sun that punished our farms is now powering our industries,” he said.
No industry benefits more from this bargain than data centres.
The race to build them, thanks to a global AI boom, revolves around who can guarantee the servers uninterrupted, mostly clean power. And in October, the Maharashtra government played an ace. It named seven major companies in a government order, granting them a special framework to build their own captive renewable-energy systems for their upcoming data-centre projects. The message was clear: build here, go green, we’ll make it work.
Five months later, Maharashtra changed the rules.
Mumbai accounts for nearly 60% of India’s data-centre capacity, a market that has nearly tripled in five years to 1.5 GW in 2025. The seven chosen companies—Amazon, NTT, Ctrls, Equinix, Nxtra, Sify, and Princeton Digital Group—are among the largest operators in that market. Three of them have already built or contracted over 250 MW of captive solar in the state.
But the green energy framework those investments were based on no longer exists in its original form…
