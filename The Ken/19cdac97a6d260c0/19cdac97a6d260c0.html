Wegovy, Ozempic, Zepbound, Mounjaro. Some of these drugs are labelled for type 2 diabetes patients or people with cardiovascular risk. All of them are used by people who want to lose weight.

The first two drugs are made by Novo Nordisk, which basically created the prescription weight-management market. But Eli Lilly has been eating into its market share, and Indians will soon have affordable generics to choose from, given that Novo Nordisk’s patent for semaglutide, the active ingredient in Wegovy and Ozempic, will lapse in a little over a week.

Go up one level in the company structure. If Novo Nordisk can’t sell its drugs, then maybe its biggest shareholder can absorb part of the infrastructure that delivers medical care.

That’s precisely what’s going on: Novo Holdings acquired stakes in Manipal Hospitals in 2024 and Surya Hospitals in January this year. After all, “hospitals are sure-shot bets to make money in Indian healthcare,” as one analyst told The Ken.

Manipal is the second-largest hospital chain in India. Surya, although smaller, is a specialist network that serves women and children. Sudeshna makes sense of the moves by the Novo Group in today’s story. If you care about how you or a loved one may receive care the next time a hospital visit is warranted, then you shouldn’t miss it.

IIT-Bombay is set to make good money from Sedemac’s listing today. So will most of its new investors, who came in during the $100-million secondary round two years ago. But no money is flowing into the company; the Rs 1,087 crore IPO is entirely an offer for sale. That, in itself, is not unusual. What is, however, is the academic-founder’s math on why a company growing at more than 25% doesn’t need capital. With extreme customer concentration and a pricey IPO, the markets are watching closely.

Read this week’s Long and Short by Seema Singh to understand why this “global leader in the making” might justify that quote.

The incumbents face execution challenges not because they are PSU’s, but because of the way the defence procurement and order system works in India. Even if private players were to replace HAL, they would likely face the same structural constraints
One key issue arises when the designer of a product is different from the manufacturer. In such cases, execution challenges emerge that the manufacturer alone cannot easily resolve. The delays in the Tejas program, for instance, would likely have occurred even if a private company had been the manufacturer.
The second issue is scale. In the defence sector, the government must be proactive in placing orders at scale. Many critical components have long lead times often around three years(aeroengines). When orders are placed in small batches rather than in large volumes, the execution cyle gets longer, which is perceived as delay..
In fact, a private player replacing HAL would likely insist on contracts that guarantee large scale orders before committing resources. PSUs, on the other hand, often accept smaller orders because they cannot refuse them and cannot openly highlight the underlying causes of delays. In many cases, these delays arises from government ordering practices or from the lead customer not extending the same flexibility and concessions to domestic manufacturers that are regularly offered to foreign aerospace suppliers.
