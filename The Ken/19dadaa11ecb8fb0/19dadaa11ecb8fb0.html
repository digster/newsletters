With the IPL season in full swing, I often find myself logging into the Jio Hotstar mobile app to check match results. When my hometown team KKR plays, I pray they don’t lose yet another match this season (spoiler: they always did, until I started writing this piece). But before I can spot the day’s match banner on the homepage, two other things catch my eye—a distractingly large ChatGPT ad and a bright reddish-orange tab called “Tadka”. 

Tap on it, and you’ll see titles like Secret Crorepati Jamai and Section F Ka Only Boy. Overdramatic plots and dialogues narrating bite-sized tales of forbidden love, cheating spouses, heroes with magical powers, and more. Launched in April, Tadka is Reliance’s official entry into microdramas: short video series meant for vertical viewing on cellphones, with episodes typically lasting one to three minutes. 

In fact, microdramas have been all the buzz this past month. Amazon launched its free platform Fatafat on MX Player about 10 days before Tadka’s release. Meta India even held a summit on the topic in Bengaluru last month, unveiling its microdrama report with media consulting firm Ormax. Film studios like Yash Raj Films (YRF) and Red Chillies are also reportedly considering an entry into this space. And all of this sits within the existing ecosystem of platforms like Kuku TV and Zee’s Bullet. 

What was once a fast-growing niche is now becoming mainstream. 

And it’s not surprising. The Meta-Ormax report highlights that 65% of viewers discovered the format in the past year, spending an average of 30 minutes a day watching microdramas. 

Industry leaders, who initially saw this as lower-quality and cheap, are now seeing that this is exactly what is turning the tables.

Money’s worth

Once upon a time, OTTs were the main challengers in entertainment media, dethroning the traditional runthrough of theatrical films. But now, OTT has scaled and settled into a mature, slow-growth phase, where money mostly comes from ads.

Which is why OTT originals fell out of fashion after peaking in 2023—their high production costs could only be covered through higher subscription fees. “India is a price-sensitive market; we don’t like paying first to watch content,” said a producer who previously worked with Jio Studios and Warner Bros. 

Microdramas offer the perfect compromise: snacking content that keeps people hooked and scrolling. “They’re faster and cheaper to make, raking in watch-time and redirecting more eyeballs to ads,” the producer added. Amazon’s Fatafat, for example, requires no subscription and is entirely ad-supported. Even Tadka is bundled with Jio Hotstar’s entire content library instead of charging separate fees. 

Consider the cost. According to Kunj Sanghvi, a senior executive at Kuku FM, microdrama budgets hover around Rs 20–25 lakh. In contrast, typical film budgets never fall below Rs 8–10 crore.

Vertical shots focus on faces, not wide backgrounds, so we don’t need to build elaborate sets and hire extras.

Kunj Sanghvi

Merely suggesting a school setting with a few props in the background, for example, gets the point across. Lower logistical constraints mean a full-length microdrama (about 1–2 hours long) can be shot in just three to four days. Plus, the format is driven by cliffhangers and story hooks rather than expensive, star-driven casting. 

And with AI now in the mix, Sanghvi explained how it is helping level up their production and create content easily to keep feeding the algorithm. Also, a factor that ensures that production costs are slim. 

All these factors make microdramas a great funnel for larger OTT players, without the need for heavy investments. It’s less about the dramas themselves and more about introducing people to a larger content library to get them hooked.

The perfect funnel

Microdramas are heavily intertwined with social media, with 89% of all viewers discovering them through platforms like Instagram and Youtube, according to the Meta-Ormax report. That’s why Tiktok launched its own microdrama app, Pinedrama, rather than just acting as a shopfront.  

This also explains why microdramas make such a cost-effective funnel for OTTs. Instead of building entirely new promotional material, “we just push out a two-minute episode as an ad, and the cliffhanger makes people want to subscribe,” Sanghvi said.

This pipeline also allows microdrama creators to calibrate content for success more frequently and easily. Kuku TV tests thousands of potential viral ads and dramatic situations on low budgets to understand what audiences want. 

“Our content is tagged by narrative, character types, and a billion other things to track interactions,” Sanghvi noted. Where viewers stop and continue, what character dialogue triggers comments—they receive millions of monthly signals on what works best. This sort of agility isn’t possible for most OTT content. 

It’s not just a symbiotic relationship with social media, but also a competing one. Microdramas offer round-the-clock engagement. Normal OTT consumption peaks after 8 p.m., whereas microdrama viewership is more evenly distributed, according to Sanghvi. 

For the Hotstars of the world, microdramas provide engagement in pockets of time—think lunch breaks and office commutes—that would otherwise go to Instagram or Youtube. Plus, two-minute episodes allow users to stop and start easily without losing the narrative. It overlaps directly with the realm of reels and shorts.

Built for us

“Microdramas aren’t a foreign concept for Indians. Growing up, it was the creative ads we’d eagerly wait to watch in between three-hour-long films,” said Rahul Mittra, producer and actor. 

From Maggi to Centrefresh, Vodafone to Colgate, those ads were the blueprints for attention-grabbing, narratively interesting content within a 30-second span. So while India’s entry into microdramas has come later than the Chinese markets, it’s not something difficult for the masses to adapt to.

Plus, microdramas are practically new-age reincarnations of Indian soap operas. Except, they also allow for solo viewing, unlike a TV show or movie that’s typically watched on shared, larger screens. 

“Most Indians live in single-TV households, so where do you find privacy to consume your content?” Sanghvi asks. They naturally default to their phones. The Meta-Ormax report highlights that 90% of people watch microdramas alone, compared to 43% for long-form OTT content. It’s also consumed “ambiently”, i.e., while doing something else by 57% of people, making it perfect for homemakers.

The meteoric growth of this content-genre will likely permeate its way into bigger-budgets eventually. “Actors have an intense fear of missing out, and when they realise that a growing demographic of people are watching microdramas, they’ll want to diversify,” said Mittra. They can use microdramas to get new audiences hooked, hoping it exposes them to their larger movies and shows eventually. “At least that’s how microdrama guys will pitch it, I’m sure,” Mittra added. 

That said, how much microdramas will shake up OTT giants is questionable. “They will be a slice of the larger content pie, but not a revolutionary one, at least not yet,” said the former Jio Studios producer, adding that microdramas don’t add prestige, just engagement. But maybe that’s all they have to do to play their part perfectly. 

As for the larger ecosystem, while large OTT players entering microdrama increases competition, they also raise awareness. With Hotstar now in the mix, smaller players don’t have to fight to make people take microdramas seriously. It’s here for good.
