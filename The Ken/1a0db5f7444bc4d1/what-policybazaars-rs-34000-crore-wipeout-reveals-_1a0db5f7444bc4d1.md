The Collection
Sat, 26 Sep 26
Multiple stories, multiple perspectives, one theme worth your time—every week.
Good Morning Ishan,
Over the past couple of days, while going through the insurance regulator’s much-talked-about consultation paper, I kept going back to something I saw. Initially, I thought I had misread it, mainly because it seemed to invert something I thought I understood about markets.
Banks that sell only one life insurer’s products paid an average total distribution payout of 13% of new business premium. Banks that sell multiple insurers’ products, which you would assume is the more competitive, more customer-friendly arrangement, paid 33%, more than double.
When you think about it, though, the mechanism isn’t all that mysterious. With one insurer on the shelf, nobody has to bid against anyone. With several, insurers start competing for the bank’s shelf space, and that competition shows up as higher commissions, not lower premiums. So you, the customer, pay the same either way.
Which is essentially to say that more competition made the product more expensive to sell, and the regulator’s paper more or less says so.
That paper, “Recalibrating Economics of Insurance Distribution,” came out this week, and PB Fintech, Policybazaar’s parent, promptly fell as much as 36% on the day, its worst single day on record. Max Financial dropped close to 10%. HDFC Life and ICICI Prudential Life fell too. ICICI Lombard, a general insurer rather than a life one, understandably rose.
Insurance itself didn’t get riskier on a Wednesday. What changed is that everyone got a very specific look at how the product gets sold, and how much of every rupee handed over never reaches anyone’s actual cover.
Irdai’s paper is almost apologetic about how little this is a new diagnosis. Reform attempts in 2016, then again in 2023 and 2024, all went after commission-driven mis-selling. Each time, per the paper, commissions grew faster than premiums anyway, erasing whatever the previous round achieved, until the industry arrived at what Irdai now calls a “high cost and commission-led business”.
Between FY23 and FY25, life insurance commissions on new business grew 2.25X against premium growth of 1.28X. Motor insurance is the number that actually made me put the paper down for a second. Premiums on motor policies rose 34% over that period, while commissions on the exact same policies rose 259%, very nearly tripling the distributor’s cut on a product whose price to you didn’t move nearly that much.
The other finding that stopped me is about group health insurance, the bulk policies large employers buy for their staff. My instinct, and I would wager yours too, is that a company negotiating cover for a few thousand employees at once has real leverage, and should pay a lower embedded commission than someone walking in alone. Irdai found the opposite.
Group health throws up another strange number. The sector-wide average commission is 12%, against 17% for individual health. But the peak commission paid by an insurer to a distributor reaches 93%. Irdai calls this “particularly unusual and counter-intuitive”.
I’d guess the mechanism is that corporate buyers fight hard over the premium number and never think to ask what’s riding along inside it. Isn’t that an expensive way to be good at negotiating? A few insurtech companies have spent years trying to build something that routes around this structure.
So why not look at how differently that has actually gone?
We reported in December that Acko, which entered retail health insurance three years after Go Digit, had already overtaken it in premium income with just 15 health insurance agents across India. Its gross premium was more than 40% higher than Go Digit’s, India’s largest listed insurtech, which still relies heavily on a traditional agent network. Acko’s idea has been that better underwriting and app-first distribution beat paying an army of people to push policies door to door, and for this one slice of the market, the numbers back it up.
The digital-first insurer is three years late to the retail-health segment but commands 40% more premium income in this space than its listed rival
Not everyone selling the tech story actually built the tech, though.
We found, in a 2023 story on Insurancedekho, that despite raising money on a distinctly Policybazaar-shaped pitch, its real distribution ran through a tech-backed field-agent network responsible for the overwhelming majority of new business, structurally closer to state-run LIC, which still sources more than 95% of its own new premium through agents, than to any software marketplace.
A line in the story has stuck with me: Insurancedekho “hardly makes money by selling new policies, as almost the entire commission it gets from the insurer goes to agents bringing in new customers”.
Cardekho’s insurance arm wants to create its own identity, but it faces a twofold challenge—the offline market is getting crowded and insurers are working on reducing their reliance on online marketplaces
At least Insurancedekho found a working formula, even if it wasn’t the one it advertised. Phonepe couldn’t find one at all. We reported in March that the Walmart-backed fintech, sitting on 600 million users, had spent over Rs 1,000 crore building an insurance business since 2020, nearly Rs 700 crore of that on advertising alone. Five years in, it had sold about 15 million policies for a total premium of Rs 2,000 crore, but generated only Rs 150 crore in actual revenue, barely 4% of the company’s top line.
If Insurancedekho shows that the agent commission problem survives being rebranded as tech, Phonepe shows the harder version of the same lesson. That even a company with more captive users than most countries have citizens couldn’t get people to buy insurance just because it was three taps away.
The Walmart-owned fintech's failure to sell insurance to its UPI users means the payments company's desire for a new business with scale remains just that
And getting someone to buy the policy is only half the problem. We reported how life insurers stop at nothing to get customers to renew policies that may already be terrible deals. At LIC, surrenders and withdrawals made up nearly a third of its payouts, while nearly a quarter of policyholders dropped off after the first year.
Of the US$40 billion payouts LIC made last fiscal, surrenders and withdrawals made up a third; it would've been hit the worst had a key reform been pushed through
We covered the flip side of that same trade-off in 2024, through Turtlemint, which made the opposite bet, that a well-run agent network builds a kind of trust software can’t fake. Turtlemint’s own commission costs rose in lockstep with its revenue as it scaled, exactly the diseconomy Policybazaar’s software-first model was built to avoid. And PB Fintech’s quarterly profits around the same period jumped 160% year-on-year on 40% revenue growth, which, at the time, looked like proof the software model had won.
One’s people-powered model will be stacked up against another’s software-centric one that clocked a 160% growth in profits in the December quarter
Irdai’s new numbers make me want to revisit that comparison. If commission payouts rose sharply across nearly every channel, banks, brokers, and corporate agents, over those same two years, the question is how much of any distributor’s recent profit, software-driven or not, was riding the same industry-wide commission inflation.
We also reported in late 2024 that both Policybazaar and Insurancedekho were nervously watching Irdai’s own state-backed marketplace, Bima Sugam, as a threat to undercut the entire aggregator model from above.
Debasish Panda, head of India's insurance regulator, is bringing the fight to the marketplaces with an unexpected new rival
This week’s paper reads, to me, like the regulator assembling the case file for doing exactly that, and doing it with a level of detail that’s hard to argue with. A table shows one insurer paying its promoter bank an 8% payout, while a direct competitor pays its own promoter bank 42% for the same kind of relationship.
Besides, distribution isn’t the only place this industry leaks money. We reported last year that New India Assurance, the country’s largest general insurer and a state-designated “too big to fail” institution, has spent a decade paying out Rs 117 for every Rs 100 it collects in premium, a combined ratio that would make most actuaries wince, and has been kept afloat mainly by investment income rather than disciplined underwriting.
Distribution costs are climbing on top of insurers that, in some cases, were already losing money on the policy itself.
New India Assurance’s premiums are rising, but so are its problems. The math isn’t adding up
Three reform attempts have already tried to curb commission-led selling. The commissions kept finding ways to grow faster than the premiums beneath them. The new paper is different mainly because it doesn’t look at insurance from the insurer’s side.
The industry has traditionally believed that “insurance is sold”, Irdai says. It now wants insurance to be “also purchased”.
That’s what stood out to me this week. If something in your inbox made you put the paper down too, write to me at sumit@the-ken.com.
