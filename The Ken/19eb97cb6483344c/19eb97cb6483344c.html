Today’s episode is part history lesson, part economics lecture, and part provocative solutions.

Essentially, I decided to do today’s episode because somewhere along the line, I couldn’t stop thinking about the SIP-FII cycle. In my head, the chain of events looks something like this:

-Indians deploy a big chunk of their savings every month into the equity market (last I checked, this was close to Rs 31,000 crore a month. And this happens regardless of what happens with the market. Up, down, doesn’t matter—the money arrives. 

-At the same time, Foreign Institutional Investors (FIIs) are using this as an opportunity to reduce their holdings in India. Partly because of the macroeconomic conditions, and partly because India doesn’t really have much of an AI story or opportunity. 

-These funds are taken out in rupees (which eventually get converted to dollars), which further weakens the rupee and puts pressure on the forex.

-The central bank tries to hold the line through its reserves, but this is a losing battle. The rupee falls. 

-The Prime Minister makes an appeal to Indians to reduce foreign travel and stop buying gold.

-The Indian saver is affected. 

How do we break out of this loop? 

This is a difficult question to answer because there isn’t a simple, easy answer. Broadly, there are two strains of arguments—one which is (often) made by economists who seem to suggest that structural reforms and policy changes are the answer, much like what happened in 1991. Then there are market experts, who argue that the only way to break the loop is to actually break it, and let the chips fall where they may. As an example, Shankar Sharma, the investor, made a pretty bold statement a couple of days back: 

The equity market, he suggested, should be allowed to find its own floor. In Sharma’s view, a sharp correction is not something to fear. It is, in fact, the very trigger that brings foreign capital back in. “Let the market fall; FIIs walk in when there is a big market crash,” he said. 

On the question of how to manage FII behaviour structurally, Sharma pointed to taxation as the most effective lever. “FII taxation is the best mechanism to disincentivise the equity train,” he added.

So I decided to put the theory to the test, and to do it, I called two guests—an economist and a market investor—and asked them both to tell me how we’d break this loop. Anupam Manur, professor of economics at the Takshashila Institution, proposed some structural viewpoints. And then, there’s Deepak Shenoy, founder & CEO of Capitalmind, who argued that we must “free the rupee and let it go”. 

Here are some of the most provocative and interesting themes from the episode.

Is this a big crisis or a regular crisis?

The story that’s been running all year is an exodus. Record outflows, a market under siege, the foreigners walking out the door. Deepak and Anupam had differing viewpoints on whether the exit that we’re witnessing is significant or not, both from a quantity and from a composition perspective.

Deepak: To give you an example. They have sold maybe 3–4 lakh crore in the last 2 years, 1.5 years. This is the number that we should look at. They have sold this much, but they still own nearly 100 lakh crore of the Indian market—probably a little less than that, maybe 70 lakh crore. So most of their money is still here. So we are complaining about the wrong thing. 

Anupam: One is that the FIIs were largely into your large caps, IT services, etc. Whereas DIIs have slightly more bought into the mid-caps and small-caps. And the mid-caps are still kind of trading at quite high premiums. I think there’s the 28 or 29X forward earnings against 11–12% expected earnings growth. So there’s a mismatch. They’re overvalued. I don’t think anybody disagrees with the idea that generally, your mid- and small-caps in India are overvalued. So the absorption is not happening at the same level of securities. There’s a composition mismatch of sorts.

The lessons from 2005, when the rupee went up

Ask anyone what the rupee does and they’ll tell you it, well, falls—it’s the one fact about the currency everyone believes. It’s also incomplete. There was a brief window, not that long ago, when the same foreign capital we’re now mourning the loss of pushed the rupee in the opposite direction.

And that stretch coincided with the one year India ran a current account surplus, which tells you the climb wasn’t an accident. 

Essentially, the currency follows the flows, and the flows have gone both ways before.

Deepak: By 2006 or 2007, I think the rupee had gone to 50 rupees to a dollar. It had come down to 38. 38. That’s a 22–23% appreciation of the rupee in that sense. So you had seen that. Now, many people don’t remember. They’re like, the rupee always depreciates. No, it does appreciate. There have been moments when you have 20%.

SIPs are either the hero or the villain

The systematic investment plan has become a point of national pride — Rs 31,000 crore a month. If you’re bullish about India, the flattering reading is conviction. i.e., Indians have learned to stay invested. 

The less flattering one is that they have nowhere else to put the money.

This is what Anupam refers to as the saver’s famous price insensitivity. In India, capital controls mean it’s not easy to buy a US stock or a Korean one, so the SIP keeps buying Indian equities whether they’re cheap or dear. On the other hand, Deepak doesn’t dispute the inflows, but he argues that it’s happening because access has become frictionless, not because of a lack of options.

Anupam: The domestic retail investor is a lot more price-insensitive than the foreign investor. The foreign investor is comparing Indian securities with those of Korea and Taiwan and Vietnam and the rest of the world. They’re asking, “Where do I get the best returns?” So if they feel that India’s equities are overvalued, they jump ship and go somewhere else. Or if you don’t have anything exciting to offer, they will go to the place that does. Whereas the Indian investor is only comparing your FD and your stock markets.

Deepak: More importantly, does this mean that the SIPs are coming in, therefore they’re exiting? I think there’s a lot of causation-correlation confusion here. The increase in SIPs started in 2000, right? One of the reasons it happened was that India went digital. It was otherwise a pain to go to a brokerage to open something. Now all these new-age brokerages have come in, and just like in the US, when E-Trade magnificently changed things in the late ’90s, Robinhood later changed the game. India already had low-cost brokers. 

And Sebi changed the rules during Covid to say you can do all this stuff online, KYC, Aadhaar-based, etc. Which means anybody could get on and just click, click to open a mutual fund account or a brokerage account online. You saw more and more participation in Indian equities, and it’s continuing because it’s so easy now. Just set up the SIP and move on.

We also go into two reasons not to tax foreign investors. After all, India is the only country in the world that taxes foreign investors on where the gain was made rather than where they live. 

And both Anupam and Deepak wanted to abolish this, but for different reasons.

Finally, when I asked them for solutions, both Deepak and Anupam had their own provocative answers—the investor wanted to “free the rupee” and the economist wanted the state to control its expenditure and reduce extracting revenue from wherever it could. 

It’s a great, somewhat wonky episode. I enjoyed it though.
