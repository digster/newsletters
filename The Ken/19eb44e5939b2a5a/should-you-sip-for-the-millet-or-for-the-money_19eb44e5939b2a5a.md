A weekly newsletter about how finance is getting supercharged by tech in India, and how you can make money work for you.
Good Morning Ishan,
HDFC Mutual Fund, India’s third-largest fund house by assets managed, wants you, dear investor, to do your bit for nature and farmers—by investing in its mutual fund schemes.
Its half-page ad in the front page of a business daily on 5 June—World Environment Day—declared that “Your SIP today, can help revive tomorrow”, exhorting us to #SeedTheFuture.
For every SIP registered between 5 and 15 June 2026, 2000 square feet of barren land will be revived through millet farming, the ad added. A mighty claim.
Curious, I checked the fine print. Here are the terms and conditions:
So, an investor needs to start a new SIP of at least Rs 3,000 a month. This needs to be done digitally or online on or before 15 June. And the SIP should continue for at least 3 years. You can pick from among [54 schemes](https://files.hdfcfund.com/s3fs-public/2026-06/Leaflet%20-%20Seed%20The%20Future%20(Nurture%20Nature%202026)_1.pdf?_gl=1*jr32uy*_gcl_au*MTE0NDQ3MTQyOS4xNzgxMDc0NDY3)—mostly equity with a couple of gold and silver options. The fund house will then issue an e-certificate within one year from the SIP registration, subject to successful completion of the proposed activity and three SIP instalments getting successfully processed.
Many ifs and buts. Yet, a key detail was missing.
How much would HDFC Mutual Fund contribute to millet farming for each new SIP started by investors? I searched through the fund house’s press release and other documents around this latest campaign—Nurture Nature 6—but couldn’t find the number.
My call to customer care also resulted in a blank. A sales executive did call back, only to say that the contribution amount was not decided yet and that it would be finalised after 15 June.
HDFC Mutual Fund’s campaigns for Environment Day are not new.
For context, in its Nurture Nature 5 [campaign](https://www.hdfcfund.com/services/additional-info/hdfc-mutual-fund-nurture-nature-5) that it ran last year, it had said that for every new SIP registered digitally in select schemes, the schemes would contribute Rs 100 towards providing school bags made from recycled plastic waste to children in need.
In its FY26 annual report, the company claims that “with over 37K digital SIPs registered under the campaign, approximately Rs 40 lakh was committed towards school bags made from recycled plastic for children in underserved communities.” Around 8,000 schoolbags were distributed, the report added.
Good deed? Of course. But this is a corporate social responsibility (CSR) activity that the fund house could well have done without getting investors to put money in its schemes.
Now, assuming that this year too the fund house would contribute Rs 100 for each new SIP, would that really suffice? Guess it would take a lot more to revive 2,000 square feet of land through millet farming—from training farmers to helping them sell the crop, as this [video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=W85DvPbn79M) says the fund house will do.
Sure, in the scorching heat that much of India has been facing, millets can be farmer-friendly, requiring less water and being better for the soil than many other crops. But campaigns like these seem more of a tick-the-box exercise by money managers, tugging at the heartstrings of investors, than genuinely solving for a problem. The lack of basic detail, including how and how much, doesn’t help. PR, branding, marketing, and CSR are best kept separate from the bread-and-butter business of asset collection and management.
Businesses will do what they think is in their best commercial interests. But should investors bite?
No, I think.
Investors should decide on their investments based on parameters that really matter—goals, suitability, time horizon, return expectations, risk appetite, and track record of assets and schemes. Not on marketing spiels that tug at the heartstrings and urge you to loosen the purse strings. That will help fund houses, not investors. For instance, investing in an equity fund or a gold fund for emotional reasons may not be a good idea if what you really need is a debt fund.
How you make money and how you use it can, and ideally should, be independent.
You can do good yourself with the money that you make. You don’t need a fund manager for that.
Invest for the money, not for the millet.
