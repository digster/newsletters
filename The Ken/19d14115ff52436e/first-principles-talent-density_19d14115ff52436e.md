Early this week, I met with Ankit Nagori, the founder and CEO of Curefoods. We spoke for nearly two hours, recording episode 53 of the First Principles podcast. Uddantika, our producer, has some insights from the conversation later on in the newsletter.
Ankit is a sharp, measured, and extremely well-read founder. He started his career as a 25-year-old at Flipkart, after meeting its founders at the Delhi Book Fair in 2010 and cold emailing them. He then had what can only be called a “meteoric” rise at Flipkart, ending up as its chief business officer in just six years.
Ankit reminisced about his early days at Flipkart and the sheer “talent density” that existed.
“But for me to be running books, which was 70-80% of our revenue, and I was just 25–26, was like a dream. I can’t tell you what those years feel like. It was just a… breeze. It was the camaraderie that we had, the talent density that we had – Mekin, Amod, Sujeet, Binny, Sachin and of course Amitesh Jha. They are all running such large pieces now, and of course, they are all legendary leaders. I think to be able to assemble that team at one place during a short period was the reason why Flipkart got to the success that it got to. So for me, that was the biggest learning that you know, just how smart people can be.”
Naturally, I asked him if talent density was a stroke of luck and timing (“bottling lightning”) or if it could be engineered. I also asked Ankit what he looked for when trying to build talent density.
“Learnability and ability to do hard work. Right now, our revenue head for Krispy Kreme is, you know, my current chief of staff. His chief of staff’s work is 30% in a week, so he is always on a 70% project, and that right now is Krispy Kreme revenue. His name is Ninad. For me, the biggest metric would be that if I give him this project, in 30 days, is he the donut expert in India or not?”
….
“So I feel younger people have more ability to learn. They are more sponge-like, if I may. Second is hard work. I think that is physical limits of hard work, you know. Like, if you have to succeed, you have to improve your surface area for your luck. That happens only with hard work. There is no other way. See, everyone says luck, this that, but you got to be fully ready when luck is ready, and that happens when you just put in all those hours. There is no replacement for that. So today, why is Zomato so wildly successful? I’m telling you, those people are a bunch of super hard-working guys. Like it’s crazy the number of hours they can put in. And right from the start, Deepinder has set that culture and is very proud of the fact. Like it’s a very, very hard-working culture. And Flipkart was also very hard-working and had a very smart set of people. A very rare combination where crazy IQ people with that many hours.
….
So just to summarise in one line: someone who’s willing to learn and put in hours. And that person can be anyone, could be an intern or could be a senior.”

Hello, Tanim here. Let’s talk books.
Last Monday, the Sahitya Akademi awards were announced. One of India’s greatest literary honours, it is given across 24 Indian languages.
Do you read in other languages? Is there anything you would like to recommend that isn’t written in English? Or anything you are reading? You can share [here](https://theken.typeform.com/FPBooks?typeform-source=the-ken.com).
[Crimson Spring](https://www.alephbookcompany.com/book/crimson-spring-a-novel/) by Navtej Sarna won the award in English. Published three years ago by Aleph Book Company, it’s a narration of the Jallianwala Bagh massacre told through the eyes of nine characters, both Indian and British. (Side thought: how come so many book publishing headquarters are in Delhi?)
I haven’t read the book, but the idea of nine characters offering different perspectives caught my eye. The power of narration really shines through in books like these if done well. It forces you to shift perspectives and open your mind to different viewpoints within the boundaries of a single event.
The title and cover aren’t the kind that immediately catch your attention. It wants you to imagine a “bloody spring”, but choosing the “crimson” instead of “bloody” makes it sound poetic while still retaining the violent tone of the event. This is what I love about language. The way different words capture different essences.
In The Waste Land, T S Eliot calls April “the cruellest month”, just three years after the massacre mentioned above. Considering that the massacre happened in April, the coincidence feels eerie, though he was writing in a slightly different context. But it was the first poem that made me think of spring differently. Reading Keats and Wordsworth while growing up in Assam, where spring in April is quite pleasant and calm, I had never imagined the month as anything but pink or green. It’s also the month of the Assamese New Year, so the air is vibrant with everyone getting ready for Rongali Bihu. But now, April feels like all the colours, not like a rainbow but a messy painter’s palette.
