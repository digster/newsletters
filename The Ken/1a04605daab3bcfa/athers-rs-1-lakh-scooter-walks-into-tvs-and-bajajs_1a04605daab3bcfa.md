Two By Two
Fri, 28 Aug 26
An abridged, narrative version of the latest episode of Two by Two, The Ken’s premium weekly business podcast.
Good Morning Ishan,
In about 24 hours, at its annual Community Day in Bengaluru, Ather will unveil the product its next decade rides on.
If you go to the website, this is what it looks like today.
The Konarc is Ather’s next big jump.
In its short history, Ather has already executed two big hops. First, it successfully launched a high-performing electric two-wheeler in India, which became the darling of motor enthusiasts all over the country. Then, just a few years ago, it took a leap into a much more demanding consumer market and launched the Rizta, which sits squarely in the “family scooter” segment.
As of last month, the Rizta contributed to nearly 80% of its volume.
Essentially, Ather successfully expanded beyond its original enthusiast customer base and created a stronghold in the mass-market family scooter segment.
And well, the market rewarded it handsomely for this.
The rise of Ather is a story in itself, and one that we’ve discussed previously with Tarun Mehta, its co-founder and CEO, in a [previous](https://the-ken.com/podcasts/two-by-two/ather-energy-was-a-pioneer-can-it-also-be-a-leader/) episode of Two By Two. At the time, Ather was on the cusp of going public, and we had a conversation about how the company intended to keep innovating on its product leadership while also stepping up and getting on the front foot to improve its market leadership.
And well, the first step in that journey was done successfully.
Tomorrow, Ather takes the next one.
But this one is going to be much more difficult, more challenging, and by doing this, it walks directly into the path of India’s biggest incumbents—TVS and Bajaj—challenging them on its home turf, i.e. the mass-market scooter.
How will this unfold?
To discuss this, I brought on two of Two By Two’s returning guests.
Kunal Khattar, founder of Advantage, an early-seed fund that has spent a decade betting that India’s EV transition will be won by startups, and Narayan Sundararaman, independent strategy and brand advisor, most recently head of marketing at Bajaj Auto. Both of them are surprisingly optimistic about Ather’s chances—but with caveats.
Together, we discussed how Ather got here, how it found the resilience to stay where it is right now, and what lies ahead for India’s most promising EV company.
Specifically, our discussion was centred around five themes:
There’s an old jungle saying among Indian VCs, which Kunal brought up:
“We joke amongst ourselves that India is a country which suffers from first-mover disadvantage. You’ve always seen people who actually come number two or number three have the ability to benefit from what the original person has created.”
Ather created the two-wheeler electric vehicle category practically from scratch—it had little access to capital, infrastructure, or demand in its early days. But it made up for that through a relentless focus on execution, product, and passion. The comparison between Ather and Tesla is almost a clichè—both companies laddered down from the top-end to the bottom-end, but we figured out that this was borne out of necessity rather than strategy, since that was the only way they could be capital-efficient.
But then there was the product itself, and Narayan gave his perspective from Bajaj’s side, observing the consumers themselves:
“There was a fair amount of—I would use the term passion—that all the people who were choosing Ather had about the product. So it was a very strong product-led story. They built their own battery pack. The provenance of the founders was that they’re ex-engineers and they’re building from the ground up… And to their credit, I think the product delivered… Unlike, say, an Ola where the technology was borrowed.”
This slow and deliberate growth was in some ways a given, because Ather had to really earn the trust of its buyers. Narayan approached this from the consumer side, and illustrated that a two-wheeler purchase is a pretty big investment for several households, especially because it’s a tool for trade. In his words, “A new technology comes along the way, they’re not going to take to it unless a lot of hard questions are answered. And to their credit, I think Ather has shown that at least on the product front, the answers have come back pretty strong.”
Strong product. Check. Reliability. Check. Start with premium first. Check.
Over the past few years, Ather has been working on a new platform—the Konarc scooter, which will be unveiled tomorrow, is the first scooter built on that. This is the platform that enables Ather to go down-market, because it gives it the ability to launch a scooter in the Rs. 1 lakh price point, where a huge chunk of the market rests.
Narayan estimates that this new platform will dramatically raise the addressable market for Ather, “Their addressable market would have expanded from, say, 30–40% currently, and it would now be 60–70%”.
Kunal was much more bullish and argued that the Konarc was just the starting point for Ather, and that its structural advantages will really generate returns at a scale others cannot match.
“I’m sure, two to three years from now, Ather will be launching more products which could be priced below Konarc as well. Because once you’re able to crack those volumes, get economies of scale going, you’re able to build similar products at a lower price… Because they own the full stack, you’re optimising on every single line item.”
However, this is exactly where TVS and Bajaj score well. This is their home territory, and I didn’t see them giving this up without a fight. Most of their scooters are in this band, and even with the Konarc, Ather’s scooters still sit at a premium of 30–40% above ICE scooters.
Which brings us to the scooter market itself, and how Ather will attack TVS and Bajaj.
Narayan explained how the scooter market is distributed in India—scooters are a regional product. As a share of total two-wheeler sales, scooters are highest in the south, the west, and the metros, and relatively sparse in the north, where the market is motorcycles. The explanations for this are both social and cultural.
For Ather, this is where things start to look a bit shaky.
Strikingly, Ather’s current market position is heavily concentrated in the south, and it’s looking to expand to Gujarat, Maharashtra, and Chhattisgarh, which are the next layer. Over the last year or so, the company has made a point of repeatedly framing North India as its next [frontier](https://www.techinasia.com/news/indian-ev-maker-ather-energy-expand-north-india) for growth, but it’s quite likely that this may not pan out. Narayan believed that they’d need to go deeper into South India instead of going north. “Are they going to open more touchpoints? Are they going to go deeper into, let’s just say, Deccan and below? Even that, to me, is where I think that battle is going to be won, not so much in the north of the country, which tends to be more motorcycle-driven.”
Kunal, however, disagreed and pointed out that going all-India would be great for Ather, regardless of how the market is structured. “Ather is still not present in 60–70% of the country. Predominantly South. So you can imagine once they have an all-India presence, their volumes will be much larger.”
Today, TVS and Bajaj together have over 50% of the market, but both my guests believed that Ather would mount a strong, credible challenge to them. Here was Kunal.
“Companies like Bajaj, TVS probably have 8,000–10,000 dealerships—points of presence. Their ability to distribute—in fact, that’s the two biggest strengths that legacy OEMs have. They have a brand, and they have distribution… If Ather has a 13–14% market share, it’s achieved that number with one-tenth the number of outlets. So, imagine if Ather gets to grow its network 10X to be equal to Bajaj. I’m pretty confident that Ather’s market share will continue to expand.”
I argued that TVS and Bajaj have both the brand and the distribution that they’d built over decades, which come in as a plus, but both my guests were less optimistic about that.
Kunal made the argument that the brand may actually be a liability, and took the example of Maruti—which is now decidedly less cool because “nobody wants to own the product that their parents had”.
On the service side, Narayan drew a sharp distinction between TVS and Bajaj. He pointed out that just because they had more centres, it wasn’t necessarily an advantage, because selling an ICE scooter and an EV scooter is completely different. The experiences need to be thought of from the ground up, and one cannot simply fit an EV into an existing ICE centre.
But there are signs that the incumbents are learning.
“Chetak up till now has a separate sales and service point. If you want to buy a Chetak vehicle, you walk into a Chetak showroom where there are no ICE products. If you want to get your Chetak serviced, you go to a Chetak service centre which is dedicated to electric. That’s how the scale-up has happened… TVS, however, has done it through the same showroom, and it’s to their credit. They are showing flexibility.”
We ended the episode by discussing what the next 12 months would look like, for both the best-case and worst-case scenario for Ather.
