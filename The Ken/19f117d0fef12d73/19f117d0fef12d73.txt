"I took leave of absence from my small company and told my brothers, don't ask me where I am or how much money I'm spending."

That was Sunil Mittal in 1991. He was 33, making push-button phones, when he saw an advertisement inviting bids for India's first mobile licences. He knew nothing about telecom. So he handed the factory to his brothers, disappeared to London for two months, hired experts to teach him the business, and spent nearly a fifth of the company’s annual profits doing it.

He later called it the best bet of his life. It was also the first of many times he'd rebuild himself and his company.

Because over the next 30 years, three forces kept trying to kill Airtel. Regulators who kept changing the rules mid-game. Technology that threatened to make his networks obsolete, wave after wave. And brutal competition, including twice from Reliance, the second time nearly fatal. I call it Airtel’s Three-Body Problem—a classic physics puzzle where three powerful forces pull in different directions, making stability almost impossible. It's also the title of Liu Cixin's acclaimed science-fiction novel. Every single time, the easy move was to defend what he'd built. Mittal chose to reinvent instead.

Intermission's third episode is out. This time, Seetharaman and I trace the story of Airtel, or really, the story of Sunil Mittal. It is, in fact, the story of three men: the boy from Ludhiana who borrowed Rs 20,000 to make bicycle parts, the chartered accountant who became the "fourth Mittal brother", and the soap salesman from Hindustan Unilever who'd one day pull off the comeback of the decade.

https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=O_dONpkY7-U

Within that story, you'll find some of the boldest calls in Indian business. When Jio landed in 2016 with everything free (voice, data, the lot), more than half the industry died or merged, and one person I spoke to gave Airtel less than a 30% chance of survival. So, Airtel did something almost no company would: it deliberately threw away 49 million of its own customers (which is the population of Spain) to rebuild a faster, premium network for the ones who'd actually pay.

It worked. Airtel's quarterly profit, which had once collapsed 87% across 12 straight quarters to just Rs 284 crore, is now around Rs 7,500 crore. It makes more money per user than Jio does. And it's become the third-most valuable company in India, with more than 650 million subscribers across the world.
