The banking regulator was in a generous mood last week. It took a step that came straight from the heart. 

On 6 March, the Reserve Bank of India (RBI) proposed that fraud victims who lose up to Rs 50,000 in a fraudulent transaction will receive a one-time lifetime compensation.

It will compensate victims up to 85% of their net loss (gross loss minus recoveries) or a maximum of Rs 25,000, whichever is lower. 

RBI governor Sanjay Malhotra said the regulator will foot this bill. It will bear 65% of the net loss amount, while the bank and the customer will each bear 10% of the loss. That is only if they report the fraud to both the cybercrime portal and their bank within five days.

“A person should learn from other people’s mistakes. But if not from others, then at least learn from one’s own mistakes. That is why we have set a limit of Rs 25,000,” Malhotra told journalists after the Monetary Policy Committee meeting in February. 

Indians have lost over Rs 4,000 crore in 2025 across 2.4 million incidents, and this number only keeps climbing every year.

One of the things that enables digital fraud is rooted in a bigger problem—the rise of dormant and inactive bank accounts.

And that problem is bigger than fraud itself.

 

Dormant trouble

Accounts become inactive if users don’t make any transactions or withdrawals for 12 months. They then slip into dormancy after 24 consecutive months of inactivity.

According to the World Bank’s Global Findex 2025 report, 25% of financial accounts in India are inactive. And it is these dormant account holders that become prime targets for fraudsters. They get dormant account holders, especially the poor, semi-literate individuals in rural areas, to reactivate their accounts by offering them a few thousand rupees in exchange for their debit card, SIM card, and banking credentials.

This turns the dormant account into an active mule account, which enables fraudsters to target other victims.

The RBI has anyway been on a mission to reduce the number of dormant accounts and the unclaimed deposits within them.

In 2014, the regulator created a fund called the Reserve Bank of India’s Depositor Education and Awareness (DEA) Fund. It was meant to hold deposits that account holders have not claimed for over 10 years.  

And the quantum of unclaimed deposits within that fund has only been swelling in size. In February, RBI declared the fund had nearly Rs 85,000 crore, including interests accrued, up from Rs 67,000 crore it had in June 2025. That’s money that belongs to the people—money that could boost household consumption and savings. 

But, with account holders not naming nominees and the onerous claim procedures, especially for those living abroad, unclaimed deposits keep rising, according to a banking consultant. 

Both the government and the RBI are trying to reverse some of this. The RBI is also running a year-long effort until September 2026, incentivising banks to encourage people to activate their inoperative accounts and claim their deposits.

Banks will get 5% of the amount or Rs 5,000, whichever is less, for an account which is inoperative for up to 4 years, and the same will go up to 7.5% or Rs 25,000, whichever is less, in case of an account that is inoperative for over ten years, as per the RBI.

RBI launches scheme to reduce stock of unclaimed deposits by incentivising banks, Economic Times

The RBI said the incentives are taking effect. About Rs 1,000 crore a month is being paid out from the DEA fund, compared to just about Rs 100–200 crore before the incentive.  

It is also pushing banks to make it easier to claim funds from dormant accounts through simpler KYC norms, such as video-based updation or KYC updation across branches, not just the home branch. 

Right now, the RBI is solving both problems separately in two different ways. But if either of them lands, the sum of the wins will be greater than its parts.
