Good Morning Ishan,
If India has a ground zero for drone warfare, it is Chhattisgarh.
Long before Operation Sindoor thrust military drones into the national spotlight—and before the government said in June it would spend $2 billion on combat drones, with the Army, Navy, and Air Force alone expected to spend that much on surveillance and combat drones over the next two to three years—India’s security forces had, over the past five or six years, settled on the drone they trusted most.
In the forests of Chhattisgarh, where drones became central to anti-Maoist operations, small quadcopters flew ahead of patrols, scanning for ambushes before troops moved. Officers say using them became standard operating procedure. If one crashed, it was quickly replaced, and the operation continued.
The drone of choice was made by the Chinese company Da-Jiang Innovations, or DJI. And that preference has spread across India’s security establishment.
The Ken found that the Chhattisgarh Police, the Central Reserve Police Force, the Border Security Force, the Indo-Tibetan Border Police, and even the Army use DJI drones from Jammu & Kashmir to the northeastern parts of India. This correspondent spoke to at least half a dozen Army and paramilitary officers, four DJI dealers across India, three Indian drone companies, and multiple training centres that work with the security forces.
The dependence exists in direct tension with the government’s own policy. Rules issued in 2020 barred businesses from neighbouring countries from participating in government procurement. In February 2022, the Directorate General of Foreign Trade went further, prohibiting drone imports outright, allowing security forces to bring them in only with special government authorisation.
Yet, The Ken found that the ban has instead spawned an elaborate ecosystem to keep DJI in the hands of Indian security forces.
