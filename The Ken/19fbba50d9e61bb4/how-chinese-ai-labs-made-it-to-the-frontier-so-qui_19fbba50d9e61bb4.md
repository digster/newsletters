Good Morning Ishan,
The Space Race was one of the greatest competitions between the Soviet Union and the United States. It was an era of dreams, when nationalistic sentiment mingled with a literal journey to the moon. By the time Neil Armstrong planted the first human footprint on the lunar surface in 1969, China had not launched a single satellite, despite Mao Zedong declaring more than a decade prior that the People’s Republic would do precisely that. It was in 1970, amid the chaos and devastation of the Cultural Revolution, that China would finally put a satellite in space. The country would blast a human into orbit much later, in 2003, a full 42 years after Yuri Gagarin’s history-making flight. Now, China’s Tiangong space station is a smaller but fully functional rival to the International Space Station, and it will likely outlast the ISS.
Jump forward to the late 2010s, Chinese technology giant Huawei had become the world’s dominant supplier of 5G network equipment. It held more than 3,100 essential 5G patents, roughly 20% of the global total. Its American counterpart Qualcomm, in contrast, controlled around 10%. Consider the actual figures of 5G base stations that were built and the gap becomes more staggering: 4 million in China vs. 270,000 in the US. American companies such as Motorola and Lucent had once dominated global telecommunications, but the innovation that knocked around within their walls no longer exists.
The technological dynamic between these two great powers has for decades been China catching up to the US, at times surpassing it. That speed of closing the gap and reaching parity has been accelerating. In hindsight, the “Deepseek moment” was a preview of what’s happening now, with Chinese AI labs giving their Silicon Valley counterparts a run for their money.
But why and how are Chinese labs getting so good, so quickly?

Tanmai Gopal built Hasura into infrastructure that most founders dream about—an open-source engine with 600 million downloads, with half the Fortune 500 companies as customers. But then ChatGPT made him decide his own company was facing an existential risk: if AI can write the code, what’s the point of a tool that helps you write it faster?
He ended up running the company through three lives in 18 months: first as Hasura the GraphQL engine, then as PromptQL the enterprise “AI analyst” that raised a $25 million Series B and a $100 million Series C on the way to a billion-dollar-plus valuation, and now as a multiplayer AI workspace pitched as a Slack killer. This was after moving his team off the collaboration platform in February and living through what he calls three weeks of “AI psychosis”.
In this episode, Praveen and Brady speak with Tanmai about all of that and explore why there isn’t an analytics agent yet. Tanmai also describes the 30-day sprint that produced his team’s current product and what it feels like when every word in a shared online workspace becomes context for an agent to act upon. He believes that we are at the precipice of a fast takeoff and predicts layoffs “the size of which we’ve never seen” within six to nine months, with only the people who can provide context surviving in the long run.
