I’ll come back to this chart, but before that, let me tell you a bit about why I’m talking about Emergent today. 

Unless you’ve been living under a rock (a good place to be of late, I’ll admit), you’d have heard Emergent being described as India’s brightest star in the fast-expanding AI universe. It’s a vibe-coding startup, and its value proposition is that it helps users with no coding experience build a functional, monetisable app or website.

But Emergent isn’t just any regular vibe-coding startup. 

Emergent was born exactly a year ago. 

And since then, it’s been on a tear. 

In less than 90 days since launch, it claimed an annualised revenue run rate (ARR) of $15 million.

By January this year, it scaled that to $50 million.

By February, that number was $100 million. 

By most conventional measures, Emergent claimed to become one of the fastest-growing consumer startups in the world… ever. It was pretty impressive, if not astonishing. 

In a story written by my colleague Arundhati a few months back, Mukund Jha, CEO and co-founder of Emergent, explains their userbase: 

Most of Emergent’s customers use the platform for free, gaining 10 credits to test out their idea. Beyond that, users have to pay $20 a month, which will fetch them 100 credits, to build, deploy, and host their apps. The company has 150,000–200,000 paid users, according to Jha, of whom 30–40% are “power users”, who spend over $200 a month. The company also makes money when people top up their credits.

Emergent founder Mukund Jha’s job depends on not worrying about the next AI model, The Ken

It all sounded wonderful—yet something about it had nagged at me

Where are Emergent’s users? 

Historically, all great consumer products follow the same script. It begins with a small, fanatical cohort who discovers it. Those users can’t stop talking about it—on forums, in group chats, at their desks, and on social media. Sometimes, referral codes and invites are sold at a premium online. Word of mouth becomes the engine. Then paid marketing arrives, pouring fuel on a fire that the early users started. All popular consumer products in India, from Cred, Zomato, and Groww, followed a variant of this playbook. 

And that’s true for AI companies as well. Cursor, which held the record as the AI company that got to $100 million in about 12 months before Emergent, got so popular with developers that it practically became shorthand for coding itself. Lovable created a culture where users couldn’t stop sharing all the beautiful websites they’d vibe-coded. And when Claude went down for a few minutes, the internet came to a halt.

Emergent has none of that. 

Search for it on Hacker News, and you’ll struggle to find anyone talking about it. All the stuff that other AI companies like Lovable, Replit and co seem to have, i.e., the showcase culture, the loyal cohort, the community of users, seems to be missing. 

So how do we square Emergent’s extraordinary revenue with its equally perplexing “invisible” users?

And to come back to the chart I shared above, it’s pretty evident that Emergent has started pouring fuel on the fire. 

I can see the fuel, but I cannot find the fire itself.

That’s the question I try to answer.

And to find out, I talk to Gaurav Bisen, founder of Masonry AI and the person who built Emergent’s early growth engine—zero to $20 million ARR in 120 days with zero paid ads—and with Sumanth Raghavendra, co-founder and CEO of Presentations.AI and serial entrepreneur who has watched the same pattern play out with his own product. 

I come in as a sceptic and ask them to bring evidence and an explanation. 

And well, we had a great discussion around that. And to understand the mystery of Emergent’s missing users, we talk about what kind of users use vibe-coding apps, why Emergent chose influencer marketing, how its economics and business work, and of course, the numbers and comparison with Lovable.

Invisibility is a healthy sign

The conventional wisdom is that great products generate visible word of mouth. Gaurav challenges me on that by arguing that Emergent’s users are invisible precisely because the product is working. I wasn’t fully convinced, but he was able to bring in his own experience to the table:

Gaurav Bisen: That invisibility doesn’t mean non-existence. Invisibility could be hidden by people. They are actually coming, they are actually using it. I have a couple of users who are on my platform who are actually making videos for their own personal needs and are working in agencies. Are they posting it somewhere? No, they are doing some freelance work which they have got. So they are making some side income but are not posting on their platform.

When you have to flip…

When we were discussing Emergent’s users, one of the things I kept coming back to was the paid user chart—clearly Emergent was burning money to boost paid marketing over the last few weeks and months, which is a big deviation from how they were acquiring users earlier through influencer marketing.

According to Sumanth, this was a compulsion.

Sumanth Raghavendra: Paid ads work only when there is search intent. When there is search volume, if somebody says that, okay, I want to build software for something, then paid ads work because you can essentially capture that intent. The reason why influencer marketing was the only way in which Emergent could actually take off was that they’re actually creating that category where that demand was never actually—never had a way to articulate itself. Everything else that flows from there is a function of that starting point—you’re now catering to people who did not even have an idea that something like this was possible.

Not your usual software company

We were discussing users when Sumanth deftly pivoted the episode to talk about Emergent’s business itself. In his mind, Emergent is not in the business of software creation or vibe-coding, but is instead a part of a much deeper market. 

Sumanth: I actually think that Emergent is not really in the business of software creation. Because the software creation part anyway, is primarily a pass-through to Claude. My argument is that Emergent becomes an operating system for software where creation is only the thin wedge. It is a loss leader in a sense, but everything after that—deployment, monetisation, distribution, maintenance—is really what Emergent needs to monetise and needs to do a good job of.

At one point, I used Lovable’s 100 million visits vs Emergent’s 20 million as evidence of invisibility. Sumanth pushed back and argued that the traffic isn’t to Lovable the app, it’s to all the apps built on Lovable.

Sumanth: If Lovable’s number one use case is websites and landing pages, that means their traffic will naturally be higher. Because that traffic is not the traffic to Lovable the app, it is to Lovable the platform, which includes all the apps that have been built on Lovable.

Cursor should not exist. GitHub Copilot had everything. It didn’t matter.

All through the conversation, Gaurav had an argument which he repeated again and again—it’s all about positioning, and some positioning beats other kinds of positioning. 

This was his point about why Emergent’s users were invisible, because the positioning of the company is different from that of Lovable or the others.

Gaurav: Cursor should have not existed. Github Copilot was there, should have been the winner. GitHub couldn’t do it. Can you really think of it? A company like Github had Copilot with them. $900 million in terms of the kind of user base they had. Lovable as a company should have not existed. Webflow had everything. Framer had everything. What would it have taken? It’s just positioning. It’s so hard once you have built a generational company, or once you have actually achieved a benchmark, to go into someone else’s territory and win that entirely as your positioning.

These are some of the highlights of our conversation. 

The episode also discusses the much-feared scenario of Claude killing Emergent by doing software creation natively, but our guests have a different take on it. And that is where Emergent’s real opportunity is. 

It also goes into other explanations, like why Emergent’s users don’t talk about the product, what kind of use-cases do they use the product for, and why AI companies are now in a completely different mode of growth. We discuss parallels with Meesho and Clue.ly, and, of course, why the old rules of users and network effects don’t seem to apply to new-age companies like Emergent. 

Oh, and at the very end, I make a prediction about what kind of evidence we’ll need to see to discover Emergent’s users. 

I remain a sceptic, but slightly less so.
