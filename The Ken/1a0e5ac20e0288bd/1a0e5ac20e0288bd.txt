More than two decades into the Indian Administrative Service (IAS), JS Deepak was sacked in late 2007 as the state project director of Sarva Shiksha Abhiyan in Uttar Pradesh. 

He filed an FIR against more than 200 schools found cheating to claim central subsidies. The list included schools owned by influential politicians in Chief Minister Mulayam Singh Yadav’s cabinet. When Deepak said he couldn’t withdraw the FIR, citing legal provisions, he got his marching orders and landed “in the inconsequential post of secretary programme implementation”. 

“I went about my new nothing job, pretending that I was not bothered by my transfer. Only to get a message from Anita [Singh, special secretary to the CM] that I should display hurt and pain on being transferred, as my normal behaviour may be misinterpreted as arising out of insufficient punishment,” he writes in his memoir, I Dared to Dial Differently.  

Deepak escaped suspension by a whisker. 

Those familiar with Indian bureaucracy will find his book confirming their biases; those blissfully distant will uncover facts that are stranger than fiction. 

Long before Deepak and many other retired bureaucrats’ memoirs took readers inside the atmosphere of officialdom in India, Upamanyu Chatterjee, then a serving officer, took an insider’s satiric lens to the institution of the IAS with his acclaimed book English, August. Deepak doesn’t have the creative freedom of fiction that Chatterjee had in the late 1980s, but he keeps his prose funny and his examples telling for the most part.
