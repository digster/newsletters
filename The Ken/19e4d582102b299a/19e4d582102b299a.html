A long time ago, I used to be the Product Manager at an e-commerce company’s customer service department, which essentially meant I’d build and maintain products to drive down costs and increase efficiency. I still remember the metrics I’d stare at every morning that would tell me how I was doing: Call to Order Ratio, First Contact Resolution, Call Abandonment Rate, Average Handle Time… the list went on and on. 

It was all new to me, but I distinctly recall my boss telling me, when I once suggested to him that maybe we need to expand our call centre if we really wanted to improve our customer experience, he just dismissed me with, “Praveen, just focus on making it efficient. It’s a call centre.” He added a couple of adjectives in there, which I’m not going to repeat. Still, the thrust of his message was—this is a part of our business that’s not generating any revenue for us, just keep the lights on and squeeze all the productivity out of it as you can.

There was also a cultural cost to call centres at companies. The people who worked there came from a different world, lived by different rules, and couldn’t do things like work from home or be a bit fast and loose with their schedules. Inside the same company, that’s a hard thing to hold together.

Anyway, since then, I’ve always been somewhat cautious of call centre operations. Keep them as small as possible, preferably don’t have them at all. They are just a pain in the backside. 

Well, I guess nobody told Bajaj Finance that. 

For 20 years, for the rest of us, the call centre was a problem to solve. 

But for Bajaj Finance, it turned out to be the answer. 

And it all began 18 months ago.

In December 2024, Bajaj Finance made a surprising announcement when it started talking about its plans for AI. Instead of calling itself “a lender investing in AI” (like most companies do), it started to describe itself as a “FinAI company”—a category of one, which it committed to in a five-year plan to FY29. 

Eighteen months in, no other Indian lender is putting AI on their slides the way Bajaj Finance is. Here are some numbers: 

31 million voice interactions converted into data last quarter. 

27 autonomous agents live, 118 more planned. 

6,00,000 loans on a single Diwali day, up from a pre-AI ceiling of 1,00,000. 

And this Diwali, they’re aiming for a million.

A million.

If you went back a few years ago, the obvious answer to who would win Indian finance’s AI era was the digital natives—Phonepe, Cred, the cloud-born fintechs with no call centres and zero baggage. Instead, the company that started financing two-wheelers and still runs one of India’s largest outbound calling operations is out in front. The thing that looked like Bajaj’s archaic legacy is turning out to be its moat.

What else did Bajaj Finance have that got it here? 

And how long before the others get there too?

In today’s episode, I’m joined by Seetharaman G, deputy editor at The Ken, who just spent 4.5 hours on Bajaj’s history for Intermission, our new long-form video podcast; and Vasuta Agarwal, chief revenue officer at Gnani.ai, which provides Voice AI to enterprises.

We work through four pieces of Bajaj’s edge: the data it built around its consumer-durables network and voice data, the incentive of being a publicly listed NBFC without access to cheap deposits, the leverage of buying from a voice AI market with dozens of competing vendors, and finally, the DNA that may not be replicable, i.e., the willingness to fight for every inch, sustained under one CEO since 2007.

Some of my favourite quotes from today’s episode:

Voice is harder than it looks, and the demo is the easy part

One of my central arguments was that Voice AI isn’t that hard to do—there are a million startups that offer it as a product or a service, and I thought that it would converge to become a commodity. Given this, it seems like all the leverage is with Bajaj Finance, and not the voice AI companies. 

Well, unsurprisingly, Vasuta disagreed.

When you talk about actual conversations in India, they are not happening on the web on high Wi-Fi connections like we are speaking now. They happen on a telephone line in a very noisy environment where you will hear horns, kitchen noises, and all sorts of things in the background. And that’s when a lot of things break.

She also went on to add that anyone can do a plausible demo, but the hard part comes in all the orchestration and stitching of parts together to create an end-to-end experience that works at scale, which is absolutely non-trivial.

Seetha agreed and spoke about his experience receiving a call from an agent.

In the first 10 seconds, I actually thought it was the person. I wasn’t paying attention. And then I said something, and then there was another person. That’s when I realised, oh, the first one was an agent.

The economics of voice AI are a top-line story

The default frame for voice AI is cost-out—i.e., replacing call centres and cutting operating expenses. 

But the numbers point the other way for Bajah Finance. Voice AI converts 3–4X better than human agents in lending, which means it’s a revenue line as well. 

As Vasuta explained:

At least from whatever we get informally and formally from customers, the interest rate that we see is 10–15%. And this I’m talking specifically about banking and NBFCs for different kinds of loans. It could be a gold loan, a personal loan, any kind of loan, but at a 10–15% interest rate, which, at least from an industry benchmark lens, from what I know, is almost 3 to 4 times what you would get from a human agent kind of scenario.

Seetha also pointed out a crucial difference between Bajaj Finance and the others.

Bajaj Finance, because it’s an NBFC, can only have fixed deposits and not savings account deposits. So whatever it can do on the cost front, even if it’s a little bit, goes a long way given the volume of loans that Bajaj Finance processes.

How Bajaj Finance got ahead of Phonepe and Cred

Another argument I made was that app data, i.e., details of which customers went to which screen, how they moved, how much time they spent on it, etc., was much richer than voice data. Essentially, my premise was that app-first companies like Phonepe and Cred probably know much more about their users than Bajaj Finance’s voice conversations would. 

Well, turns out, not exactly.

Voice, the thing is that it is very hard to analyse that data at scale, right? Because if you go beyond just the simple yes, no, and interactions of a customer, I think the richness of that data over a period of time can lead to very, very sort of different outcomes. So, for example, simply knowing when to contact a customer, when your connect rates and answer rates will be best, what the customer disposition and behaviour will be at that time, things like that, knowing some of that over a very large scale of data.

Seetha added his perspective.

The likes of Phonepe and Paytm, which obviously originate loans for lenders—sure, for personal loans, I would go on Paytm and look for the best deal possible, but when it comes to home loans, I would think of HDFC, I would think of SBI, I would think of Bajaj. That is the difference in what the two are selling.
