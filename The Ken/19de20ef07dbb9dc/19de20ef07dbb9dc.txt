Bengaluru doesn’t have a ‘water’ problem. As flooding and shortages coexist, the city keeps layering solutions with tankers, pipes, and now AI, without ever fixing the whole

Bengaluru is building what it calls Asia’s largest AI command centre for water. A system designed to track how drinking water moves through the city in real time and detect leaks before they spread. It will also predict demand across neighbourhoods before shortages bare their fangs.

It’s an understandable plan to take a complex, physical network and make it a little more like software. Optimised and responsive. It’s India’s IT capital, after all.

The new centre will sit on top of a system that already stretches over 14,000 km of pipelines and supplies roughly 1,450 million litres a day. That sounds massive, until you consider that that the city needs closer to 2,100 million litres and makes up the shortfall through a mix of borewells and private tankers. In a place that has built its identity on solving problems with code, this kind of solution doesn’t feel unusual; if anything, it feels inevitable.

And then it rains.

Earlier this week, a pre-monsoon storm brought over 100 mm of rain to parts of the city in a matter of hours. It was kind of a burst, I am told, that is not supposed to be catastrophic. And yet it is. Roads were soon flooded, underpasses filled up, power lines snapped, and in one part of the city, a compound wall collapsed, killing several people, including children. And it’s not even the monsoon.

Soon, what will follow will be similarly familiar. A few days or weeks later, the tankers will become more visible again, lining up outside apartment complexes and moving through neighbourhoods that the formal network doesn’t quite reach. Resident groups will start tracking supply more closely, checking borewells every now and then, and the city will slip back into a routine where water is measured in intervals.

From the outside [I am a Kolkata resident], this is the part that takes a moment to process. How does a city that floods after a few hours of rain find itself worrying about drinking water not long after? It is tempting to read this as dysfunction, but no. It’s more structural, as we have documented.

Bengaluru’s problem is not that it lacks solutions. It has many of them, each designed to address a specific failure, but rarely the system as a whole. Pipes that don’t reach where the city has already grown. Tankers that fill the gaps they leave behind. Groundwater that is drawn down faster than it can recover. And now, an AI layer that promises to make all of this more efficient.

Two systems too many

If you follow water in Bengaluru for a while, you realise there are at least two systems that don’t quite overlap. The official one, visible on maps, runs from the Cauvery through treatment plants into a network of pipes that, on paper, covers most of the city. It is budgeted yet permanently behind demand. The other system, however, is harder to map but easier to see. It arrives in tankers.

In large parts of the city, especially the newer ones that grew faster than the pipes could follow, this second system is the one that actually works. Apartment complexes don’t wait for connections that may or may not come. They call a number and get water delivered. Over time, this has stopped being a stopgap. 

The state has tried, occasionally, to formalise it. There was even an app meant to offer cheaper, GPS-tracked tanker deliveries. It was supposed to replace what politicians like to call the “tanker mafia”. It didn’t quite work.

Outsource the problem

At some point, the line between the two systems begins to blur. Tankers are hired from the same operators they are meant to regulate. Contracts are handed out to private companies to fix supply gaps and build infrastructure. The work moves outward, into layers of contractors and subcontractors, until responsibility becomes difficult to locate.

You can see this most clearly in how the water crisis is being “solved”. Companies that step in with engineering solutions to lift irrigation, recharge projects, pipeline extensions are often backed by government contracts. Some of them are run by people who were, until recently, part of the same system they are now supplying to.

Infra arrives before the system

Of course, the urban-infra issues aren’t limited to water.

Or take electric buses. The city is adding them quickly, as part of a national push towards cleaner mobility. But the power infrastructure that supports them hasn’t kept pace. When multiple buses charge at the same depot, nearby neighbourhoods feel the voltage drop. The grid adjusts in real time, because it has to.

In both cases, capacity is added, and usage follows immediately. It’s the underlying system that catches up later, if at all.

Refuses to decentralise

Part of the pressure comes from the fact that Bengaluru is still expected to do too much.

For years, Karnataka has talked about building alternative tech hubs in Mysuru, Hubballi, Mangaluru, but the actual machinery of decision-making remains concentrated in Bengaluru. Approvals, infrastructure planning, investment—all roads lead back here.

Meanwhile, neighbouring states have started doing something different. Instead of building one dominant city, they are building networks in tier-2 cities with their own infrastructure and administrative capacity.

One model concentrates demand and then tries to manage it. The other distributes demand before it becomes unmanageable. Bengaluru is still very much the former. Which means every new industry, every new company, every new worker adds to a system that is already stretched across water, transport, housing, and power.

There is a tendency, in cities like Bengaluru, to think of infrastructure as a supply problem. 

Not enough water? More pipelines. More tankers!

Not enough roads? More buses! 

Not enough power? More charging stations. 

Now, more data!

What is harder to address is demand. Not just how much water a city uses, but how it uses it. Who reduces consumption when supply tightens. Who adapts, and who doesn’t. Other cities have had to confront this directly.

Here’s how Cape Town, during its near “Day Zero” crisis, forced a behavioural shift and a city-wide effort to reduce usage. Bengaluru, by and large, has largely avoided this conversation.

Seen this way, the AI command centre doesn’t look out of place. It fits perfectly. It is another layer of “optimisation” added to a system that has grown by solving problems in parts rather than as a whole. 

And it may well work, in the way many of Bengaluru’s solutions do.
