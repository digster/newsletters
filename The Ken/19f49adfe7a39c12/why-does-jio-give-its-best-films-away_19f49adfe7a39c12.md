Two By Two
Fri, 10 Jul 26
An abridged, narrative version of the latest episode of Two by Two, The Ken’s premium weekly business podcast.
Good Morning Ishan,
One of the things about Two By Two is that I try to come in every week with a hypothesis: essentially a story of what should be happening, but isn’t.
And well, today’s episode for me began when I saw this.
Huh.
So here is Jio Studios, which made India’s biggest film, and then gave away the streaming rights to Netflix, essentially a competitor.
How does this make sense?
As long-time readers know, I have a long history of writing about Disney, Hotstar, Jio, and Netflix. In fact, after Jio took over Hotstar completely and Disney left the scene, I [wrote](https://the-ken.com/the-nutgraf/disney-leaves-jio-the-keys-to-the-kingdom/) that Jio is now in the prime position to run the Disney playbook in India—essentially a complete, vertically integrated, loop and system that locks in customers from multiple entry points and runs them through the mightiest content flywheel in the country, and possibly the world.
In 2017, Disney did something that looked, at the time, like leaving money on the table. It was earning handsomely by licensing its films to Netflix—Marvel, Star Wars, Pixar, the most valuable catalogue in entertainment—and one day it walked away from all of it. It pulled the content, absorbed the lost cheques, and spent the money building Disney+ instead.
The reasoning was one of every integrated media company since: if you own the content and you own the pipe, you never rent your crown jewels to a rival. The content feeds the platform, the platform feeds subscriptions, the subscriptions fund the next film, and the brand, the data, and the franchise all stay in-house. Apple runs the same closed loop. So does Amazon. Owning both a studio and a streaming service is supposed to mean one feeds the other.
Back in 1957, Disney founder Walt Disney laid out the company’s strategy in a sketch he made on a napkin. This is known as Disney’s “Synergy Map”.
And inspired by this, I drew Jio’s synergy map a couple of years back, updated for the new era.
The part that you should be interested in is the line that links Jio’s exclusive content with OTT streaming along the axis of “brings in advertising and subscriptions”. Well, as far as Dhurandhar is concerned, Jio has different ideas. It’s not just about one movie.
In a recent interview, Jyoti Deshpande, the head of Jio Studios, [said](https://economictimes.indiatimes.com/industry/media/entertainment/jio-studios-invests-5000-crore-in-content-amid-box-office-challenges/articleshow/131161257.cms?from=mdr) that “98% of our content is being released on ‘other’ platforms.” Platforms include Netflix and Amazon Prime Video.
So clearly, nobody told Jio about this plan. And none of this made any sense to me.
So what does Jio know that the rest of us don’t?
To find this answer, I roped in two guests—they are both seasoned senior executives, one from Disney and later Amazon Prime, and the other from Netflix. Incidentally, both of them now produce movies.
And one of them comes from a family with a long lineage in Hindi cinema.
Vijay Subramaniam is the founder of 29th September Works. He spent 11 years at Walt Disney India as VP of media networks, then built Amazon Prime Video India’s content engine as head of content. Now, he is also a movie producer.
Srishti Behl is the founder of Starfish Stories, working on IP and international co-productions. She’s also the former director of original films at Netflix India (Bulbbul, Serious Men, AK vs AK) and former CEO of Phantom Studios.
And we had a great discussion.
I opened by asking each of them whether they were surprised by what Jio was doing. “I’m not surprised at all,” Vijay said, before I’d even finished the question. Neither was Srishti. And immediately, the lines were drawn—it was me against both of them.
For me, the question stopped being “Is this a mistake?” and swiftly changed to “Why do two people who have run streaming platforms think closing the loop would be a mistake?”
Vijay laid out three reasons, and the first became the theme he returned to all through the episode: you cannot monopolise storytelling.
His analogy even predated video entirely. He argued that no single publisher, and no cartel of publishers, has ever cornered the market for books, because the barriers to entry might be high, but the opportunity to break through can come from anywhere. Stories don’t behave like the moats tech people (I think he was talking about me) are used to describing—the ones that are too expensive to cross.
His point was, in content, someone will always find a way across, whether through the shape of the story, the way it gets made, or where it gets distributed.
His second reason was that a platform and a studio are two genuinely different businesses, not two halves of one.
Srishti made the same point from the numbers: inside Reliance, Jio Studios and the streaming service sit as separate P&Ls, with separate expectations, each expected to monetise on its own terms.
But Vijay pushed it past accounting into philosophy. A platform has to programme for everybody—in his own home, he pointed out, what he watches, what his spouse watches and what his parents watch are three different things, so a platform’s hunger has to be fed on a 24/7 basis. A studio can afford to be deliberate. It can be discreet about its choices, leave opportunities it doesn’t want, and get good at one thing until it becomes the gold standard at it—which by definition means there is a great deal it is not good at.
Disney made kids and family its fortress, and precisely because that fortress had walls, Marvel and Star Wars had to happen elsewhere.
His third reason was organisational DNA—building durable IP is a decade-long effort of singular focus, something no technological advantage can shorten, and he thought Jio Studios had understood this early and chosen to play the long game.
Srishti’s own framing was blunter. She dismissed the idea that Jio had shown a lack of conviction by giving Dhurandhar away. She wasn’t talking about conviction at all, she said—she was talking about hedging your bets.
“Everything doesn’t have to be a do or die. I think conviction is a bit overblown. At the end of the day it’s called show business, not show show, you know.”
Dhurandhar, on this reading, was a vastly over-budget, over-long film that had been broken in two, sold non-exclusively while the studio hedged, and only when it went through the roof did Part 2 come back in-house. As she put it, echoing an old industry line about Sholay: “Sholay banate nahi hai, ban jaati hai”
You don’t manufacture the biggest blockbuster ever. It happens to you.
All this is fine, but then I decided to bring it back to Jio Studios.
So in defence, I brought up a [piece](https://the-ken.com/story/with-jio-studios-reliance-conquers-the-final-frontier-of-its-media-empire/?utm_source=web&utm_medium=homepage&utm_campaign=con_1_unit_5) by Debanjali, my colleague at The Ken, “Jio Studios strikes it rich by being exactly what its rivals gave up on”. In it, she wrote that Reliance’s film business was a throwback to the old integrated studio model just as Bollywood’s biggest producers were cashing out. Vijay disagreed with it, once again, sharply and immediately (this was becoming a pattern).
His objection was to the very idea that you can measure this business the way the story did. There is, he said, no market share in film. One year might see 300 releases, the next 150; nobody sets the number, nobody says an industry must release one film a week for 52 weeks. The market is neither saturated nor uniformly measured, and India lacks the reporting standards—like the American Rentrak, a comparable audited box-office source—that would make the numbers speak in one voice.
“The very same numbers can be flipped,” he said, to tell whichever story a pitch requires: India as a guaranteed-returns goldmine, or India as a place where the unit economics are broken by too few screens. Zoom in on this business and it looks like intraday stock trading; you have to zoom out, because nobody has ever thought of a film on Monday, made it Tuesday, and released it Wednesday.
So what, if anything, makes Jio Studios structurally different from the others?
Broadly nothing, Vijay conceded—it’s bigger, better capitalised, further along the path to being professionally run. The one real exception is its access to a set of non-theatrical distribution businesses: the TV networks for satellite, the streaming platform for digital.
And here he introduced the metaphor that would organise the rest of the conversation: Those owned platforms are a lifeboat. Something to reach for in case they need it, not the vessel they set out to sail.
At this point, I interrupted (hah!) and said I didn’t agree that they were lifeboats, and that seeing them that way might be the industry getting it backwards.
But that’s the core of the disagreement: I wanted the platform to be the destination.
My guests saw it as insurance.
So I tried another tack.
Netflix and Amazon are famously data-driven; surely there were moments when the metrics screamed that a certain kind of content was underserved, and surely you wished you had a studio you could simply hand that brief to. As an example, I invoked House of Cards, and Sacred Games—both examples where the data led to the content being created by in-house studios.
Srishti attacked the same fantasy from the creative side.
“But why will I run an organisation to do that,” she said, “When I can give that same brief to 300 people?” India has enormous content competence at every level; you can broadcast a brief widely without owning anyone.
And the premise that data produces hits was, she said, simply false: “If that were the case that you could just look at data simply and create blockbusters, then nobody would make a flop.” Whether a gap is genuine is a human call. “Is it underserved or is it underperforming? That is left to the executive.”
Owning the studio was also, in her view, self-defeating, because it flattens the very diversity of minds and lived experiences that makes something connect. She kept circling one unanswerable question as her proof: “When you’re looking at the numbers of Sacred Games compared to the numbers of Kyunki Saas Bhi Kabhi Bahu Thi, what is success?”
Vijay backed her with the version he’d learned in the buyer’s chair: even setting aside quality, an owned studio is still just a vendor with its own agenda. “There are 300 studios that we can brief,” he said, “but you don’t control them.”
The illusion is that control buys you something.
In his view, it mostly buys you obligation.
I ended the episode where I began, and asked what it would take for Reliance to close the loop the way Disney, Apple, and Amazon did.
Vijay offered two scenarios, neither of them the flywheel.
The first is time—decades of building IP powerful enough that an exclusive link finally makes sense. The second is a change in the weather: “It can change if all the other buyers exited,” he said. “Assume there’s a big drought for a period of time. That’s why I called it the lifeboat.”
He pointed to cricket, where the BCCI faces what is effectively a market of one; and to the smaller-language markets that pushed Stage, the Indian OTT platform specialising in regional-language content, toward integration, because “they may just find it more efficient to build everything from the ground up.”
Srishti was much more sceptical. “It’s a matter of time,” she allowed, “but it’s a little bit utopian to believe that there’ll be a platform which is being exclusively served by one production house.” To extract the real value of your content, you need “the independence to be able to get the value that you believe your content is going to get,” and a market of one makes that impossible.
“Somebody or the other,” she said, “will have to suffer.”
Which is the paradox of the episode.
Reliance owns every piece of the machine that made Disney. The people who ran the platforms it would be feeding think switching that machine on would be a mistake.
And so the biggest studio in the country will keep making the biggest films in the country—and, for now, keep giving them away.
You should listen to the episode. We talk about other aspects too, like what exactly is a studio, how companies like Stage have created an exception, and why Bollywood is going through a difficult time.

Praveen
