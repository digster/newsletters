Inside KKR and Blackstone’s ‘platformisation’ of Indian healthcare

Acquiring beds insatiably, earning more per bed, reducing employee benefits—private equity’s operational and financial engineering is kicking in fast

Blackstone and KKR now sit atop close to 100 Indian hospitals between them, with KKR’s August buyout of Medicover valued at 46X Ebitda. The old game of buying private, listing public, and pocketing the arbitrage is slowly vanishing. Across PE-owned hospitals, doctor consultation fees are compounding 3–7 percentage points faster than pay for salaried staff. Hospitals are paying more for the patient flow and reputation that star doctors bring with them. Goodwill routinely makes up over 70% of the price paid in Indian hospital acquisitions, roughly double the global norm. Buyers are paying a steep premium for brand value and other intangibles. A parliamentary committee has flagged corporate hospital consolidation and the high tariffs that come with it, and is weighing price caps on these tariffs. [Read full story here]

TAS was Tata’s leadership factory. Now it’s a glorified recruitment agency

Once seen as Tata’s dark horse, Tata Administrative Services has since lost its power within the group

In its 70th year, Tata’s flagship general management programme seems to be struggling to keep pace with the group’s growing leadership demands. What benefits TAS officers—higher pay, cross-company mobility, no function-based restrictions—is exactly what companies struggle to accommodate. Plus, without Ratan Tata’s backing, many TAS officers today struggle to wield the same level of influence as their predecessors did. The programme is in need of a revamp, so that its prestige can match the crucial role it once played within the Tata group. [Read full story here]

Airtel Money is worth $7 billion because Africa never got UPI

Airtel Payments Bank takes roughly the same cut on every rupee as Airtel Money does on every shilling. The African wallet just moves far more. With its sibling now trading in London, the Indian business has a year to explain the difference

Airtel Money, which completes its London listing this week at about $7 billion, moves cash across 13 African countries with more than 2.3 million shopkeeper agents. Bharti Airtel runs a similar business at home through Airtel Payments Bank, which takes a similar cut of every payment and must list by next year. Last year, though, Airtel Money made $373 million in profit and the Indian bank made about $12 million, because UPI has taken most of India’s payments and left the bank a shrinking cash niche. That is what the bank’s bankers will have to price, and it comes just as Africa’s own UPI-style payment switches start to cut into what makes Airtel Money so valuable.  [Read full story here]

Google and Amazon’s newfound fascination with India’s paddy fields

India’s voluntary carbon market has startups, farms, and self-audited private registries. Big tech is buying from it anyway

Google, Amazon, and Microsoft are scrambling to meet their internal net-zero targets, with the data centre build-out pushing their emissions upwards. A new crop of startups in India are betting on generating carbon credits by introducing “ecologically sustainable” practices on Indian farms. Yet, the methodology for validating these credits is untested at scale and rice-methane credits have been embroiled in scandal. There are also questions about how much of the revenue drips down to the farmer and partner organisations, as well as how much real change is delivered on the ground. [Read the full story]
