When engineers in Silicon Valley were racing to build the world’s most powerful AI chatbot, no one warned them that Indian bureaucracy would one day remake it in its own image.

Like any good official, government chatbots these days answer questions with files.

Take Asksarkar, a government chatbot offering “authentic information from government and verified sources”. Ask simple questions like “I want to create a startup. What government schemes can I avail?” and it returns a list featuring Pradhan Mantri Awas Yojana and Swachh Bharat Mission. Basically, everything under the sun except for startups. Ask about AI policies, and it offers a few links. The first leads to a 170-page PDF, the second to the Wikipedia page of the US’ Defence Advanced Research Projects Agency, and the third to the IndiaAI Mission website. 

…

Following the AI Impact Summit in February, it asked every ministry to submit AI proposals on how they planned to use AI. By early this month, the National Informatics Centre (NIC)—one of Meity’s arms—had cleared 47 of 90 proposals. Of the 80 companies that bid to work with NIC and the National e-Governance Division (Negd), Meity has empanelled six private firms to supply manpower and AI tools to ministries. 

Yet ministries are looking beyond these six. A Meity official told The Ken that several are independently in talks with global players like Elon Musk’s xAI and Anthropic, both of which are actively vying for their own government partnerships. If anyone thinks global contracts can solve India’s AI problems, they are in for a surprise. Unstructured data and numerous government updates account for only half of those problems.

That scramble says less about India’s appetite for AI than about how little agreement there is on how and what it should be used for. 

Somewhere in that rush, ministries are discovering that before teaching AI how the government works, someone first has to teach the government how AI works.
