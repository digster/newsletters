“Don’t adopt a pet.”

That is a line from Anthropic in an engineering post this week about its new “managed agents”. It borrows from an old cloud-computing idea: treat systems more like cattle. It is also a fairly good description of where the AI industry finds itself right now.

The problem the company describes is everything that comes after intelligence. If an autonomous system is too dependent on a specific setup or workflow, the builder ends up with something that has to be constantly nursed back to health. A pet. What you actually want, Anthropic argues, is something more interchangeable and controlled; something that is also easier to restart when it fails. Like cattle.

Which is a great way to think about “agents”, a term that has suddenly become unavoidable.

Elsewhere, the story is more expansive, to put it politely. Meta wants codebases that AI can rewrite. Microsoft is turning Copilot into something that can run workflows. OpenAI is building systems to deploy these tools across companies. And Perplexity AI has reportedly seen revenue jump about 50% in a month after leaning into this direction.

Put differently, the pitch is that it’s about time that software, instead of just responding to instructions, started acting on them. The implementation, at least so far, looks closer to Anthropic’s version.

Mainly because autonomy, while attractive, also raises questions about cost and reliability, as well as the slightly unnerving tendency of these systems to be confidently wrong. So what gets built tends to be narrower than what gets described. Systems that operate within constraints and handle specific workflows. 

Systems that are designed to fail safely. And that gap between the ambition and the implementation is where things get interesting.

It is also what we’ll spend time on at our upcoming Zero Shot event. What happens when there is less need for users to browse and decide, the very thing platforms have historically relied on?

Say, your next order never touches Zomato because a system finds the same meal cheaper elsewhere and routes it directly. Payment apps like Phonepe or Google Pay recede as transactions move straight through APIs. Platforms like Policybazaar compress into a query that takes seconds.

Moving on, The Ken’s reporting has been tracking how AI is already changing businesses in more immediate ways.

Selling speed, buying time

If AI can do the work, what happens to the people who charge for doing it?

Consulting firms like BCG and McKinsey are selling AI as speed. That means faster insights and quicker execution lead to fewer billable hours. It’s an easy pitch when clients believe AI can compress timelines. Inside the firms, though, adoption is slower and more selective. If AI actually delivers on that promise, it changes how consultants work as well as what clients are willing to pay for.

In a world where AI can increasingly do the work, the premium shifts from execution to judgment. Quite a hard thing to scale.

Middleman is optional

Take something simpler: choosing insurance. Today, platforms like Policybazaar exist because comparing policies is hard. It takes time, effort, and a tolerance for fine print.

Now imagine that work collapsing into 90 seconds. Banks and financial institutions are already sitting on the raw material, like risk data and customer profiles. AI helps them underwrite better and bypass the interface entirely.

Which is why some banks are starting to monetise that data directly, turning insights into products, and products into distribution. (Read: advertising). Banks like HDFC and Kotak are already using years of behavioural data to predict what you might buy next. Besides, there’s no oversight on this yet.

Workflows don’t wait

While consumer platforms are dealing with thinner discovery, enterprises are dealing with standardisation.

Companies like TCS and Cognizant are embedding AI into everyday work, including contracts and tooling. Engineers are expected to use AI coding tools, whether they prefer them or not. Part of this is practical. Enterprise agreements bundle these tools in, and switching becomes difficult. 

Part of it is also strategic. If AI changes how code is written, firms want that change to happen inside their systems. Developers may complain that the tools slow them down or require more verification. That matters less at the organisational level. Because this is not about productivity yet.

“Developers will always scrutinise output quality. Management, on the other hand, asks, ‘Are we AI-enabled?’ Usage dept and productivity nuance are second-order questions”

Former manager, Tata Consultancy Services

The margin is the opportunity

To me, the most interesting impact of AI is what it exposes.

Every platform margin, including commissions, convenience fees, and “hidden charges”, exists because users don’t have the time or tools to optimise for themselves. If an agent can compare the options and route every transaction to the cheapest alternative, then margins may look inefficient. Which is why companies like Bajaj Finance are leaning into AI as infrastructure. They are tightening pricing and competing on precision.

The game shifts from attracting users to winning the decision.

The funding reality check

This is also why the funding mood around AI has shifted.

Instead of chasing “AI-powered” as a category, investors are saying, Let’s see what happens when AI removes friction rather than adding value.

Because if a business depends on discovery and commissions, then AI is not a tailwind. Which is why VCs are becoming more selective.

“There is a realisation that the gestation period to get to produt-market-fit is longer and needs more capital in AI. That’s why there are far fewer deals and far more capital.”

Arun Raghavan, Managing Partner, Arali Ventures

AI didn’t kill services. It rewired them

AI was supposed to reduce the need for human-heavy businesses like outsourcing. It has made them more valuable.

Someone still has to train and operate these systems. Call centres and BPOs are still not complete casualties. That explains deals like Capgemini acquiring WNS. The labour has moved closer to the machine.
