Trade Tricks
Tue, 14 Jul 26
A weekly newsletter about the biggest changes in commerce—focusing on shifts that matter to you.
Good Morning Ishan,
If you’re anything like me, you’ve probably spent the last few months glued to multiple screens at once, constantly cursing at world-class athletes and thinking about how you could have done a better job from the absolute comfort of your bed. It’s been a sports season for the books.
Sunday’s gentlemen’s singles final at Wimbledon was yet another thriller. The four-set clincher had us in its clutches from the get-go as Jannik Sinner went neck and neck with Alexander Zverev, fresh off his French Open victory. But Sinner, the Italian top seed, eventually raced past him to defend his title.
While the drama unfolded over two weeks in southwest London, for Indians, the attraction was closer to home—a strawberries-and-cream kulfi at Kuremal Kulfis in Delhi.
“What relation does Wimbledon, India, and kulfi have?” Vishal Sharma, the fourth-generation owner of Kuremal’s Kulfi, recalled thinking when he first got a call in late March from the All England Lawn Tennis Club (AELTC), the company that runs and organises Wimbledon.
But AELTC wanted to collaborate with his brand. And the brief was clear and specific: strawberries and cream, a Wimbledon tradition since the 1870s, were non-negotiable.
After multiple trials and a visit from the AELTC team, the strawberries and cream kulfi stick went live on 22 June, the day Wimbledon began, and was available across four of Kuremal’s outlets in Delhi until yesterday.
But what’s more interesting than the kulfi is what Sharma didn’t ask for—he didn’t charge Wimbledon anything for the collaboration.
For a small business owner, not charging Wimbledon made sense. The marketing and branding they got from this deal were worth way more than what they could have charged AELTC. And that gamble is already paying off.
Sharma told The Ken that they sold around 2,500 special-edition kulfi sticks per day, each priced at Rs 80. That’s over Rs 2 lakh in daily sales from just one flavour. At an 18% profit margin, Sharma says the kulfi would amount to roughly Rs 36,000 in profit per day.
Which raises the obvious question—why is a 149-year-old tennis institution going to this length for a kulfi shop in Old Delhi? Turns out it’s part of a much bigger playbook.
“India continues to be an important market for us,” an AELTC spokesperson told The Ken. “It is our biggest international market and one we want to continue growing.”
That’s not new.
In 2022, the AELTC identified the US, China, and India as priority growth markets. By 2023, they were already making the numbers move: Wimbledon’s Indian viewership on TV rose 96% year-on-year from 2022, while Indian entries into the public ballot grew 74%. In 2024, they introduced Hindi commentary on Star Sports, which alone drew in over 18 million unique viewers.
Their latest numbers are their strongest yet.
AELTC told The Ken that average TV audiences in India grew 40% to nearly 10 million in 2025 from about 6 million in 2024, while unique audience reach across all touchpoints—broadcast, digital, and social—combined, grew 14% to over 82 million. Social media audience grew by 200,000 people, the biggest increase of any country in the world. In-person attendance from Indian fans at the tournament also rose to 62%.
India, AELTC told The Ken, is now its largest market anywhere by audience reach.
Taken in this context, the Kuremal collaboration does not seem like a rogue marketing move after all. It is part of a much larger effort to make Wimbledon culturally relevant in a market thousands of miles from Centre Court.
But it wasn’t just a goodwill exercise to keep Indian fans happy and monetise on some numbers. There was a harder underlying financial logic.
On paper, the AELTC looks like an absolute commercial juggernaut. Its turnover has roughly doubled in under a decade—climbing to about $568 million in 2025 from $271 million in 2016. But look closer at the financials, and a distinct operational pressure emerges. AELTC’s profit margins have shrunk to 12.4% in 2025 from 20.7% in 2016.
The bottom line is that Wimbledon’s cost base is climbing, even as some of its most lucrative domestic revenue levers remain deliberately constrained.
The tournament deliberately keeps baseline ticket prices affordable and limits general price increases. Under its “clean court” philosophy, it also famously limits sponsorships to a curated set of prestigious brands it calls its “official partners,” and strips the grounds of advertising logos.
Because expanding local commercialisation is structurally off the table, growth of the international audience is one of the few remaining ways to expand the revenue pie.
Interestingly, over half of AELTC’s total income comes from a single channel: media rights. And most of it is also from outside the UK. The BBC’s deal for domestic broadcasting is worth about $80 million (£60 million), and is a fraction of what AELTC earns from ESPN in the US and the rest of its international rights.
To make these international media rights more lucrative, Wimbledon needs deeply engaged, massive overseas markets. Markets exactly like India.
Jiostar holds the tournament’s TV and streaming rights in the country through Star Sports and Jiohotstar. While the exact financial terms of their broadcast deal remain closely guarded, the partnership operates on various distinct tracks.
On social media, Wimbledon has been collaborating with Star Sports to package the tournament for Indian audiences by pairing tournament clips with Bollywood music and featuring interviews and interactions with Indian cricket players.
But in the case of Kuremal’s kulfi collaboration, Jiostar did not work with them directly; instead, they featured and reshared the collaboration on their various social media handles.
All this isn’t just a one-year push for the AELTC. Future expansion plans include tailored, India-specific versions of its main campaign trailers and posters in various regional languages, including Telugu and Malayalam. They also plan on inviting a larger roster of Indian creators, digital influencers, and Bollywood stars to the grounds—specifically tasked with capturing lifestyle and food narratives, beyond just tennis updates.
Which brings us back to Old Delhi. An Rs 80 kulfi sold across four outlets in Delhi is a rounding error compared to a tournament that makes well over $550 million annually.
But Sharma, for one, isn’t treating it as a one-off. “We might make strawberries and cream a signature flavour that comes out every year during Wimbledon time,” he said. “It creates a legacy. People will look forward to the kulfi during Wimbledon.”
A 100-year-old kulfi shop, in other words, has landed on the exact same instinct that’s been driving Wimbledon’s India strategy since 2022—show up every year, be patient, and let the legacy compound.
