Indian IT stocks are having a rough time in the age of AI. February was their worst https://www.reuters.com/world/india/indian-shares-set-open-flat-weak-cues-keep-markets-consolidation-2026-02-27/ month since the global subprime financial crisis of 2008. The latest shock came after Anthropic released two tools (Claude Code and Claude Cowork) that can perform complex programming and analysis tasks. Investors took the hint: if AI can do white-collar work faster and cheaper, the economics of IT services might change dramatically.

For me, the market panic was surprising. The idea that “AI will replace humans” has been floating around for years. What Claude did differently was position AI not just as a productivity tool, but as a competitor, particularly for knowledge workers like programmers and analysts. AI stopped being a crutch and started looking like a colleague.

And yet, there is a deeper problem lurking beneath the hype. Even the best AI models may not reason the way people assume they do.

A few weeks ago, I spoke with Satyam Dwivedi, an AI/ML researcher and founder of deep-tech startup Vaikhari AI. Over five months, his team tested several prominent large language models to measure their reasoning capabilities.

The results, quite unsettling, are published on a public leaderboard that ranks leading AI systems on a simple skill: recognising when two concepts are related. And when they are not.
