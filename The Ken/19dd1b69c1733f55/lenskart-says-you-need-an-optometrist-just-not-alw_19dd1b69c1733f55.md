New Delhi: A visit to a Lenskart in Delhi no longer begins with browsing frames. Before a customer even reaches the shelves, they’re often asked to pause for a quick face scan.
It’s a small moment, but a telling one. Face scans feel intuitive online, where virtual try-ons bridge the gap between screen and reality.
But in a physical store, surrounded by mirrors and actual products, the need for one raises a different question: when the real-world experience already exists, what exactly is the scan solving for?
Interestingly, the answer is everything.
Lenskart is positioning itself as an AI-led eye care and wearables company. Its most recent bet makes that ambition explicit: AI-powered smart glasses, slated for launch this year, designed to compete in a category being shaped by companies like Meta and Google.
But unlike these tech-first players, Lenskart is leaning on its 3,000+ stores and millions of annual customers as a distribution and data advantage. You can already see this play out inside its stores. Take eye testing.
Since its October listing last year, Lenskart has flagged a shortage of trained optometrists across its markets as a key business risk. At the time, the company employed 136 optometrists at two central hubs in Kolkata and Gurugram, who conducted remote eye tests for 168 stores in India and select international markets. The company has not updated that figure since.
Its workaround has been to scale AI-enabled remote testing, now rolled out across more than 500 of its stores.
Traditionally reliant on in-person specialists, eye tests are now shifting to what Lenskart calls “remote and self-optometry”. It is pushing for AI-led systems where the white-coated specialists consult over video and proprietary AI-enabled diagnostic equipment. However, the eyewear mentioned to The Ken that “each store has one or more qualified eye-care professionals”.
In practice, AI handles the routine while optometrists supervise and step in selectively. As you can see, this increases the number of eye tests that one optometrist can monitor in a single day.
“We are now deploying this technology across our international markets, where optometrist scarcity is equally acute,” the company said. Q3FY26 saw 6.3 million eye tests globally, up 11% quarter-on-quarter and 54% year-on-year.
In the first half of FY26 alone, the eyewear giant conducted 9.3 million eye tests in India, already surpassing its FY24 full-year total, with nearly half (49%) from first-time users.

Much of this growth has been driven by remote testing infrastructure, which has reduced wait times from 19.5 minutes to 15.8 minutes and expanded access in lower-volume stores, according to the person cited earlier.
In January 2026, they began offering eye tests for children aged eight and above (previously their threshold was at least 12 years) across its stores to further increase their customer base.
Optometry bodies argue that eye care extends well beyond prescribing lenses.
The human eye (one of the few places where blood vessels are directly visible), can offer early clues to conditions such as diabetes, thyroid disorders, and glaucoma, explained Dr. Rajeev Prasad, secretary of the Indian Optometry Association (IOA) and president of the Asia Pacific Council of Optometry.
And this is something that AI-led eye tasting cannot do yet.
But Lenskart isn’t the only one to blame. There’s a deeper labour problem at play.
India produces an estimated 6,000–7,000 optometry graduates each year, according to the IOA, yet many remain underemployed even as companies cite shortages.
The gap is largely driven by low pay. At Lenskart stores in Delhi, four optometrists with diploma degrees and a couple of years of experience told The Ken they earn between Rs 20,000 and Rs 25,000 a month.
Policy hasn’t helped. The regulatory framework oscillates—between one-year diplomas and advanced degrees, between four- and five-year programmes—without clear demarcations either from the government or the UGC. “The lack of clarity is discouraging students from entering the field, worsening the shortage over time,” said Prasad.
For Lenskart, the fallback is data. Previously, it was through virtual try-ons, now its face scans at the entrance. The company now captures over 1,00,000 face scans daily, as disclosed during its Q3 earnings call, feeding its recommendation engine with information on face shape, preferences, and purchase behaviour.
However, the company says customers can opt out. “The face scanning feature in stores is completely optional, and customers can choose to skip it,” it said in a statement to The Ken, adding that users can also request deletion of any associated data.
Earlier confined to its app, taking this capability offline closes the loop, turning every store visit into a data input for its algorithms.
In contrast, Lenskart’s closest competitor, Titan Eye+, sticks to traditional in-store eye tests conducted by optometrists, built around a “20-step zero-error” process. Titan Eye+ operates around 900 stores in India; Lenskart runs more than 2,500.
As it scales this model beyond India, face scans and the AI behind them are quietly redefining who, or what, shapes eye care going forward.
