State Bank of India Funds Management (SBIFM) is India’s largest asset management company (AMC), owned by the country’s largest public bank and Europe’s largest asset manager. As if to stay on theme, the promoters also paid themselves an unusually large interim dividend just months before the initial public offering (IPO).

SBIFM is looking to list by September and hand its two promoters—State Bank of India and French asset manager Amundi—about Rs 13,000 crore in what is entirely an offer-for-sale. 

But this won’t be the promoters’ first major liquidity event. The company that manages the country’s largest pool of mutual fund assets issued an interim dividend four months before filing its papers, and the numbers made my DRHP-drained eyes squint a little.

One, because the Rs 3,500 crore dividend exceeded the company’s profit in the same period by about 46%. Two, because it was three times what the company paid last year, which wouldn’t be as remarkable had its profits grown similarly. But the spike in dividends was far higher than the growth in profits over the same period (annualised).

Just another episode of promoters squeezing their lemons before handing them to public investors, you’d think. But as far as numbers go, this particular lemon (figuratively speaking) was squeezed past the pulp. 

The last time we saw such a big spike in pre-IPO dividends was when Hyundai India paid its Korean parent a Rs 10,700 crore in 2024, nearly twice its profits that year. Go back further, and you find Indigo, the highest flyer in this contest, whose promoters paid themselves nearly Rs 2,100 crore in interim dividends within two years before listing, Rs 1000 crore of which came in a single payout before the IPO.

This left the company with a negative net worth at the time of its IPO. A profitable airline, but worth minus Rs 139 crore after the dividend squeeze. Analysts called this practice “bottom scraping,” saying it didn’t bode well “by any standards, accounting or financial, or moral”.

All of this, by the way, is legal under Indian law. It’s the promoter’s house, so to speak. She can choose to sell it with the furniture or strip every last curtain before handing over the keys. 

What does demand some examination, though, is whether unusually large dividends have implications for the business itself, and by extension, for the retail investor who walks in right after.

Cash is not king

For you, dear investor, a large pre-IPO dividend isn’t necessarily bad news.

When a company issues large dividends to its promoters, it is essentially drawing from its accumulated cash pile. And that cash pile means very different things for different businesses.

An airline like Indigo could use extra cash to reinvest in its business. Maybe lease a few more planes or service some existing debt. An auto company like Hyundai needs cash for capex, factories, R&D, and the like. These are businesses that eat cash to grow or stay competitive.

An AMC like SBIFM, on the other hand, already has a massive distribution infrastructure in the form of SBI’s resources, including its growing network of over 23,000 branches and the SBI brand. It doesn’t have to buy distribution, and the operational costs for the AMC business are remarkably low. HDFC AMC operates at an 83% operating margin and ICICI Prudential AMC at 76%, thanks to lean costs, the biggest of which are employee expenses. 

So for an AMC-type business, there’s not much the cash can do other than sit in a bank and generate some treasury income. Which means that a large dividend payout may not starve the business in any meaningful way since the business doesn’t eat much in the first place. 

Having said all that, a dividend squeeze by itself, even for a capex-heavy business, is just one of many variables. Even after a large dividend squeeze, Indigo listed at a 13% premium on day one and rallied up to 20% in the following year. Hyundai India’s story was less cheerful. It was listed at a discount and is trading below its IPO price even today.

Yield to none

And as it happens, dividend-yielding stocks matter more than usual when markets are shaky—with wars, commodity shocks, and other uncertainties looming over them, like is the case now.

This is an unsurprising and well-documented tendency: the Nifty Dividend Opportunities 50 index (a basket of the highest-yielding stocks on the NSE) has historically fallen less than the Nifty-50 during major market falls, while still participating in the recoveries that followed. When markets rattle, a portfolio does well to include stocks with predictable dividends over unpredictable capital gains. The trouble is in picking the right ones. 

The textbook approach to adding dividend-yielding stocks to your portfolio is to pick an index, sort its stocks by dividend yield, and buy the ones at the top of that list.

But this method can be misleading, for many reasons. 

First, dividend yield calculated over the last 12 months, for example, may include a one-time balloon dividend that artificially inflates it. A recently listed stock with the afterglow of a Rs 3,500 crore pre-IPO payout looks most generous even when it’s really not. What gets paid to promoters pre-IPO need not get paid to public investors post-IPO.

The dividend yield could also be high simply because the stock price has dropped. Since yield is effectively dividend by price (Dividend/Market price), a falling stock mechanically inflates the yield even without the fundamentals getting any better.

Or, the company might be distributing a large part of its earnings, say 75% or more, which could leave little room to absorb a bad quarter. If profits dip, the dividends could dip with them.

Investors who bought into Indigo or Hyundai expecting large dividend payouts to continue would have since realised they were chasing a mirage. Post Indigo’s listing, dividends (and the dividend yield) steadily decreased from Rs 34 per share to Rs 5 per share, before vanishing completely for the next five years. Much like Indigo and Hyundai, SBIFM will almost certainly not distribute dividends to its public shareholders with the same generosity it showed its promoters

A one-off dividend spike may well be just that, and tells you little about whether the stock will pay reliably over the next few years. For reliable returns, it is generally better to look for businesses that are more suitably positioned to pay dividends consistently, year after year. Public Sector Undertakings (PSUs) like Coal India and Power Grid Corporation fit this criteria well. Because the government is their largest shareholder and relies on predictable cash flow to fund national budgets, these companies are encouraged to maintain steady payouts. 

Coal India, for instance, has paid dividends every single year since listing, with yields consistently hovering between 4–8%. Power Grid has done much the same, at around 3–5%.

In sum, have a checklist. Is the dividend consistent or a one-off? Is the payout ratio sustainable? And most importantly, is the yield a reflection of what the company will do going forward—or what it did once, to a different set of shareholders, and under entirely different circumstances?
