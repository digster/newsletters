For a long time, cybersecurity experts warned of a coming calamity. But the sound of sirens was drowned out by the roaring applause for LLMs and their miracles. 

And then on 7 April, the calamity revealed itself like a prophecy fulfilled. 

Claude Mythos, Anthropic’s newest large language model, inadvertently turned out to be so good at finding and exploiting vulnerabilities in software that it instantly sent all of Big Tech and world governments scrambling. In India, Finance Minister Nirmala Sitharaman had to convene a high-level meeting with top bankers where she likened the situation to “a threat of war”.

And not without reason. 

Consider, for example, that Mythos could autonomously discover a 27-year-old vulnerability in OpenBSD, an operating system that powers Apple’s macOS, Android, Windows, and firewalls used by billions. Despite the OS’s reputation as “one of the most security-hardened platforms on earth”, Mythos found a critical bug every previous audit since 1999 had missed. All for just under $50. 

India’s regulators had no time to waste. Within a fortnight, the RBI had held urgent consultations with the US Federal Reserve and the Bank of England to assess the fallout, and on 27 April, CERT-In issued a high-severity advisory to Indian MSMEs.

The urgency also rippled through India’s BFSI ecosystem. The State Bank of India floated a proposal for a new Cyber Defence Centre, while institutions all across, from the NPCI to large fintechs like Paytm, are lobbying Anthropic for direct access to the model.

India also enters the post-Mythos world uniquely vulnerable. It’s already the number one target for phishing attacks in all of Asia, and the rate at which it faces cyberattacks is 60% higher than the global average. 

The country may have digitised at breakneck speed, but the cost of that was certain structural compromises…
