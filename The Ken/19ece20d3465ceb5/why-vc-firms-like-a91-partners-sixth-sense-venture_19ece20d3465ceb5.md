Last week, Pushp Brand, a spice-maker from Indore, filed draft papers with the market regulator Sebi for a Rs 800–1000 crore IPO.
The brand is little known nationally, but it is a well-established regional player from Madhya Pradesh, with products like “hing” and “achaar masala” as its heroes.
Started in the 1990s by second-generation entrepreneurs Mahendra Kumar Surana and Surendra Kumar Surana, the founder-brother duo pivoted from their father’s spice-trading business to building a brand under the name Pushp.
Till today, about 96% of its sales comes from spices, while spices like chilli and turmeric, and 95% of those sales happen at kiranas. This is as traditional as an FMCG business can get.
Mumbai-based venture capital firms, A91 Partners and Sixth Sense are very well aware of it. In 2020, A91 bought a 28% stake in Pushp. Four years later, in 2023, Sixth Sense bought an 8% stake as secondary from A91.
The IPO,entirely an offer for sale, will see both investors and promoters selling some stake. For A91 Partners, they will take home about Rs 450 crore , which translates into returns of about 6.5–8.5X. Sixth Sense is expected to cash out Rs 165 crore, or about 2–3X returns, per back-of-the-envelope calculations done by The Ken.
After the IPO, A91’s share will drop to 5.13% from 20.14%, and Sixth Sense’s to 2.31% from 7.81%.
The listing is expected to get Sebi approval in the next three to four week, with the listing planned for around October, a person familiar with the matter told The Ken.
What made a kirana-first, big on single-category spice brand from Madhya Pradesh investable to begin with?
Since Pushp’s legacy business was in spices trading, the founders’ strength lies in knowing what to procure and when, explained an investor in the consumer space. They don’t compete on pricing. This is why they have been able to grow at a 20–25% annually, consistently, with Ebitda margins of 15–20%.
There has been a broader trend where investors are tracking down old-school businesses that tick two checkmarks. Scale, even if limited to a city or state, and profitability, at least 15% Ebitda margins, according to Satish Meena, founder of Datum Intelligence.
Around the pandemic, several such businesses caught the eye of VCs, from spices to sweets, categories that don’t typically attract venture capital. Brands like Pushp are a case in point.
Even for Sixth Sense Ventures, exits or partial exit have come from businesses started around 1990 to the early 2000s, such as watch retailer Ethos, founded in 2003, and dairy company Parag Milk, founded in 1992.
Another example is Anandhaas, a traditional snack and sweet shop founded in 1998 in Coimbatore. It now has 30-plus stores in Tamil Nadu and Kerala and is part of A91’s consumer portfolio, which invested in 2025.
The proposition makes sense for both sides. Investors offer capital in exchange for stable returns. Founders, who don’t want to take expansion risk with their own money, get the push they need.
Now, there are several such old school businesses in India – from spices to sweets – something that does not catch the eye of VCs. But around the pandemic some of these brands did. Brands like Pushp are a case in point.

The experiments investors suggest, expanding distribution beyond a state or adding more stores are the kind of bets traditional founders wouldn’t make alone.
MTR, or Mavalli Mavalli Tiffin Rooms from Bengaluru is a useful example here. Orkla, a Norwegian conglomerate, acquired MTR Foods in 2007 and invested in spices brand Eastern in 2020. MTR got listed on Indian bourses in November 2025 at a 3% premium.
Pushp is A91’s first exit in consumer space. First a stake sale to Sixth Sense in 2023, now the upcoming IPO. HealthKart had a secondary round at a $500M valuation in late 2024 but has not filed for an IPO. Rare Rabbit raised its first institutional round only in 2024.
Their oldest consumer investments in the consumer portfolio is filled with new-age brands like Atomberg, Sugar Cosmetics, and Paper Boat and remains largely unmonetised. [Atomberg](https://www.moneycontrol.com/news/business/ipo/smart-fan-maker-atomberg-picks-bankers-for-rs-2-000-crore-ipo-13724969.html) is planning an IPO and Paper Boat is considering a secondary deal, a person in the know said.
The period after the pandemic has been a reset. The valuations D2C companies once demanded have collapsed, customer acquisition has become expensive, and while quick commerce appears to be an attractive distribution channel, it has compressed margins. This is why strategic investors such as conglomerates as well as financial investors—VCs and PE firms—have set thresholds for profitability.
“These are inherently good and strong businesses. You may not expect 10–15X sort of returns from them, but you can easily expect 2–3X multiples,” says Meena. A91 which stayed for six years doubled that average for itself.
Pushp’s revenue in FY26 is over Rs 480 crore, with reported Ebitda of around Rs 85 crore.
Putting those numbers in context of its competitors might not be noteworthy but if we compare returns, it has outperformed.

The attraction of traditional businesses is their strong backend, be it in procurement, manufacturing, or supply chain. Digital-first businesses, by contrast, rely on building the front end through brand-building and distribution. There is only so much you can grow with quick commerce, and it’s also very expensive, Meena explained. “This is why investing in traditional businesses acts as a hedging strategy.”
But such businesses come with a catch. It’s not easy for any of these players to become like a Bikanerwala or a Haldiram.
Or for a regional spice-maker to become an Everest or MDH, which collectively have about 40% market share. Over 50% of the spice market is unorganised, and within the organised segment, there are over 2,000 players who struggle to scale beyond their region. As [The Ken wrote](https://the-ken.com/story/fmcg-giants-are-caught-in-a-spice-war-mtrs-owner-beat-them-to-it-years-ago/) last year:
The spice market is like the mafia: each boss controls their own territory. The brands Aachi and Shakti rule Tamil Nadu; Brahmins, Nirapara, Kitchen treasures and Eastern dominate Kerala; and Sunrise is big in eastern India. And Karnataka? “You can’t run a supermarket in Karnataka without MTR,” said the senior executive quoted above.
Undoubtedly, this presents a scaling challenge for brands ambitious enough to break out of their own territories. For others, it’s a protective moat repelling foreign invaders. But the broader verdict is unanimous: national FMCG companies looking to achieve pan-India dominance on the backs of standardised products must bow down to regional tastes in spices.

Now, as for Pushp and its future IPO, capital market investors look at scale and expansion when looking at consumer companies. While Pushp has been expanding into categories like tea and soya chunks, Indian spices still make up its largest revenue mix.
Compare that to Orkla, which derives 60% of its revenue from spices. Yet its stock is down 18% from all-time high and about 12% from its listing in November last year.
The lesson for investors appears to be that old-school businesses reward patience, not ambition. A91 knew that. Whether public market investors will agree is the next test.
As for the broader shift, investors are now applying the same lens to new-age companies too. Aglitas, the sportswear brand, set up manufacturing first and then brought investors on board, Meena said. The line between old school and new age is blurring but what remains constant is the threshold: be profitable before you ask for capital.
The old-school bet was, in many ways, a correction. The question now is whether public market investors, who have their own appetite for scale and expansion, will reward the same qualities that private markets did. Pushp has the margins. Whether it has the selling point is another matter.
