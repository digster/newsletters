Back in Bengaluru, I am embarrassed to even mention the daily evening rains, given what Nepal is dealing with. But I am happy because I finally fixed Bribo’s outdoor shelter with a proper rainproof cover. He’s now often snoozing in it when I go looking for him at dinnertime. When I call him, he stretches out inside and needs to be coaxed out with a mix of belly rubs and pets.
Gabru is still iffy about morning walks post the rains. He needs to be coaxed too.
One of these slightly chilly mornings, as we were walking our normal daily route, I could see smoke rising from the makeshift huts housing construction workers on the side. It was firewood. They must have been cooking. I could smell it too.
Immediately, I was transported decades back in time, to Kerala in the 1980s. The smoke was from my grandmother’s hearth in the mornings as I woke up. Breakfast was being made. The roosters in the coops, ours and our neighbours’, had already finished their crowing.
And that was that. With no TV, no music, no books, no phones, and no telephone, time lost meaning. I didn’t own a watch too.
During those two summer months there was nothing to do and nowhere to go. You took each day at a time, as it came.
The smell of woodfire triggering nostalgia is, I know, a mark of my privilege. But I give myself permission for the occasional indulgence of memory sans guilt.

The Japanese have a wonderful vocabulary to express the aesthetics and emotional beauty of things that are worn and imperfect.
Let’s start with Keinen Henka, the phrase that comes closest to describing patina.
The Japanese term keinen henka(経年変化) also describes the natural changes materials undergo through years of exposure and use. Many people associate these changes with deterioration, imagining that materials simply fade or weaken. Yet when we look at traditional houses or centuries-old temples, we see something different. Wooden pillars and floors passed down through generations soften at the edges, gain a gentle sheen from touch, and deepen in color. Small dents, scratches, and worn areas hold memories and reflect the presence of the people who lived there.
There’s wabi-sabi, a compound term about the beauty in the understanding that everything is imperfect, impermanent, and incomplete. I learnt only today that it was imported back into Japan from other countries.
Wabi is a noun derived from the classical Japanese verb “wabu,” related to the modern verb “wabiru” and adjective “wabishii.” Wabu means to languish or be miserable.

Sabi is a noun: in this case, derived from the classical verb “sabu.” Today, the verb “sabiru” means to rust, with its connotations of age and decay. The modern adjective “sabishii” means lonely.
Perhaps you’ve heard of Kintsugi? Or even seen it?
When a bowl, teapot or precious vase falls and breaks into a thousand pieces, we throw them away angrily and regretfully. Yet there is an alternative, a Japanese practice that highlights and enhances the breaks thus adding value to the broken object. It’s called kintsugi (金継ぎ), or kintsukuroi (金繕い), literally golden (“kin”) and repair (“tsugi”).

It doesn’t look like much. Imagine a computer mouse, but made entirely of a single piece of rubber. You can even pick it up like one, since it fits inside your palm.
On the underside are roughly 30 conical nubs, arranged like spikes. It’s the Kong Zoomgroom, the ultimate dog deshedder and massager. I use it to brush Gabru before using a second grooming brush. When you rub the Zoomgroom on your pet’s fur, it draws away loose fur, dust and dead undercoat hair. As a bonus, or perhaps the main deal for the dog, it gives them a nice and gentle massage.
Gabru loves it. And for the last few months, so does Bribo!
The rubber spikes on the Zoomgroom get worn down with use, so when I bought a new one for Gabru earlier this year, I didn’t throw away the older one. One day, on a lark, I tried it on Bribo. He loved it!
So, now, our daily dinner routine is food, followed by water, followed by a Zoomgroom brush-down and massage!

Think of a turtle. What comes to your mind?
Is that image influenced by mythology or popular culture?
I immediately think of Master Oogway, the embodiment of serene, ancient wisdom from Kung Fu Panda. That image isn’t random. It’s the echo of a global archetype, imprinted on our collective consciousness long before Dreamworks got involved.
Vietnam has the Golden Turtle (Kim Quy), the divine guide. India has Kurma, the cosmic tortoise that carried mountains on its back to help gods and demons churn the ocean for the nectar of immortality (amrut). China has Xuanwu (black-turtle-snake), the stalwart guardian of the north. Even across the Pacific, Native American tradition names the continent itself Turtle Island, because they believe that it carries the world on its back.
This ancient gravitas carries into our modern libraries. Douglas Wood’s Old Turtle casts it as a god. Terry Pratchett’s Small Gods traps divinity inside a tortoise’s body (Yes, I know turtle and tortoise are not the same, but hey!)
Imagination is wonderfully elastic. It also gives the gentle charm of Franklin and the gritty vengeance of The Last Ronin. The shell that stood for divine guidance can also be reimagined as a child or a warrior.
No myth is set in stone. The turtle’s symbolism has shifted through popular imagination just because one person or more thought about it differently.
Every story we consume carves a new possibility out of the old.

I know, it’s been some time since I wrote here—and I’ll make it up to you with some brilliant movies for your weekend: two biopics that might have flown under your radar but definitely deserve your attention:
1. Eddie the Eagle (2015)
It’s corny, it’s predictable, but this movie has heart—and a lot of it.
Taron Egerton pulls off an incredible portrayal of Eddie “The Eagle” Edwards. After the credits rolled, I checked out videos of the real Eddie and gotta say, Taron has got him to a T.
Has a lot of well-crafted, well-shot scenes, especially the climax sequence, with Van Halen’s ‘Jump’ playing in the background, which is probably the best use of that banger we’ll see in a movie.
Streaming on Prime Video/Apple TV, depending on where you are.
2. The Straight Story (1999)
Based on the incredible story, the movie follows a retired farmer in the US, Alvin Straight, as he makes a long trip from Iowa to Wisconsin on his old lawnmower to visit his ill brother.
David Lynch does an incredible job of peeling back the layers of this scrawny, straightforward (by design) but innately loving character, and by the time the titles roll, he’d have you in joyful tears.
Leave it to David Lynch to make the “ordinary” look and feel absolutely stunning. Rest in cinema.
