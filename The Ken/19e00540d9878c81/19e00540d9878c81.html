On a Wednesday evening in April, in a conference room in Mumbai’s Bandra-Kurla Complex, Ganesh Lakshminarayanan tried to get ahead of the first question.

It was his first earnings call as CEO-designate of Tata Communications. He had been in the job for 10 days. The investor-relations team had prepped him for this moment. The team’s head, he said, was “looking at me very anxiously”. There was, apparently, a right answer to what comes next: what is the company’s north star?

Lakshminarayanan declined to give one. “I’m still finding my way around our corporate headquarters,” he told investors. Then he offered something else. Joining Tata Communications, he said, was his “personal investment thesis”.

It is not immediately obvious why. The clue is not in how the company describes itself, but in who is willing to bet on it.

Nvidia, after all, does not give its best chips to everyone. The GH200, the successor to the H100 chips that Microsoft and Amazon have been stockpiling at roughly $30,000 apiece, goes only to companies Nvidia considers serious about AI infrastructure at scale. Companies that have the network, the data-centre relationships, and the enterprise customers to actually use them.

In India, there are only two such. One is Reliance Jio, which has spent the better part of two years loudly marketing its AI ambitions. The other is Tata Communications, which has said almost nothing about it.

Most people, including many investors, do not think of Tata Communications as an AI company. The roughly $4.7 billion market-cap firm does not seem particularly eager to correct that perception either. It is neither building a chatbot nor training a foundation model. What it is building is harder to explain.
