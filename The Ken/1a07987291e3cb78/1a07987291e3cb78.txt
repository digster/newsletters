Fourteen years is a long time, and $145 million is a lot of money, enough, one would think, to build a wellness and nutrition app that could stand the true test of clinical science. But it was not to be.

In late August, Healthify, formerly Healthifyme, merged with US-based Berry Street in an all-stock deal. For India’s, rather the world’s, largest AI-driven nutrition app, the salvation came via a medical path, not performance marketing. Four-year-old Berry Street, which has raised less than half of Healthify’s total funding, provides “evidence-based metabolic care through a network of 2,000 clinicians”.

... 

On 3 September, when Ultrahuman announced its $70 million funding, the press release said, “The financing marks the next chapter in Ultrahuman’s evolution from a wearable health company into a human-computer interface company built around the body.” (Emphasis mine; I’m still trying to grasp the absurdity.)

The long press release, an alphabet soup of high-fidelity claims, also included a section titled: “Science as a product advantage.” That list of assertions has serious issues, but more on that later.

August also saw Deepinder Goyal’s Temple, another wearable startup, acquire a UK-based longevity medicine practice, Longevous. Temple is in the same position Healthify was for most of its life—explicitly designated as non-medical.

Meanwhile, Cent, another wellness company that wants to scan its way to a business, strutted its collaboration with Philips last week. “The ideal consumer is anyone above the age of 35, who wants to start thinking about their health,” the company told my colleague Sudeshna earlier this year.

This is the ‘sick-until-proven-healthy’ trap, as some biomedical experts view this new phenomenon.

Welcome to the wellness industrial complex, which, as Deborah Cohen’s new book Bad Influence shows, sells health anxiety to healthy people with little clinical accountability.
