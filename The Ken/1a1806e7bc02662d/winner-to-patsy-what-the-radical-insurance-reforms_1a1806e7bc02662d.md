Good Morning Dear Reader,
India’s health insurance sector is in for a shake-up.
The regulator Irdai has been working out plans for over a year to deal with rising inflation and has settled, broadly, on two proposals. The first targets the middleman by slashing agent commissions. Irdai has extended the deadline for collecting feedback on this proposal to 31 October, but has already received more than 650 responses expressing concerns about the potentially negative impact on revenues and agent earnings.
The second proposal seeks to pass the cost onto the patient, with the General Insurance Council suggesting a co-pay of 10%. But it’s less a concrete proposal, an analyst close to the Irdai argues, and more “a pressure tactic to get hospitals and related bodies to standardise the pricing a bit”.
Hospitals are resistant to the idea of standardised pricing. They even picked a fight over it with insurers last year. Hospitals complained that insurance premiums weren’t keeping up with medical inflation, while insurers retorted that hospitals were quoting a wide range of charges for similar treatments.
The fight wasn’t just theoretical. It led to the suspension of cashless services for a time. In the end, both blinked, but without reaching a resolution.
The dispute has since widened to encompass other key stakeholders, mainly patients and agents, and has come to rest around the fundamental question of who should absorb the mounting costs.

Sajjan I Commented In a country where roughly half the population still pays for healthcare out of pocket, hospitals priced off 33–46x EBITDA valuations will recover that premium from patients. The fix is not a blanket price cap. It is enforceable accountability. The Union Government should do five things. First, notify the Parliamentary Standing Committee’s 176th Report (August 2026) recommendations on mandatory price display, binding pre-treatment estimates, and caps on consumable and device mark-ups. Second, push states to adopt and actually enforce the Clinical Establishments Act, 2010, including public reporting of infection rates, readmissions and mortality. Third, require CCI review of hospital roll-ups at the district level, where one chain can dominate a local market. Fourth, require disclosure of goodwill, acquisition multiples and doctor revenue-share arrangements for PE-backed and listed chains. Fifth, fix PM-JAY package rates and expand public secondary and tertiary capacity, so patients are not left with only the private sector. Accountability rests with the Union Minister of Health & Family Welfare and Chemicals & Fertilisers, Shri J.P. Nadda, and MoS Smt. Anupriya Patel, who oversee hospital regulation, PM-JAY and drug and device pricing through the NPPA. State health ministers share the responsibility, since health is a State subject. They have the committee’s report. The question is when they will act on it. |
