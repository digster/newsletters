Happy Sunday and welcome back to First Principles, Ishan!

This is the first time we’re publishing a second version of a seasonal playlist, after our first Monsoon playlist of 2024. We’re growing up!

2024 was a sprawling, 67-track, genre-hopping mixtape. 2026 is a tight 29-track set. The vibe has decidedly shifted from “everything anyone associates with rain” to a more deliberately chosen mood.

India is rare in having entire ragas written for the rains (the Malhar family), and the 2026 list leans into it: Kishori Amonkar’s “Meha Jhar Jhar,” Lakshmi Shankar’s “Barsan Lagi Kari Badariya,” “Garaj Garaj” from Bandish Bandits, Lata’s “O Sajna Barkha Bahar Aayi” (1960), and “Bole Re Papihara” (Guddi, 1971, invoking the papiha, the rain-bird of monsoon poetry). The 2024 list had almost none of this layer. 

Since I’d used the same form for both the playlists, I caught a nice bit of irony. A 2024 respondent had complained that no list captures “malhaar, megh and miyan ki malhar.”

Well, the 2026 list answers that.

Where 2024 chased the word “rain,” 2026 chases the feeling through vernacular vocabulary: the Malayalam/Tamil “mazha/mazhai” (rain) recurs across “Pavizha Mazha,” “Etho Mazhayil,” “Mazhai Thuli,” and “Mazhai Varum,” alongside “Mungaru Maleye,” “Saawan,” “Barkha,” and “Badariya.” It’s definitely a more rooted monsoon lexicon.

There’s also a strong Kerala tilt, which is only fitting since the monsoon makes landfall there first. 

Several artists recur across both years: A.R. Rahman, Hariharan, Green Day (“When I Come Around” → “Wake Me Up When September Ends”), and Steven Wilson (Porcupine Tree’s “Lazarus” in 2024 → his solo “The Raven That Refused to Sing” in 2026). A consistent FP-community taste signature that persists underneath and across years?

1. Riyaaz Amlani is on the FP Podcast

This week on the First Principles podcast, I sit down with Riyaaz Amlani, the man whose company, Impresario, runs Social, Smoke House Deli, AntiSocial, Bandra Born, and Mocha. He opened the first Mocha in Bombay 25 years ago, when most Indians (me included) still thought a café meant Coffee Day. We talk cities, cafés, the marble racket he ran at six (yes, six, not sixteen—apparently people got that one wrong for years), and what it actually takes to keep building “handmade” restaurants for a quarter of a century. About an hour in, I ask him how he’s handling the shift from frontline operator to running things from the boardroom. I loved his answer.

“I am transitioning much against my will. Kicking and screaming. I am trying to grow up. I am trying to become that boardroom warrior and not a frontline warrior.”

Riyaaz Amlani

Part 1 releases on Monday, 15 June. Part 2, the next Monday. And as always, the full two-hour conversation is only for subscribers and releases on 15th as well.

2. Learning from others vs. learning by changing yourself

First Principles started out as a podcast for me, then a newsletter, and then a way for me to constantly write down my own thoughts, refactor them, and reconcile myself each week.

For the past month and a half, it has become something new. I’ve started a weekly mentoring program for a bunch of colleagues at The Ken based on the principles that I’ve spent much of my career discovering, and most of the last three years explaining. We reference a lot of First Principles. There’s a lot of Systems Thinking. 

In simple words, I’m using past editions of the First Principles newsletter to create a new coaching program for my colleagues based on them. 

I’d thought that was too meta for me. Until I started writing today’s edition, which is premised on observations from within the program! I feel like an Ouroboros! 

Anyway, about halfway through one of our recent sessions, a bunch of new questions started coming. Tell us about the most interesting people you’ve met. The patterns you’ve seen that we can’t find online. How do you get people to open up? How do you build conviction behind a decision? Is there a deck for that, can you just send it to us?

Now, none of those questions were lazy. They were the questions of sharp, ambitious, and curious professionals trying to borrow someone else’s sample size. 

One of my colleagues even said it perfectly. We haven’t seen enough people yet, so lend us yours.

When you’re starting out, you don’t have a back catalogue of your own experiences to draw on, so you go looking for other people’s. You want the playbooks and methods. 

But I was still surprised. Because I realised that I no longer thought the same way about learning. I tend to look inward at my own beliefs and actions to see which ones I should change. 

When I was younger though, I too looked outward to learn. That is natural, because we humans are wired to learn by watching others. As our younger selves develop, we look for role models who can model our abstract aspirations into more concrete and attainable “possible selves”. 

Even in terms of methods, beginners learn more from studying worked examples than from solving equivalent problems themselves. Our goal is to expand our repertoire “horizontally” by adding more knowledge, more skills, and more methods. (Though even that advantage fades as expertise grows, leading to the “expertise reversal effect”). 

At some point in our lives and careers though, our frame of reference shifts from “others” to “self”. We shift from organising around external authority and approval, towards self-authorship.

And as we do, reflection becomes more important. 

One of the most famous and yet simplest proofs of this was a field experiment run by researchers at a Bengaluru call centre run by Wipro. Trainees who spent the last 15 minutes of each day writing and reflecting on what they’d learned outperformed a control group by 22.8%, even though the latter group spent those 15 minutes continuing to work. 

“The practitioner allows himself to experience surprise, puzzlement, or confusion in a situation which he finds uncertain or unique. He reflects on the phenomenon before him, and on the prior understandings which have been implicit in his behaviour. He carries out an experiment which serves to generate both a new understanding of the phenomenon and a change in the situation.”

Donald Schön, The Reflective Practitioner (1983), p. 68

And as we become better at reflection, we realise that the real “unlocks” around growth won’t come from more horizontal development (“what we know”), but instead vertical development (“how we think”).

“Traditionally, leadership programs have focused mainly on horizontal development. What is it that leaders need to learn, and how do we give them that? 

[…] a vertically focused program places less emphasis on the content to be poured into the leader and more on the cup itself (the mindset, identity, and mental models of the leader). The aim of Vertical Development is not to add more to the cup but to grow the size of the cup itself.”

Nick Petrie, Vertical Leadership Development—Part 1 (CCL, 2014)

This brings me back to where I started. I had instinctively created a vertical coaching program for my colleagues while forgetting that many of them are still in the horizontal learning phases of their careers.

Now, this isn’t a bad thing. We are perfectly capable of doing both. In fact, I wish someone had done this for me early on in my career. 

I’m excited about where this program will take me and my colleagues!

5. Movie pick of the week:

Hey there! It’s Vishnu with a movie for your weekend.

This time, I have probably the stupidest smart movie I’ve watched (stay with me on this):

Burn After Reading (2008) – Black Comedy/Espionage

What if you wrote a movie about seemingly ‘smart’ people who are painfully basic in reality, and astronomical dimwits who somehow end up making ‘intelligent’ decisions? Unintentionally, of course.

Now, imagine this thing written and directed by the Coen brothers, with an absolutely PACKED cast of George Clooney, Frances McDormand, Brad Pitt, John Malkovich, Tilda Swinton, and Richard Jenkins!

You then get the absolutely random, chaotic, dry-wit-filled, relentlessly random Burn After Reading. Ideally, go in dark. Don’t watch the trailer, or read about the movie. Trust me.

Streaming on Prime Video.

6. Girls vs. boys

Hello Ishan! This is Tanim, and to close out this edition, I have something for you to mull over. 

But before that, what are you reading today, apart from First Principles? We feature reader picks once a month, so if you are in the middle of something good, tell us about it here. We would love to share it with the community. 

Today’s section is inspired by First Principles reader Kirti, who sent in a sweet email after last week’s section, and it’s been on my mind since. 

“I read this whole section with great interest, especially as my teen has been reading what my friends call “intense books”. 

She picked the habit up, and I have never denied her books (always bought her one if she asked). Then we said no more buying, can’t afford this habit, and ended up getting her a library card. Then she had a phase where she didn’t want to read (that was soooo hard on me). Thankfully, she picked the habit up again! 

I have never controlled what she has read. I gave her a caveat when she was younger: if you start to feel uncomfortable with the content, or you come and tell me, ‘I am not sure if I should be reading this, then stop. Else read what you want. This means she reads Korean translated books, something on Covid, and she also got into something like ‘A Good Girls’ Guide to Murder.’ Last year she worked on ‘The 57 Bus’ (which I read and found intense as hell, as it dealt with identity, class, and so much more) for her English exhibition, which got her a lot of kudos. 

It has been an interesting time as a parent to see how her reading has evolved, and she sometimes recommends the books she reads to me too.”

This note got me thinking about “intense books” and who reads them. 

There’s no hard data showing that women and girls read more of that kind of fiction than men and boys. But there is a great deal of conversation around girls reading more than boys in general, and around women being the ones keeping the novel alive. 

The New York Times published a graph on this two years ago. 

Sources: National Assessment of Educational Progress (NAEP); Sean Reardon, Stanford University

That worry is worth sitting with. Reading is one of the few ways we develop empathy. If we lose that, the world seems bleaker than usual.

Let me close the edition with that thought.
