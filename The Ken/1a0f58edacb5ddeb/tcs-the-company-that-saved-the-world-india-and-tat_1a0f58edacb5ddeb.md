As it turns out, that accident in 1974 went on to save three different things.
It saved the world. When every computer on Earth threatened to fall over on 1 January 2000, the rich world did not have the engineers to fix it. India did. TCS built a software factory that churned through 2 million lines of code a day and helped humanity cross into the new millennium without the meltdown everyone feared.
It saved India. With no oil, no ore, and no factories, TCS built an export industry out of pure intelligence. It is now close to a tenth of India’s GDP. It built the plumbing the rest of us take for granted, from the NSE to the depository that holds your shares to the income tax network. And it took first-generation graduates from small towns, taught them to knot a tie and talk to a client in Ohio, and handed them a middle-class life.
It saved Tata Sons. Over the last 25 years, TCS has handed the group close to Rs 2.7 lakh crore, roughly 80% of Tata Sons’ dividend and buyback income. That money paid for the group’s ambitions, from Air India to a semiconductor fab. When the House of Tata argues about its future, it is really arguing about how TCS’s money gets spent.
That brings us to now. TCS was born because Indians once feared automation and left a computer in its crate. Sixty years on, it faces an automation of its own, and AI is commoditising the very work it industrialised. The servant that saved everyone may now have to save itself.
Tune in to find out how a company built on an accident ended up carrying the world, a country, and India’s greatest business group, and what it is worth now that the one thing it sold, human intelligence, has become cheap.
