India has spent the better part of the last decade persuading the world to buy more of what it grows. Trade deals have been signed, markets have been opened, and agricultural exports have become one of New Delhi’s favourites.
This year alone, it [signed](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressNoteDetails.aspx?NoteId=158370&ModuleId=3®=3&lang=1) a free trade agreement with New Zealand, is busy [putting](https://www.business.gov.uk/campaign/alive-with-opportunity/the-uk-india-trade-deal/) the finishing touches on the UK deal, and [is](https://commission.europa.eu/topics/trade/eu-india-trade-agreement_en) courting just about every major market willing to lower its tariffs.
That is just half the battle won, for the other set of trouble begins after the container lands.
Australia has [held up](https://www.hindustantimes.com/cities/chandigarh-news/biosecurity-audits-leave-200-cr-indian-basmati-stranded-at-australian-ports-101783884580770.html) nearly Rs 200 crore worth of basmati rice. Japan and Nepal have [rejected](https://www.wionews.com/trending/why-nepal-joins-japan-in-banning-indian-mango-imports-how-it-could-impact-exporters-1781077387724) mango consignments. Europe has become increasingly [picky](https://www.deccanherald.com/india/eu-flags-365-indian-products-with-pesticides-heavy-metals-4029332) about Indian spices, herbs, and nuts. Even India’s biggest seafood export, shrimp, is being turned away over traces of banned antibiotics. In 2026, banned antibiotics accounted for [43%](https://timesofindia.indiatimes.com/india/banned-antibiotics-behind-43-of-shrimp-export-rejections-cdsco-seeks-stricter-enforcement/articleshow/131544737.cms) of Indian shrimp export rejections by major markets.
At first glance, these look like a run of bad luck. But they’re symptoms of the same problem: India has become rather good at negotiating its way into global markets, but not always at getting past the inspection counter.
