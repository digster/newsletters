There is a printed notice stuck on the wall at IDC Kitchen in Indiranagar. It says:

View in Browser

All my colleagues and I eat and drink at this place every working day. There’s something about the notice, apologetic but not dramatic, that makes it feel more real than any headline I have read this week. The menu board prices have been covered, and the filter coffee, always Rs 25, was Rs 30 yesterday morning. This is what our guest described as a second-order effect: higher LPG costs for commercial establishments get passed directly to the consumer. The war shows up on the news. But now, it shows up on your bill too.

The war between the US, Israel, and Iran began on 28 February. By 2 March, the Strait of Hormuz, through which roughly 20% of the world’s crude oil, natural gas, and LPG flows, had effectively closed. By last week, 30–35% of hotels and restaurants had shut down due to a near-complete halt in commercial LPG supply. My flatmate spent an evening looking up induction cooktops online as a backup and the other one said, “I have a contact for black market cylinders.” We have not acted on it. Yet.

This is the episode we recorded eight days ago, on 12 March. When Mohit Satyanand sat down with Praveen Gopal Krishnan and Rahel Philipose, he said, only half joking, that if the war resolved itself in the next few weeks, we would not need to run the episode at all. It is now 20 March. The war is on its 21st day, oil is above $100 a barrel, and Iran has said it will continue to use the Strait of Hormuz as a pressure point. We are running the episode.

The man you call when everything goes sideways

Mohit Satyanand was on Two by Two last year to make sense of the Trump tariffs. Rahel put it plainly in the episode: at some point, you have to accept that the next couple of decades are just going to look like this. One crisis after another, each one requiring someone to explain the second- and third-order effects to the rest of us.

Mohit is that someone. He is a successful entrepreneur turned investor, chairman, and founder of Teamwork Arts, which produces the Jaipur Literature Festival. He is not an alarmist, which somehow makes everything he says feel more alarming.

His first and most important point is not about the war itself. “When you have an economic crisis,” he says, “when you have raw material crises, there are two ways of dealing with it. One is pricing and the other is rationing. And we know that our country has a long and glorious history of rationing.” We are already there. The government has invoked the Essential Commodities Act, cut commercial LPG supply, and is prioritising household kitchens over restaurants, hotels, and Darjeeling tea estates whose first-flush crop is currently sitting unprocessed because they have no gas.

The uncomfortable long view

The most unsettling part of this episode is not the immediate crisis. It is what Mohit says lies beneath it.

India has been dependent on imported energy for fifty years. The first oil shock was in the early 1970s. 

“If we look at what is the contribution of energy, of imported energy to our GDP, it has not changed. We are still as dependent, roughly about 30 percent, as we were in the early 70s. And this is despite recognising that we are an energy-deficient economy.”

The roads, the airports, the metros, all meant to reduce how much oil we burn through more efficient transport, have not moved the needle. Bengaluru’s metro remains, in his words, “an apology of a subway system” for a city of ten million people. London, half the size, has 350 metro stops; we have just a fraction of that.

His argument is not a vertical one about energy policy but a horizontal one about implementation. “Thinking has never been as big a constraint as implementation. My own view is that we are all Brahmins. None of us are manual labour who get out there and actually work in the trenches.” And I agree, because every oil crisis has produced excellent position papers, and yet half a century later, the import dependency number has not moved.

The second-order effects he walks through are genuinely worth listening to in full. The Strait of Hormuz carries 45% of the world’s sulphur. Sulphur makes sulphuric acid, and sulphuric acid is used to leach copper and nickel, and to etch chips. So a choke point in the Persian Gulf has a knock-on effect on semiconductor manufacturing in Taiwan. LPG disruption hits fertilizer plants, and if that disruption extends into June, it could affect the kharif sowing season. “I’ll leave it to you to imagine what the second and third and fourth-order effects of that are.”

What Mohit would actually bet on

PGK, being PGK, eventually slides in the investment questions despite insisting he was not going to. Mohit’s answers are measured but worth noting. He first mentions geographic diversification: “My income is in India, therefore I must diversify.” IT services, cautiously, because while the model is changing, the underlying role India plays in global technology is not going away. Manufacturing, carefully, because the Apple assembly story is real, but India is still capturing only a fraction of the value that China does, and moving up that chain is the actual story worth watching.

But none of that changes what is happening right now. The war continues, the strait remains closed, the second- and third-order effects are still unfolding, and the fourth ones have not even been named yet. Mohit said we can respond to everything, but “eventually” is a big part of that sentence.

I am also still waiting to see when sambhar will be back on the menu.
