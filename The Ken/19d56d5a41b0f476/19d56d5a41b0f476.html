“Indian internet was just about getting to monetising user attention, now it’s screwed again,” said an Indian founder to me over lunch last week.

By “monetising user attention”, he was referring to all sorts of fees that leading Indian e-commerce aggregators and platforms make by virtue of having a human user’s time and attention. Examples include premium product listings, convenience fees, and advertising on post-order screens.

The core business for most is still loss-making, he said. “We were getting profitable after years of paying taxes to our US ‘zamindars’ (landlords) like AWS, Meta, and Google. Now, there’s a new zamindar,” he half-joked, half-bemoaned.

“AI” is the new landlord.

Our very first Zero Shot live event on 12 April is entirely about imagining, debating, and understanding the disruptive transition for Indian internet and e-commerce over the next two years. Which means, we get together to visualise and debate what things might look like in April 2028.

At the heart of the event will lie numerous hyper-detailed but speculative scenarios involving real companies. Each scenario is only one guess of many possible futures, so they are not meant to be taken literally or as predictions.

The thread that ties all of them together is AI agents.

“Unlike a chatbot that simply responds to a single query, an agent is coordinated by an orchestrator that plans tasks, uses external tools, and then sequences steps to accomplish a goal. These connections are enabled through application programming interfaces (APIs) and increasingly standardized by the model context protocol (MCP), which makes plug-and-play autonomy possible. To work effectively, agents also rely on memory to carry context across sessions, authentication credentials to securely interact with external systems, and a persistent agent profile that allows them to remember users across time. If a chatbot is like a copilot for a single task, then an agent is more like an autopilot managing multi-step workflows with minimal oversight.”

Our goal at the event is to figure out ways in which we can all evolve our thinking around a future where AI agents are present in many spheres of our lives.

What principles should we use to imagine the possibilities?

What constraints should we set?

How should we learn to see the incentives of everyone involved?

How can we learn to visualise not just one likely event, but also its second- and third-order effects?

Here are 12 of the possible scenarios we might discuss during the event. They’re all speculative and made-up, and will be open to comments, rebuttals, and debate on 12 April. Since we may not have the time to discuss them all during the event, you can also upvote the ones you’d like picked.

Caveat: The way to really think about these scenarios is not by viewing them as inevitable, but impossible. Once you think they are impossible, think of the reasons why. Were the companies more innovative and nimble than we assume? Did they co-opt agentic AI better than others? Did consumers refuse to change? Did regulators?

These scenarios exist not as predictions, but as very clear, hypothetical foundations. So it becomes easier for us to imagine and debate the future.

1. Instamart

By mid-2027, personal AI shopping agents begin assembling grocery baskets across platforms in real time. They compare Blinkit, Zepto, Bigbasket, Amazon Now, and Instamart simultaneously, ruthlessly optimising on the price of each item (down to the rupee), and how soon it can be delivered (down to the minute). This triggers yet another brutal price war, causing a collapse of Instamart’s net margins.

Its ad revenue, meanwhile, has started collapsing from a peak of 14% of total revenue and 6–7% of GMV, because algorithms (which drive AI agents) don’t “browse” or succumb to impulse “dark pattern” marketing. Brands stop paying for shelf placement that agents never see. Non-grocery expansion stalls as agents have directed electronics and beauty purchases to Amazon and Flipkart instead.

2. Big Billion Days

By the time BBD 2027 launched on 26 September, AI agents had already pre-indexed every stock-keeping unit (SKU), every coupon, and every bank offer across Flipkart, Amazon, Meesho, and Jiomart simultaneously. They also had access to price history across all platforms.

In just 400 milliseconds, AI agents found the latest Samsung smartphone cheaper on Amazon with an HDFC card offer. Many fashion orders, too, got routed to Meesho, where margins were thinner but prices were lower.

Flipkart’s 600+ million visits collapsed to less than 200 million as single API calls replaced dozens of page-loads. Ad impressions cratered 70%. Sponsored listing revenue, which funded the discounts, evaporated. Amazon’s Great Indian Festival, meanwhile, went from being a “discovery-led” shopping event to a price-lookup utility. The festive season’s GMV dispersed across multiple platforms in real time, with agents capturing 8–12% cashback through affiliate arbitrage that no human would have found.

Both the models had hinged on one thing: humans browsing.

3. Meesho and Rapido

In March 2026, Meesho launched Vaani, a voice-first AI-shopping assistant. The architecture was purpose-built for the agent era: edge computing for sub-second speech, a multi-agent framework for complex transactions, and fine-tuned LLMs trained on 251 million annual transacting users’ behaviour across tier 2+ India.

By mid-2027, Meesho opened Vaani’s rails to external AI agents. Any Sarvam-powered, Gemini-native, or Whatsapp-embedded household agent could query Meesho’s 706,000 sellers through a conversational API—in Tamil, Hindi, Bengali, and Marathi. The zero-commission model, once a vulnerability (no ad revenue from agents who don’t browse), became the weapon: sellers paid nothing, so prices were 20–30% lower than Flipkart and Amazon. Every price-optimising agent in India routed value-segment purchases to Meesho by default.

Meanwhile, Rapido had already cracked by 2025 what Uber and Ola couldn’t: a subscription model where captains paid a small daily fee and kept 100% of fares. By mid-2027, Rapido launched Captain Connect—an open dispatch API letting any AI agent book rides, deliveries, and errands programmatically. The pricing was transparent and subscription-based: agents paid Rs 499 a month for unlimited API access with priority matching.

No surge. No commissions hidden inside fares. An agent comparing Rapido, Uber, and Namma Yatri would consistently find Rapido 12–18% cheaper on identical routes.

By April 2028, 60% of Rapido’s rides—from grocery pickups to pharmacy runs, and airport drops to office commutes—were agent-initiated.

4. Zomato

By mid-2027, meal-planning agents integrated into Google Home, Healthifyme, and Whatsapp-native bots began bypassing the Zomato app. They parsed restaurant menus via API, cross-referenced nutritional preferences, household budgets, past orders, and real-time Swiggy pricing in under two seconds.

Zomato’s restaurant-advertising revenue collapsed first. The 15–30% commission model held for longer, but restaurants were angrily asking why they should pay for discovery when the agent had already decided.

Worse, agents began routing to direct restaurant ordering via platforms like Thrive, Dotpe, and Ownly, because they found identical menus at 18–22% lower prices (Zomato’s commission). Restaurants, no longer shackled by Zomato’s exclusive fleet, route deliveries to independent riders managed by localised AI hubs using the state-owned Open Network for Digital Commerce (ONDC).

By January 2028, Deepinder Goyal was back as CEO.

5. Phonepe and Google

By mid-2027, AI agents orchestrating household spending, bill payments, and e-commerce checkout stopped opening Phonepe. They initiated UPI transactions directly through banking APIs, bypassing third-party apps entirely (“Pay all bills”, “Get me the cheapest flight”, “Pay Amit for dinner”). NPCI’s open UPI rails were what powered Phonepe’s rise. But that same interoperability has now made the company optional.

The 30% market-share cap that NPCI had deferred to December 2026 became moot.

Meanwhile, Google, in early 2026, launched the Universal Commerce Protocol (UCP) alongside Agent Payments Protocol (AP2), developed with over 60 partners, including Mastercard, Visa, Paypal, American Express, Flipkart, and Shopify.

Gemini-powered agents began managing Indian household finances by late 2027, transacting through AP2 natively, with authorisation, authenticity, and accountability baked in at the protocol layer. Google’s stack became the preferred one for everything—from agent-initiated grocery orders on Blinkit to Rapido rides and Meesho purchases.

6. Makemytrip

It’s mid-2027, and Gemini and GPT-powered travel agents continue to search Makemytrip, but they are also querying hotel property-management systems, airline APIs, and bus operators directly. A family planning a Goa trip would tell their agent a budget and dates. The agent would then pull live inventory from 12 hotel channels simultaneously, find the same Taj property at 22% less by booking through the hotel’s own engine (Makemytrip’s commission), and assemble flights via Airline Direct Connects.

Makemytrip’s entire value of aggregation, comparison, reviews, and curation has collapsed into a single API call. Its dominance had relied on human friction. They’d pay a premium on hotels because comparing 50 properties was exhausting. But AI agents rejected it by finding zero-markup alternatives via decentralised protocols.

7. The anti-aggregator agentic stack

By early 2026, ONDC had already crossed 200 million cumulative transactions. Though small, it was structurally radical because it was a working open-commerce protocol—any buyer app could talk to any seller app with no platform gatekeeping the middle.

Shopify powered storefronts for millions of merchants globally, with ONDC-compatible integrations already live for Indian sellers. Razorpay processed payments for over 10 million businesses. And Sarvam had just launched its 105-billion-parameter sovereign LLM, fluent in 11 Indian languages with a voice latency of under 500 milliseconds, already integrated with Razorpay for conversational checkout.

By 2028, an upgraded version of ONDC acts as the universal ledger, connecting Sarvam’s agents to Shopify’s headless storefronts. Razorpay served as the critical payments layer, providing agentic escrow services that release funds only upon verifiable ONDC delivery pings.

8. Urban Company

Urban Company’s moat was supposed to be trust—verified, trained, rated professionals you couldn’t find on your own.

By early 2027, Sarvam and Gemini-powered household agents began doing what Urban Company’s app did, but without the commission. Ravi, a plumber in Indiranagar, used to earn Rs 26,000 a month on Urban Company after a 25% platform cut. He later registered on ONDC’s services layer, listed himself on a Shopify-powered micro-storefront, and let his own AI agent handle scheduling, pricing, and customer communication in Kannada. Now, his agent negotiates directly with household agents. Razorpay settles payments. His effective earnings have jumped 30% overnight.

The supply-side exodus started with top-rated partners, because they were the product. Their personal agents carried their ratings, reviews, and availability as portable reputation (the latest regulations mandated it), interoperable across any buyer app on ONDC. Urban Company’s training and verification became a credential that partners exported.

By April 2028, the best electricians, beauticians, and AC technicians had become solo SaaS businesses, each powered by a Rs 500 per month AI agent that replaced a 25% commission.

9. Cred

Like Makemytrip, Cred’s moat too was “friction”, albeit in a different way. It had cracked the hard task of making affluent users enter the app to pay bills and discover luxury direct-to-consumer brands.

By mid-2027, the same affluent users Cred had painstakingly curated began delegating financial management to personal AI agents. These bots bypass Cred’s user interface entirely, ignoring “Cred Coins” and gamified jackpots. Without human eyeballs, Cred’s lending business came under siege.

Agents could now scan the entire market for a 0.1% lower interest rate, and it rendered Cred’s “exclusive” pre-approved loans a commodity. They even found identical products for most Cred products on D2C brands’ Shopify stores for 30% less, settled via Razorpay.

By April 2028, Cred’s revenue mix inverted: lending shrank as agents disintermediated loan discovery, the store withered as agents killed curated commerce, and UPI was commodity plumbing.

10. Uber

By mid-2028, household AI agents are querying Uber, Rapido, Namma Yatri, and ONDC-connected fleet operators simultaneously. They are comparing the ETA, price, surge multiplier, driver rating, and cancellation probability in real time. The agent books whichever platform has the highest completion likelihood at the lowest price at that exact moment.

Worse, Rapido’s subscription captains (who kept 100% of their fares) were cheaper for agents to route to. Uber’s commission model meant higher base fares, which agents penalised algorithmically.

11. Policybazaar

Policybazaar’s moat was comparison. People would spend 45 minutes on Policybazaar comparing 14 health plans, toggling sum insured, checking co-pay clauses, and reading claim-settlement ratios.

By mid-2027, a Sarvam or Gemini-powered financial agent could ingest a family’s medical history, age profile, existing coverage gaps, and employer benefits, and then query every insurer’s API directly, parse policy wordings programmatically, score claim-settlement ratios, flag sub-limits and exclusions, and recommend the optimal plan in under 90 seconds.

The comparison engine became a commodity capability embedded in every household’s AI stack. As insurers realised they were paying 15–35% commission to an aggregator that agents had made optional, the smarter ones launched agent-friendly APIs with 5% direct-purchase incentives.

12. Bookmyshow

By mid-2027, when Coldplay announced a 2028 India return, household AI agents didn’t frantically refresh Bookmyshow at 10 a.m. They registered intent across Bookmyshow, District (Zomato’s events platform), and the venue’s own ticketing API simultaneously. They auto-selected the optimal seat-to-price ratio, picked credit card offers, and completed checkout in 300 milliseconds.

Convenience fee, which was Bookmyshow’s highest-margin revenue line within ticketing, came under pressure as agents comparison-shopped across platforms. The Rs 50–150 fee per ticket that users never questioned became the first line item an agent tried to avoid.

The live-events business still held. But ticketing, the founding business, was becoming a commodity pipe.
