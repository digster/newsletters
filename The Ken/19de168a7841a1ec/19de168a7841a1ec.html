I did not participate in Hyrox. Nor do I intend to anytime soon. 

So you can sit back and relax. This isn’t a story of how I attended Hyrox, pushed myself, had a great time, and wrote a motivational Linkedin post about it. No. Pushing and pulling sleds are for actual athletes or reindeers—not for me. 

But I will admit I got quite curious about it. Hyrox is the fitness race everyone in your gym is training for and everyone on your Instagram is posting about. Also for a while, I kept constantly hearing the same thing…Hyrox this, hyrox that. Random strangers would bump into me in Bengaluru and ask me what my Hyrox time was. So finally, I got very annoyed and asked a few people who went that weekend “what exactly happened at this Hyrox event?” 

Well, some of the stories I heard were pretty bizarre. 

“It’s like a carnival”

A carnival? Isn’t it a fitness event?

“I wanted to participate but they ran out of tickets. So instead I paid to go and watch”

Wait, you paid to go and watch people sweat in front of you?

“People were getting their make-up done on the sidelines with professional photographers”

Make-up?!?

Honestly, the stories they told me ran counter to my mental idea of what a fitness event was supposed to be like. Who wants to go and watch people sweat and jump around? That too, just ordinary people who aren’t even athletes (even if some of them believe they are). In fact most of them were regular folks who wouldn’t be out of place in say, a pub in downtown Bengaluru or at a VC startup event.

But that’s just one part.

The more interesting story is how it has been monetised. 

Hyrox is also one of the most interesting business models in global sport. It’s a premium commercial format, with revenue lines through event tickets, a global licensing model, a PUMA deal, and local sponsors. That commercial stack sits on top of a culture that markets itself on community, participation, and well…vanity.

And to find out why the product and the business model really took off, I sat down with Prasanna and Dilip who took part in Hyrox Bengaluru for the latest episode of Two by Two. 

Incidentally, these are people who know a bit about fitness and the business of fitness. And because they are actual athletes and not pretend ones, they were gracious enough to share their Hyrox pictures. 

Prasanna Akela is the co-founder of Belong, a personal training studio in Bengaluru. Before Belong, Prasanna was an early growth leader at companies like CRED, Apple, and Uber India. He’s also competed at the national level in ultimate frisbee and has trained extensively in endurance and strength. He brings the operator’s view: what does someone building a fitness business in India actually see when a global format like Hyrox walks in?

Dilip Kumar leads investments at Rainmatter*, Zerodha’s health and fitness fund, which has deployed over Rs 250 crore across dozens of investments in health and fitness—including Hyrox India, Ironman, and Devil Circuit. He’s also a serious endurance athlete with a 2:55 marathon personal best and a finisher at the Boston Marathon. He has publicly called Hyrox as India’s “2008 IPL moment” for fitness.

Here are some of my favourite parts of the discussion: 

How Hyrox “broke even” by selling tickets of Rs 9,000 each

One of the most interesting things about Hyrox was where it made its money, and how it fundamentally changed the game. Unlike every other fitness event before it, like marathons, it made money from participants…and not sponsors. 

And well, Dilip was the one who told us how that happened.

 

Everyone on social media was like—Rs 9,000, Rs 8 crore. I can say we broke even just in that one event. Rs 8 crore. If you multiply the math—number of people, the tickets—that’s the number you make just from registrations. There is no other event, not just in India but anywhere outside of the Hyrox universe, which can make that kind of ratio money through participant ticket. And the remaining—which is a top-up—is sponsorship.

He went on to add how the business model inverted, which made it completely different from the past.

 

In this business, unlike any other sports fitness participatory format including marathons, 75 to 80% of the revenue is made by tickets. The remaining is sponsorship. In almost any other participatory sport it’s exactly the reverse—90% sponsorship, 10% tickets. You as an event operator can actually make money without relying on or being dependent on the sponsor. That is the biggest unlock. 

Getting sponsorship in participatory sport has been hard because brands are saying—who’s participating? I am better off in the queue for IPL five years from now.

The LTV of running vs the LTV of Hyrox

Prasanna, on the other hand, was much more sceptical about the long-term prospects of Hyrox. Especially from a lifetime value (LTV) perspective. In his mind, the LTV i.e. the total amount that a product can extract from a customer over a lifetime is much more for something like say running than for Hyrox.

 

In five years, can I extract, let’s say, Rs 3 lakh from this consumer because they did Hyrox at time A, now I want to do it at time B, then I want to do it at time C? 

I think the only vanity I see that’s happening is let’s go to Hyrox in Bangkok. Okay, that’s fun. Let’s make this a holiday and then let’s do one in Singapore. 

And that I think is a much more limited financial engine as opposed to, you know, running for ten years. I can ask Dilip—if he had to put an LTV of how much money do you think you’ve contributed to running as a product—I think it’ll be significantly more than what people would do in Hyrox.

Same people. More money. Different format.

Dilip on the other hand was optimistic from a different angle. 

Maybe the number of people who’d do Hyrox over time was limited, but their spending power wasn’t.

 

What is happening in India is that the TAM (total addressable market) is not increasing significantly. That includes anything—whether it’s a protein powder or a bar or organic juice or a wearable or a shoe. That TAM is not increasing. But what is happening is the wallet share of the existing TAM is getting bigger. The same guy is going to a longevity clinic, going to a fitness studio, has a nutritionist. 

That is still sizable for a business to operate in India.

Can that be a hundred million, billion-dollar company? Maybe not today. But our bet is that maybe five, ten years from now, the wallet share has increased significantly and the TAM has meaningfully increased — not 5x, 10x, but it will increase because we are talking about a cultural shift.

I recommend listening to the episode in full. There were many things we discussed like why an Indian company, like say Cult couldn’t have come up with Hyrox, how the format itself may go through some fatigue, and whether both of them would participate in Hyrox again. 

Spoiler: they would.
