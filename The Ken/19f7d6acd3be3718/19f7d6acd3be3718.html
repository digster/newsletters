In a narrow Chandni Chowk street in Delhi, jumbled wires snake across the sky. Anwar picks the one connecting to the streetlight and starts soldering it. Carefully, he splices one wire into another to route the electricity elsewhere. The meter nearby blinks. 

The 27-year-old electrician is demonstrating how electricity is stolen. 

“It’s an art,” he says proudly. He does it as a side gig. “Sometimes even rich people call me. They’ve got four or five ACs and don’t want to pay the bill, so they just steal electricity.”

Katiya, or hooking an illegal wire onto someone else’s power line, is one of the oldest tricks in the book. Old electromechanical meters make the process a lot easier. 

This is all part of a chronically leaky system that has plagued power-distribution companies for decades. 

Over the years, AT&C losses have fallen from around 22% in FY14 to 15% in FY25. The government is pushing state-distribution companies to bring them down even further. Especially considering they’re sitting on more than Rs 7 lakh crore in debt. Private-distribution companies like Adani Electricity—which has a revenue of over Rs 17,000 crore and AT&C losses of 4.7%—are doing better. But even it has filed 1,166 FIRs against power theft in FY25 alone. 

This is exactly the problem the government’s Revamped Distribution Sector Scheme (RDSS) was designed to solve. Launched in 2021, it aims to bail out state power-distribution companies through systemic reforms. Including spending $30 billion to install over 200 million smart meters across the country.
