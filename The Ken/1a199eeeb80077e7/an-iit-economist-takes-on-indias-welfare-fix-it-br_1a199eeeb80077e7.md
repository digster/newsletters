Every month, households that rely on subsidised grain must press a thumb to a scanner at their ration shop before they can collect it. If the machine doesn’t recognise them, or the server is down, or the connection drops, they go home and come back another day. In 2023–24, UIDAI, the agency that runs Aadhaar, earned more than Rs 600 crore from authentication services.
Over the past decade, the Indian state has adopted technology quickly and uncritically, and the people least able to absorb its failures have paid the price.

IIT Delhi economist Reetika Khera’s latest book Freebies or Rights goes beyond political binaries to offer an impassioned defence for coherent social policy. Inspired in part by hearing criticisms in her IIT and IIM classrooms that “betray a misunderstanding” of social policy, a hitherto “neglected field of study”.
The book grew out of over two decades of research, beginning with her Phd in 2002, and draws on surveys of thousands of citizens and public servants across 14 states. That fieldwork gives it a dizzying array of first-hand accounts of hastily-planned technological intervention.
We learn that village nurses and midwives must upload a photo of a breastfeeding mother that they have just counselled, and are punished if they can’t find data connectivity. Public officials in schools and hospitals work unpaid extra hours to log their efforts on the online system imposed from above, because the internet is better in the evenings.
