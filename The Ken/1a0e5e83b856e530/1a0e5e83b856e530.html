Like every GPU cloud, Yotta Data Services buys chips from Nvidia. Unlike most other clouds, the chip giant may be helping the Indian AI cloud-service provider scramble to raise money for those chips.

On 11 September, Yotta told Nvidia it wanted 80,000 GPUs in two tranches—a $12 billion order, the company’s third in seven months.

In February, it had ordered 20,736 Blackwell Ultra chips. By August, that had grown to 50,000 Vera Rubin chips. That’s some intensive retail therapy for a company that made $108 million in revenue in FY25 and is carrying about $1.1 billion in net debt.

Where is the money for this coming form?

For starters, The Ken has learned that Yotta is set to join Nvidia’s AI Compute Partnership. Nvidia has essentially agreed to rent Yotta’s unused GPUs at a set rate, guaranteeing that Yotta will have a revenue one way or another and making it easier for the company to finance its purchase.

The financing itself is more dispersed. The company is planning a public listing by Q1 FY28, with a target to raise $1.5 billion at a valuation of $6 billion. Among other things, the money, according to founder Sunil Gupta, will be used to buy more GPUs (again) and repay debt, which is expected to touch $1.4 billion by the end of FY27. Another chunk, though, will come from HNIs and family offices (around $150 million) and Abu Dhabi’s sovereign wealth fund Mubadala (which is mulling a pre-IPO stake).

This is all to say, trace where the money for India’s GPU-and-data-centre ecosystem comes from, and the address is firmly foreign…

James C. Scott’s Seeing Like a State turns on one idea: legibility. He means that a state can only tax, conscript, and administer what it can first flatten into standard categories – surnames, cadastral maps, fixed measures, official procedures – and that most of real social life is not that.

I think we have to ask what “seeing like an agent” would require. My view is that agents need a similar kind of legibility, and not just at the end of a job. It is not enough that the final result is checkable – did the insurance get bought, did the transfer go through. Every step from the first action to the last has to be something the agent can actually see and act on. If any stretch of the path is informal, discretionary, or only real when a particular person is present, the agent does not have a task.

If personal assistants take off in a place like the US, that is evidence that the underlying tasks there are already legible – online, searchable, form-shaped, automatable end to end. India is nowhere near that shape and form and that level of legibility. It is full of work that looks agent-ready from a distance because we already hand it to someone. Break any one of those jobs into the actual sequence of things that have to happen and it stops looking ripe for personal agents. When systems are not legible, the human you hire is not a temporary stand-in waiting to be automated. They are doing the parts no agent can see.
