In the first quarter of FY27, Suzlon Energy, which powers nearly one in every three wind turbines in India, recorded a net profit of over Rs 300 crore. 

Ten years earlier, in the first quarter of FY17, it was straddled with net losses amounting to Rs 260 crore. 

And even though India’s windmills may have been relegated to the shadows of its ever-multiplying solar panels, the country still achieved its highest-ever wind capacity addition, over 6 gigawatts (GW), in 2025–26, a nearly 50% increase from the previous year. 

Overall, it’s almost as if wind energy’s fortunes are tied at the hip of Suzlon’s. They’ve always tracked each other: from crackling, high-growth beginnings, to a painful slowdown and a gradual turnaround. 

As Prabhakar Sharma, renewable energy research lead at Gurugram-based JMK Research and Analytics, comments, “For the same power capacity, wind costs approximately twice as much as solar to set up,” and Suzlon’s confidence in wind could only go so far in sun-soaked India without some help.

In FY26, Suzlon clocked in a revenue of close to Rs 16,700 crore: nearly six times as much as six years ago. And, as of 14 August, its market capitalisation stands at over Rs 64,385 crore, according to financial platform Screener. At Rs 47.1, the stock price is not stratospheric, like the mid-2000s when it was oversubscribed nearly 15 times on day one of its IPO. But it is far better than the worst-ever, at the turn of this decade.

India could be on the verge of a reckoning with wind, moving towards optimising its nearly 700 GW wind energy potential. And playing second fiddle to solar might actually set it on a course with a favourable tailwind.
