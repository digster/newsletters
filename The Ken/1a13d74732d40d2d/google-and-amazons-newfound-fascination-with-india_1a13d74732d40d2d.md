Good Morning Ishan,
Every monsoon, Manu works in counts of five.
In the first half of July, usually the fifth month in her lunar calendar, she starts a motor and floods her paddy field with up to five centimetres of water. For five days, she lets it soak, and once the paddy stands in relatively dry soil, she digs a channel and drains the excess. Five days later, she floods the field again.
Manu’s family has farmed rice in Nashik, Maharashtra for nearly 20 years. “They would keep the field filled with water, all the time,” she said. “What I have been doing for the past six years saves water.”
The technique is called alternate wetting and drying (AWD), and it saves more than just water.
In 2024, out of nowhere, Manu got a call from an organisation called Value Network Ventures. They wanted to pay her Rs 2,000 per acre to do exactly what she said she’d been doing all along.
Turns out, wetting and drying paddy fields can cut down methane emissions. Paddy fields contributed to 3.2 million tonnes of methane released in India—about 16% of the country’s total methane emissions—according to its 2020 report to a United Nations body.
Right now, a company that can show a farmer using AWD can count the methane that wasn’t released, turn it into carbon credits, and sell it to buyers who want to cancel out their own emissions.
