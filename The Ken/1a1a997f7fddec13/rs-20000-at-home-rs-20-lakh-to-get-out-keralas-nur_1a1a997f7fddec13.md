Good Morning Dear Reader,
Shuttling between an ailing mother with a baby in the neonatal ICU and a profusely bleeding ‘Code Omega’ patient urgently needing a transfusion is probably not how most of us picture a new nurse’s night shift.
But that is where GM found herself, in the last hours of a shift one September night in 2023. She had just finished her BSc in Nursing in Bengaluru and had taken her first job at a large private hospital in the city.
Like the other nurses in this story, GM asked to be identified only by her initials for fear of repercussions. She still remembers how excited she was to clear her probation. But nothing could have prepared her for what lay ahead. Within months, she would resign.
Her days, and alternate nights, went in racing up and down the hospital ramps to fetch emergency medicines from the ground-floor pharmacy; tending to two ventilated patients at once, despite the 1:1 nurse-to-patient ratio that such treatment demands; and getting back to her hostel in ambulances when shifts ran over and she missed the last bus. She held out for a year and three days before moving to a hospital back home in Kerala. She left with aching joints and sprained ligaments.
So why do nurses put up with it? For most of them, a job at home is a toll they pay on the way out. They want a job abroad, ideally in the UK, Ireland, or Canada. And those jobs usually ask for experience at home first.
…
So Kerala’s nurses are stuck between the devil and the deep blue sea, still paying the toll at home with less and less certainty that the road leads anywhere. The hospitals collecting it know that.
