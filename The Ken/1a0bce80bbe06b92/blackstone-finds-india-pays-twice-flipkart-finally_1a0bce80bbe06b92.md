[How Blackstone went from buying AGS Health to cashing out in a year](https://the-ken.com/story/how-blackstone-pulled-a-buy-and-flip-in-less-than-a-year/?utm_source=weekly_email&utm_medium=email&utm_campaign=weekly_recap_card)
Global private equity has figured out that US medical-billing companies like AGS Health sell for twice as much on the Indian stock exchange
Blackstone financed the AGS Health acquisition using debt, and nearly 90% of the fresh capital raised in the Indian IPO will be used to clear those liabilities. The private-equity firm filed for this public listing less than a year after the initial acquisition, targeting a valuation reportedly double its purchase price. The valuation increase is primarily driven by geographic arbitrage, as Indian public markets trade US-facing revenue cycle management firms at 24–27X EV/Ebitda, compared to 11–15X in the US market. This deal establishes a structural precedent for the sector, with an estimated $10–15 billion in similar PE-backed RCM assets currently positioned for potential Indian listings. The new ISI Bill 2026 is triggering nostalgia for a freer time and protests in equal measure
In August, the ISI Bill was introduced in the Lok Sabha, looking to change the structure of India’s leading statistical institute. The bill proposes a new administrative structure: 11 board members, where seven will be markedly aligned with the government. Meanwhile, the country is contending with debates on statistical sanctity in the wake of former finance secretary’s comments on GDP numbers. ISI has long shaped India’s statistical tradition, but it will now be closer to Delhi than before. [India wants to insure its space boom. Insurers want a space boom first](https://the-ken.com/story/india-wants-to-insure-its-space-boom-insurers-want-a-space-boom-first/?utm_source=weekly_email&utm_medium=email&utm_campaign=weekly_recap_card)
From Skyroot and Agnikul to New India Assurance and Tata AIG, India’s nascent space industry is forcing two very different sides of the market to meet
A failed mission can wipe out tens or hundreds of crores, making the cost of a policy difficult for young companies to justify. Global insurers have already retreated from the sector after a run of expensive satellite losses, leaving Indian operators with limited capacity to tap. The problem gets harder with new rockets and components, where insurers have little flight history to assess reliability or price the risk. And as reusable rockets and larger satellite constellations bring new forms of liability, India’s rules still leave important questions about who pays for a very large claim. It sells everything from atta to paperboards. But the business that keeps paying the bills is still the one it has spent decades trying to outgrow
Government rules in the 1970s forced ITC’s British parent to reduce its stake, leaving the company with funds to diversify. That diversification eventually brought ITC into conflict with its parent, triggering a corporate battle and the exit of senior executives. The new leadership chose to press ahead, using ITC’s agricultural network and expertise to build e-Choupal and, eventually, Aashirvaad. Years later, the strategy evolved again, as ITC bought established food brands while shedding the capital-intensive business of owning hotels.
