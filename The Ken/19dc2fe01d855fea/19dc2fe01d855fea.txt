It’s been about a week since the latest episode dropped of what’s fast becoming a monthly SaaSpocalypse series. This time, it was design tools https://x.com/immasiddx/status/2045177648897495538.

noop:void(0)

So, I called up a good friend who also happens to be, well, a designer. Dhruv Saxena is co-founder at Presentations.ai and was a former design head at Phonepe, and before that, co-founder and CEO at design firm Obvious.

We had an opinionated conversation around a question I posed to Dhruv: Can any software maker ever hope to compete against its own customers and users?

Dhruv’s thinking and answers were too real and unfiltered for me to want to convert them into my own words (you do know that’s what we journalists mostly do through reporting and writing, right?)

So, this week’s Zero Shot column is a Q&A. I think you’ll enjoy it. If you don’t, please write and tell me why.

Rohin Dharmakumar: If you look at what Figma did to Sketch, or what everybody is doing to Adobe, or what’s happening to Microsoft—there is one strain which is really these large organisations and products that have basically just extracted value over the years, and people had no choice. There was a lot of pent-up anger against Adobe, etc., and people are just glad to stop using it.

But that’s not the case with Figma. Nobody hates Figma.

My hypothesis is that it’s very hard for a business to compete against its own customers. Traditional SaaS products involve you building a product for your customers and competing against another SaaS product that is also competing for those customers. You’ve got to build a better feature so your customers remain your customers. Now what’s happening is something different. Your customers are sort of like termites. If you’re Figma and your users are like, “Screw you, I don’t want to use Figma. I’m going to try and figure out how to use Claude Code to do A-B-C, or I’m going to use Claude Design,” there is no running. If your customer is fighting against using your product, then it’s game over.

You’re not fighting against a bunch of people in a garage, coding something together to compete against you. That you can still understand. It is literally the users who are paying you money who are saying, “I will build something.” And you can’t compete against them because every person knows how they work better than you ever will.

And, of course, the underlying thing is that all of these were just abstractions along the way to the final product. And now code is like the only truth, especially if you’re building a digital product, which is actually true. So, this is one strand of thought where people are saying that the standard path was somebody builds a better abstraction along the path and wins the market. Sketch did something, Figma came along and did something in the browser, etc., but now there is no more scope for abstractions because now it’s just you tell a computer what to do and it generates code. There’s nothing else.

Dhruv Saxena: I think everybody who bought it [Figma] initially is still using it. I think this hypothesis can be looked at in two ways. One is the way you’re looking at it: your customer is saying, “I’ll build it myself.”

Rohin: Or “I know a better way to do this.”

Dhruv: But this has always existed for developers. Now it’s just that everybody’s doing it…

This week on the Zero Shot podcast

Hi! I am Vidhatri, the producer of Zero Shot!

Last week, I told you about our “Zero Shot ideas” Slack channel. It’s the place where we share everything we hear, see, listen to, and think about AI. The range is pretty staggering (unsurprising, as AI seems to be practically enveloping our lives). 

A recurring theme is the similarities and divergences in how the US and China approach AI development. We have some favourite thinkers and writers on the subject. And one of them is Afra Wang, a researcher who writes Concurrent http://concurrent. on Substack.

In her own words, it focuses “on the parallel and colliding tech and cultural currents shaping Silicon Valley, China, and beyond”. I highly recommend reading it, especially her posts on a Beijing vibe coder https://afraw.substack.com/p/story-of-a-chinese-vibe-coder and how Chinese society is embracing AI. https://afraw.substack.com/p/an-ai-maxi-new-year

Afra was the guest on our latest episode, and the discussion covered a lot of ground. Afra is specific about how Silicon Valley and China misread each other. For example, Chinese entrepreneurs assume Washington funds AI the way Beijing does. Americans think Deepseek came out of nowhere. In reality, it was an outcome of 20 years of open source culture, industrial policy, and a Huawei ban that forced China to build its own everything. And then there are certain ways in which China uses AI, for example, in the leather manufacturing industry. America would never be able to relate to it, simply because it doesn’t have that industry. 

All in all, a fascinating conversation with someone who understands both places deeply!
