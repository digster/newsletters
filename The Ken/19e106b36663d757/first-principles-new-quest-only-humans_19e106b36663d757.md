Dear Ishan, good morning. I don’t know about you, but I am absolutely sick of reading AI-generated words that humans put out as theirs.
Also, I am double absolutely tired of programs telling me how I should write. A microsecond after I hit enter on my first sentence, Microsoft Word, the word processing software part of the office productivity suite formerly known as Microsoft Office but now just Microsoft 365 Pilot, told me I should get rid of the word “absolutely” because “more concise language would be clearer for your reader.”
Now, getting pissed about AI language, which is all mostly generated from large language models, is pyrrhic.
Because language is how we humans communicate, which is why AI models are so good at what they do. Because they hacked us humans via our language. Seriously, imagine a future intelligence that had the power to travel through time and wanted to defeat humanity. What better way would there be than to co-opt and use our own language against us!
Anyhow, it’s too late to rue that. The language cat is out of the bag.
What I am pissed about is humans passing off machine-generated language as their own. Imagine a software engineer (before they too became vibe-coders) sending you code in an email. Or a person who speaks a language entirely alien to you, talking to you sans a translator or interpreter.
You see how ridiculous it is, right? Because language, though universal and powerful, requires context and personality.
I can absolutely share my code (if I still remember how to write it) with another engineer. Someone comfortable in, say, Malayalam, can absolutely speak in that language with me.
But when we take AI-generated language, no matter how “polished” it might look, and present it as something that came from our brains and consciousness, we’re pretending.
I hate it. I hate how suddenly everyone is talking about the tapestry and the substrate. Or the scaffolding and what parts of it are load-bearing.
When did you learn to view ideas and concepts as architecture?
Then, of course, there is the ridiculous “not x, but y” rhetorical hook. Sometimes it’s inverted as “everyone is doing x, but y is the answer.”
Seriously, you ought to be ashamed of using this obvious tell not once, not twice, but four to five times in a single post!
Landed differently, did it? Yes, everything hits, lands, and drops these days. Who knew there was a physics to AI prose and that gravity would provide us the tell?
Finally, there is the predictable symmetry and rhythm to everything. An asinine hook as the first sentence and a recap at the end. Every other paragraph ending in a flourish.
If I were younger and cared more, I’d type “AI wrote this” as a comment or reply to each instance I encountered.
But since I am not, and because we—as the First Principles community—care more about imagining possibilities rather than tilting-ranting at AI windmills, let’s look at this differently.
Last week, I [posted this](https://www.linkedin.com/feed/update/urn:li:activity:7456961178456715265/) on Linkedin, in a fit of frustration.
“It’s incredible how quickly our brains are rewiring to spot and recognize imperfections, rawness and authenticity, after *decades* of being trained to do the same for perfection and polish.
As soon as I see the tell-tale signs of AI-generated structure and polish in things that people write or create, my brain recoils with an “Ugh!””
So, today we’re starting a new First Principles Quest, called Only Humans. I hope this can become a running and evolving quest.

The first thing we’ll build together is The Originals: a reader-curated archive of writing you are certain no AI could have produced. Letters, essays, blog posts from the early Blogger/Blogspot days, the email your father sent you when you were in college, the paragraph in a book you keep returning to. Any medium, any country, any era.
The filter is evidence, not taste. I’m not asking for the best writing you have read. I’m asking for examples whose language you’d attest to as undeniably human. Whose authorship is so visible that no model, however sophisticated, could simulate it without giving itself away. A line arguing why this could not have been generated would make your submission even more useful to everyone else.

Happy Mother’s Day!
This is Tanim with the First Principles books section.
Today, we have community recommendations about books that try to put into words the idea of a “mother”.
Before you dive into that, do let us know what you are reading [here](https://theken.typeform.com/FPBooks?typeform-source=the-ken.com).
Srishti Bajaj recommends Blue Sisters by Coco Mellors:
“Generally, I think relations between mothers and daughters are more complex. This book is about 4 sisters, their lives, and 1 tragedy that changes their lives forever. When you look at their lives, the impact of their mother is clearly evident: how they are, and why they are such. It shows how absent fathers are somehow okay, but issues with moms linger lifelong. These are very, very difficult to live with. The book brings this out beautifully, along with a closure that you didn’t expect all along. Very good read!”
Amar Chandramohan has two books that might interest you:
First is Shuggie Bain by Douglas Stuart: “Unrequited love is often used to describe a mother’s love for her child; in Shuggie Bain, you get the same theme, but here, the roles are reversed, with the child serving as the source of that unrequited love.”
Second is Em and the Big Hoom by Jerry Pinto, which “Explores the child/ parent bond against the nebulous backdrop of mental illness, which is never easy to write about.”
And so many of you wrote about Mother Mary Comes to Me by Arundhati Roy.
| Photo by Madhu |
Ramya Kancharla’s thoughts: “Mother Mary Comes to Me is Arundhati Roy’s unflinching memoir of her relationship with her mother, Mary. All mother-daughter relationships are complicated. But Mary and Arundhati Roy’s relationship is especially so, complicated by abuse, resentment, abandonment, betrayal, and love. Roy seems to have steeled herself to write this memoir, to take an unflinching look at her mother and her outsized impact on everyone around her, both magnificent and terrible. And she writes about it all with devastating beauty; even when, rather, especially when, she’s writing about sorrow and heartbreak. I found myself pausing mid-sentence again and again just to savour the language. What a writer, what a book!
Vanshika wrote: “This was my first read (or first listen, I heard it being narrated by Arundhati Roy on Audible. It’s fantastic) of 2026. It’s about surviving and thriving against all odds, having the courage to talk about complex relationships, dealing with grief, friendships, and standing up for injustice. As a woman of the late 90s, the book gave me the audacity to dream, be free, and be yourself.”
In Madhu’s words: “We all know Arundhati Roy as the acclaimed author of The God of Small Things, an activist, a gifted speaker, and more. But this book goes into who she was before she was all that and how her life was that led to her becoming all this as well. It will leave you surprised, confused, empathetic, and overall good for sure. From her troubled childhood and complicated relationship with her mother to the courage she has shown in making the choices she has made and continues to make, it is a powerful book to read. Her excellent writing is an added bonus to the amazing story she is telling. I definitely loved that.”
Sunaina appreciated: “The beauty of prose, the lucidity of language and sheer joy of immersing yourself in a book that by all accounts should not be a good book to read.”
Colonel Sekhar (R) also shared his words: “I have been fortunate enough to meet Ms Mary Roy in her School; had limited awareness of her wild mood swings. Yet, this book is a brutally honest tour de force with the author, laying bare her life with her mother, insecurities, warts, et al.”
