Good Morning Ishan,
Last week, Rohin wrote [about](https://the-ken.com/columns/zero-shot/our-anthropic-fever-has-broken/) Anthropic and fever. So this week, it’s only fitting that I write about OpenAI and winter.
I don’t know if you’ve been paying attention, but OpenAI has been doing some…interesting stuff of late.
First, it killed off not one but two of its consumer products, [Sora](https://www.theverge.com/ai-artificial-intelligence/902368/openai-sora-dead-ai-video-generation-competition) and [Atlas](https://techcrunch.com/2026/07/09/openai-is-shutting-down-atlas-but-its-ai-browser-ambitions-are-still-growing/).
If you remember, Sora was OpenAI’s video-generation app, and Atlas was its agentic browser. According to a report by the Wall Street Journal, Fidji Simo, OpenAI’s CEO of AGI deployment, told employees that, “We cannot miss this moment because we are distracted by side quests. We really have to nail productivity in general and particularly productivity on the business front.”
Then, it abandoned its original plan of owning and building data centres. And now seems to want to lease capacity from third-party providers. Almost a year ago, OpenAI had announced Stargate, a joint venture with computer-tech company Oracle and investment giant Softbank, which was supposed to invest $500 billion in AI data centers in the US.
But well, that plan is gone now.
And it’s not just Stargate, but [other](https://www.digitimes.com/news/a20260610VL219/openai-data-center-infrastructure-investment-nvidia.html) data centers in the US too.
Oh, also OpenAI put on-hold a plan to build a data centre in the UK, citing “restrictive regulations” and “high energy costs”. In response, UK AI Minister (they have an AI minister!!) in a statement to the Financial Times said that the “only thing that has changed [since] the moment of those commitments…has been the financing environment for OpenAI”.
Of course.
And now, more recently, OpenAI proposed giving the U.S. government a 5% stake in the company, worth roughly $42.6 billion. Why? Because OpenAI CEO Sam Altman thinks that [giving](https://www.ft.com/content/7c803eab-8e80-4431-9a87-e943bf00e00b?syn-25a6b1a6=1) the public a financial interest in the company is the best way to share the upside of AI.
Fun.
So what’s going on?

Hi everyone! This is Vidhatri, the producer of Zero Shot.
A lot of charts, graphs, and numbers get shared on our Slack channels. One of them caught my attention today and I want to point it out to you too.
It’s a [bar graph](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/kimi-k3-launches-57-artificial-analysis-intelligence-clwlc/) from the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index, an aggregate benchmark for evaluating AI models, which puts Beijing-based Moonshot AI’s just-released Kimi K3 at the number three spot. After Fable 5 and GPT-5.6.
The graph ties into conversations we’ve been having internally at The Ken about what it actually takes to develop cutting-edge AI models. Every day, there is a government directive here or a sweeping statement there about building AI. But nobody really answers how good frontier models are built or delves into the conditions required to build them.
When I try to understand these questions, more often than not, I end up thinking about what countries do. What steps can the state take to enable tech innovation? How does the private sector step in? What should research look like? How should you incentivise it?
All of these questions tie back back to China and its AI economy. That’s because understanding AI today requires understanding what’s happening in China.
This week, we covered a China story. But It’s not typically what we do on Zero Shot, where we usually get operators on and try to understand what’s happening within a company. This time around, we got Kendra Schaefer of Trivium China, a policy research firm. Kendra heads tech policy research there and manages Trivium Tech Daily, a brief which is read by global investors, companies, and policymakers tracking Chinese tech regulation, AI, and data policy.
She helped us understand that regulation in China—and the way it moves—actually reveals a lot about an industry. After giving us the overview of how tech companies in China navigate bureaucracy and the government, she focused on one specific registry that the Chinese government mandates companies to fill for every algorithm they develop and release.
This registry is supremely valuable because it has now become a public database of thousands of registered algorithms, each with a company name and a short description of its function. Basically, it’s the closest thing anyone has to a real-time map of who’s building what in Chinese AI.
Kendra essentially gave us a history lesson that helped us understand why Kimi K3 is where it is. It was a fascinating conversation.
