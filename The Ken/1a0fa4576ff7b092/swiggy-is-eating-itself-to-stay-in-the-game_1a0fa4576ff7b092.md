Good Morning Ishan,
Like all great stories, today’s story begins with a chart someone sent me that made me go WTF.
Here’s the chart from the brokerage firm CLSA, citing Sensor Tower data.
Alright, so Toing has almost as many weekly active users as Zomato. Sure, that’s impressive, but frankly, anyone can get users on their app once a week—you could just say, “Get burgers for Rs 10 only today”, and watch the swarm of users land on your app. Then when they check out, you could say, “Oops, offer expired.” And that’s how you get a fantastic WAU.
So, obviously, I was sceptical, but then I found a more interesting note, this time from Bernstein, also a brokerage and equity research firm, in their report titled “India Food Delivery: Lessons from China’s expensive ($30 billion) war for market share.”
For most of the period, Swiggy and Zomato together held a steady ~80% share of MAUs. In the last quarter, this has dipped, driven primarily by Toing (owned by Swiggy) gaining users, along with some early traction for Ownly (Rapido). We believe part of this disruption by Toing is the incumbent cannibalising itself to reach lower AOV customers.
Huh.
Food delivery is one of my oldest obsessions. But for the last few quarters and years, it’s been fairly unexciting, and the whole market has settled into a boring duopoly between Swiggy and Zomato. Both of them also seem to have carved up the market between themselves and are focused on their respective quick-commerce businesses. Everything is stable, and quite uninteresting.
All that changed earlier this year when Rapido decided to launch Ownly and brought their zero-commission play to food delivery. At the time, I did an episode of [Two by Two](https://the-ken.com/podcasts/two-by-two/rapido-broke-the-uber-ola-duopoly-can-it-now-break-the-swiggy-zomato-one/) where I was sceptical they’d make inroads and unsettle these two players.
Well, as it turns out, there is an insurgent challenger who is taking market share away from Zomato and Swiggy, except it’s owned by Swiggy itself. Toing is a food delivery app, serviced by Swiggy’s rider partners, selling food from often the same restaurants that are on the main Swiggy app, but at a lower price. Swiggy is funding this difference through lower commissions and lower delivery fees. As Deepinder Goyal, former CEO of Eternal, said back in June (when he was still CEO of Zomato), “The customer traction is purely price-driven & price-driven traction without structural economics tends to resolve itself.”
Umm, sure, but clearly Swiggy sees it differently. Since June, if anything, they have been spending much more, not less, on Toing.
So why is Swiggy prepared to bleed itself to stay in the game? It’s not under any imminent threat and is already waging a brutal price war with multiple challengers on the quick-commerce side.
All signs point to the fact that Swiggy does not have to do this, and yet it chooses to.
To answer why, Jignanshu Gor, director and senior research analyst at Bernstein, returns as a guest. He covers Indian internet and emerging consumer brands, including Eternal and Swiggy. He is also the author of the Bernstein report I alluded to earlier. In today’s episode, he brings back more war analogies, and together, Jignanshu and I try to piece together why Swiggy is doing what it’s doing.
Oh, also, as a special bonus, Brady Ng from The Ken also makes a special appearance to give us a closer look at what happened in China when Meituan was challenged, and how the market dynamics shifted, but in many ways, it didn’t.
It’s a great episode.

Jignanshu Gor
It has always been a question whether food services in India is a structural story or a cyclical story. The reason is that higher income levels, higher women’s participation in the workforce, greater technological adoption and greater organisation of the economy have, in every other large global market, driven the food services space much faster than GDP, than grocery spend and so on. To the extent that in the US and in China today, if you consider the total food spending that a household does, half is outside the home and half is inside the home. In India, that has not happened, and our contention is that even for the top 5% of Indians, that has not happened. This is a cultural issue, not an economic problem. Indians, when we get rich, we hire a cook rather than eating 3 meals outside. […] I don’t think India is China with a lag. India will have a different path of evolution, and that goes deep down to culture more than availability of food. Again, the idea is not that we will not eat outside or that the frequency won’t grow. It’s just that the pace is going to be completely different, and maybe even the end state might be completely different.
Jignanshu Gor
Even when we initiated on Swiggy and Zomato a year ago, our entire food delivery thesis was that we estimated growth rates which are under what everybody else was estimating at that point in time, because we saw that if I cut the food services market by income level or by average order value or by tier of city, or by restaurants which are actually registered with FSSAI, food delivery had a 30–35% share of the food services market already. […] So our thesis was that you cannot have a strong growth rate for longer until you expand the pool of people that are taking food delivery as a service, and that you cannot do until you go down the AOV. […] The average order value of a fast food restaurant is around Rs 120, and that of a normal restaurant is around Rs 185. And the average order value on Zomato and Swiggy is closer to Rs 500. So that’s what tells me that, versus where average India is, food delivery is selling essentially very expensive food.
Jignanshu Gor
Interestingly, over the last 2 years what has not changed is the number of annual transacting users. From FY22 to ‘26, specifically for Zomato, the number of annual transacting users increased by probably 15% over a 3–4 year period. But the monthly transacting users are growing fast, so the ATU are becoming MTU, which means consumer frequency has gone up. That’s what Zomato has seen. And last year was an interesting uptick for both Swiggy and Zomato. They saw a significantly higher number of annual users, so new to food delivery users came in, driven by a lot of the focus on quick commerce, which adds to ordering food inside the home as well, but also a lot of value initiatives.
Jignanshu Gor
The margin profile that a platform captures is a factor of the customer it serves versus the cost of servicing the customer. If you’re a platform business or an e-commerce business, your marginal cost of delivering an incremental order is non-zero. That is defined by what I call the cost pool. The revenue that you generate from that incremental customer is defined by a revenue pool of customers. In the case of Chinese food delivery apps, the revenue pool and the cost pool are reasonably close to each other. The cost pool is essentially your delivery riders. Now in India, like we mentioned earlier, food delivery annual consumers are around 90 million or so, out of a population of 1.4 billion. In China, this number is above 500 million, close to 700 million. So your revenue pool is inching closer to where your cost pool is. And when your incremental marginal cost and incremental marginal revenue are similar to each other in that income scale, the margins that you make are lower. That’s my mental framework.
Jignanshu Gor
A corollary is Meesho versus Amazon and Flipkart. It’s the same thing: your business model is governed towards making money via commissions. Restaurants will pass on those commissions to customers. So the whole idea is that customers should not feel I’m taking money from them, so I will take money from restaurants. And we should do the same thing, saying I don’t need money from you; let’s be fully transparent to both sides; we’ll figure out how to monetise eventually. That’s what the existing platforms could not do. I think it’s very tricky to do it on your own on a single platform, because there can be conflict on both the consumer side as well as on the seller side.
Jignanshu Gor
I think this is a defensive approach again, in some frame of mind similar to what they did on Instamart, but here the defensiveness is against new entrants: to make it difficult and unattractive for new entrants to come in, and force them to ask the question about scalability and profitability before launching. Typically, what happens is that when a private company launches it, the conversation about profitability also remains a private conversation, because there is no scale data available. Now, because your data is public, you can estimate how much growth Toing is bringing to the table; you can estimate how much loss they’re bringing to the table. An investor for any challenger company will be forced to ask those questions upfront, and there is no obvious answer. So today we think it is an effort to make it more difficult for others to scale up quickly.
Jignanshu Gor
On the user side, we only have third-party numbers, and on the revenue side, we only have third-party numbers, because the company has not really shared it. It reports all metrics related to Toing as platform innovations. However, at least for us, our channel checks indicate that there is serious pickup of Toing in every way. The daily, weekly, and monthly active users seem almost abnormally high for an app which is in 50 cities, versus a Zomato or a Swiggy core app that operates in 700-odd cities across India. Which, in some sense, also tells you how concentrated the food delivery market really is in the large cities. But we don’t know specific numbers beyond that. What we know is that it is trying to establish a different offering. What is different about the offering is fundamentally geofencing the number of restaurants that a user will see, to reduce the distance the rider has to cover and to enhance the cost that Swiggy has to incur. So that’s part number one. The other part of the puzzle is that because the distance is lower, you can then batch multiple orders in a single rider trip. The industry average for us is roughly, let’s say, 1.2 today. If you can scale that up, you reduce the single largest cost of delivering a product. And the third part is that there is specific monetisation of delivery fees. There is an explicit monetisation on the Toing app of a delivery fee, telling the user that yes, I’m giving you offline prices for your products, but you will need to give me a delivery fee, and hence that is a fair exchange of services for money. So that collectively seems to be the current setup for Toing: yes, we are different, but we are still not trying to burn down the house; we’re trying to do this rationally.
Jignanshu Gor
It will be a combination of both. I don’t think you can expand the market initially without cannibalising some part of it. There will be value seekers who will shift to your other app. I think it’s unfair to expect that the real world is as clean as a strategic 2×2 is. So yes, I think there is some cannibalisation, no question. Are they really expanding the market? I would say they’re expanding not the user base but day parts of the user, when you typically would not be sharing a meal, and hence a single dish may not cross the threshold which makes sense for you to pay the fees and so on. So maybe you’re going out, or you’re going to your canteen or somewhere, and hence maybe a little bit more lunch than what Swiggy as a platform does. So yes, there are elements of that happening.
Jignanshu Gor
I think that becomes very important if you’re trying to estimate what the unit economics of this thing can look like. While we want to do that, the question can be flipped around: what are you willing to pay for growth? Let’s take it this way. If your core food delivery business is delivering, in the case of Swiggy, let’s say, Rs 300 crore Ebitda with, let’s say, a 17–18% annual growth rate, would you be okay with a 22-23% growth rate but a 180 crore Ebitda? That’s the sort of call that Swiggy does not want investors to make today, because, as they say, it is pre-PMF. And this is the same challenge Swiggy has: typically, very early-stage products are not dissected in public markets. But now that you are seeing the traction and you’re vocal about it, people will take a stab at dissecting it, and we will come up short. But whether I worry about whether there is some cost in Swiggy’s business which is in Toing, or the other way around, of course we do. This was a key discussion at the Capital Markets Day for Swiggy as well.
Jignanshu Gor
What happened in China about 1.5 years ago is that Meituan was obviously the leader for food delivery in China. It had around 70–73% market share, with JD and Alibaba combined having 1/4. Suddenly, one morning, JD woke up and said I declare war. I have access to a lot of consumers, but on higher-margin, lower-frequency products like e-commerce. Food is my high-frequency item. I want more consumers on my platform; how do I do that? […]
So what JD did was they said, I want market share: massive consumer incentives and discounting. They gained, I think, from 0 to 15% market share in one quarter, and then Alibaba jumped into the fray, and they went from 27% to 45% market share. And Meituan’s market share went down from 73–74% to 45–46%, over the space of 2 quarters. Now this is massively detrimental to any food delivery player. Their margins went negative in those 2 quarters, and of course their share price followed. What it means is that if you have enough users on your platform, getting restaurants on your platform is not a problem. So the whole theory about network effects- that if you lock in supply or consumers, it’s very difficult to dislodge you- essentially works only if there is a lack of capital commitment from the other side. If the challenger commits enough capital, you can break the network effect.
Brady Ng
Meituan is the big food delivery platform in China. Everybody knows its name. If you live in a big city, chances are you order from it at least a couple of times a week. […] It was actually founded as a group buying site, a Groupon clone, essentially. That was in 2010. And I bring this up because it really tells you something about the company culture, the way it’s run. After it launched in 2010, there were thousands of copycats it literally had to fend off, dozens of competitors that were popping up every day. It took a few years, but it won. During those few years, it tried a few other things, like movie tickets, hotel bookings, your standard things that we think about buying through an app or online now. It didn’t merge with a food delivery platform until 2015, and that’s how it really got into the business. And now it’s the core of its business. It took the expertise that it got when it was still a group buying platform, when it had to work with extremely low margins and run this aggregate of millions of micro tweaks every day, and it’s applying that to food delivery. That’s why it’s managed to grow that segment really significantly, and that’s why it’s the dominant platform today.
Brady Ng
So there was a change. The big thing was last year, when JD and Alibaba made a huge push. They really wanted to eat into Meituan’s market share, and they did cause a dent. But Meituan did defend it by rolling out this huge subsidy campaign. It was worth, I think, 100 billion yuan. And also a zero-commission policy. So it was taking the race back to the bottom. It was dragging everybody back down. […] It has this habit of taking the race constantly back to the bottom, because that’s where it thinks it can burn out everybody else. This is a company that stockpiles cash for these moments. Whether that’s a good way of running a business is a separate conversation, but that is its strategy. And if you come in and fight it, you can’t be a startup that’s trying to outprice it, because it will burn you out in a matter of weeks. That’s why only Alibaba and JD were able to really keep up this race with Meituan, but even then it didn’t really dislodge it, because Meituan has other moats that it could lean on.
Brady Ng
I think so. At one point it was, I think, the 3rd or 4th largest listed internet company. I’m not sure if it still is. And shareholders do ask these questions, like, how come we’re burning billions of dollars? But I guess company management looks at the outcomes: if we can get through this in even a year and a half, 2 years, maybe it’s worth it, because they can try to rebuild that market share.
Jignanshu Gor
I would also say Flipkart, because Flipkart has been talking about launching a food delivery vertical for a while now. And what really makes it attractive for anybody else to enter is what is called Tobin’s Q, which is the ratio of market cap to replacement of assets, very simply put. If the cost of replacing my assets is X and I make 5 times, 10 times that in my market cap, I can create so much value as an investor. So that’s why an investor will always want to fund a new product. And the amount of margins that food delivery companies are making, which is by far the highest in the world, the consistent growth that they’re showing, and hence the valuations they’re getting, is what attracts an investor to look at this.
Jignanshu Gor
As I wrote in the note, in China we estimate the total cumulative loss of all 3 players over 5 quarters at $28 billion, and that loss is still continuing. Now we don’t need $28 billion. If you scale it down to India’s scale, you need less than 1/50th of that. So is there somebody who will allocate, I don’t know, maybe $150-200 million to this? And if you do, what happens to the profits that you’re enjoying at both Zomato and Swiggy?
Jignanshu Gor
Again, the question is: wait for what? If the market share loss has already started, it’s going to become increasingly more difficult for you to gain either the customers or the restaurants back. I think the real question to ask is: why not do this on Instamart, but do it on food delivery? They could have done this on Instamart as well, but the objective of doing this here is: why wait for the horse to bolt before you close down the stable? You make it more difficult for the newcomer to gain traction. Attack is the best form of defence. There’s a very interesting statement in Consumer that the reason why investors love growth and not margins, or they forgive margins if there is growth, is because a customer lost is incredibly difficult to gain back. But a customer who is with you, you can monetize him later. And I think that’s the intent: why should I let a customer, which in this case is either the consumer or the restaurant, give the opportunity for a Rapido or for a Flipkart or anyone else to come and say, okay, I’m halving your commission structure, or I’m making it zero, and get them a foothold? Why do that? And if you can afford to, why not defend? I think the more interesting question is also why the other person is not doing it, why it is only Swiggy doing it.
Jignanshu Gor
I think [Zomato is] phenomenal at execution, and they’ve proven it in food delivery, where interestingly, just pre-COVID, Swiggy was actually a bigger player in India overall. I think the execution during and after COVID was just phenomenal. The same is the case with quick commerce right now; there’s just no competing with them. Why they’re not jumping in today: I think there are 2 reasons. One is geography; the other is market cap. The geographic reason, in my mind, is that both the players who’ve been expressing interest in entering this are in Swiggy’s backyard. And hence the potential impact on them would be higher versus the potential impact on a Zomato business. The second part is that Swiggy’s market cap today is almost entirely made up of food delivery. […] I’m not saying food delivery does not matter to Eternal; it of course does, but it matters far, far more to Swiggy as a business.
Jignanshu Gor
I think it’s a phenomenal example of a company willing to change what it values in order to defend what it values more. I see another example, and it’s completely off track, so I’ll not go too much into detail, but I think Titan has done exactly the same thing. […] It was a company that famously used to cherish the margins that it was making, which were coming from its design and brand strength. Over the last 18 months or so, they have shifted their approach to say that I need to protect my consumers. So I need to protect the market share that I have with my consumers rather than margins today, and hence completely deprioritised, even from a medium- to longer-term guidance, what margin I make, as long as I ensure that the revenue that I make is incremental to my profit. They’ve not necessarily gone down the curve, but they have let go of profitability; that’s what I’m trying to say.
Jignanshu Gor
I think in my mind there are only 2 possible options which make sense, and it’s a barbell. Either Swiggy is successfully able to fend off imminent competition, and it is not able to find unit economics for Toing, and says that we really separate out Toing versus Swiggy so much that most of my current Swiggy users don’t like Toing at all. And that would be done by narrowing the value proposition of Toing: the kind of restaurants that you have, the kind of SKUs that you have on the platform, or maybe even just the customer experience, like 100% ensuring order batching, ensuring a lot more ads than you see on the core platform. The first part is not a bull case; it’s what they were doing earlier. But the real fascination would be if they find an answer. And if they find an answer which consumers are okay with, which restaurants are okay with, and that makes sense for them, any such answer will, in my view, have a lower margin profile than the core current business. But then that would really expand the TAM, because I genuinely believe that the only way to expand the TAM in this market is to reduce the average price of the product that you’re selling. There’s no other way to expand TAM in this market.
Jignanshu Gor
The challenge is the nature of the product, because you wouldn’t like to eat a pizza hours after it was baked. So I think there is a limitation, and hence I do think that while it might work, it might work in a few pockets; it might work with a lower margin profile. But if it expands the TAM, possibly I think the process and the experiment is worth it. Operating a platform on a higher versus a lower cost structure is fundamentally different. […] So at the least I think Swiggy will learn a lot by trying to operate and think through and manufacture a low-cost food delivery network which can be transposed to the core business.
