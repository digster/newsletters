In 1953, the Indian diplomat VK Krishna Menon gave the world a new word for refusing to pick a side: non-alignment.

Every schoolchild in India knows this story. We studied it in our history books (last I checked). When countries all over the world were being forced to choose between the US and the Soviet Union, India, along with a few other nations, decided that the best course was to actively stay in the middle. 

This position was articulated using five principles, called Panchasheel:

  1. Respect for sovereignty and territorial integrity
  2. Mutual non-aggression
  3. Non-interference in internal affairs
  4. Equality and mutual benefit
  5. Peaceful coexistence

I’m tempted to claim that I remembered these principles from my schooldays, but I didn’t. Non-alignment is something we studied, and immediately forgot, mostly because it had become practically irrelevant by the time we were growing up, so it felt like ancient history. But the reason I went and looked it up is that I’ve been thinking about non-alignment a lot over the last few days.

For nearly half a decade, Indian IT was waiting for the opening and now, thanks to a set of specific circumstances, it’s finally here. I’m convinced that the great AI supply-chain crisis of 2026 has created a positioning for Indian IT services: the vendor-neutral integration and governance layer of global AI. 

And it’s modelled on India’s oldest foreign-policy instinct, non-alignment. 

It’s still a hard road, and plenty can go wrong. 

But for the first time, there’s a road…
