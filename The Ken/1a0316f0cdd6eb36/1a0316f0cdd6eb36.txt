Pharma is the rare industry where the villain and the saviour are the same company, depending on the patient or the policy you’re looking at.

Earlier this month, the Delhi High Court directed Sun Pharma to seek its permission before launching semaglutide tablets, in response to a patent infringement suit by innovator Novo Nordisk. Sun already sells injectable semaglutide, but for the oral form, Novo claimed Sun “could be” violating a formulation patent protecting its tablet Rybelsus.

The historic weight-loss drug is a classic case where the decades-long cry of inventive pharma for regulatory data protection in India manifests in many forms. While India grants patent protection, which safeguards the invention, it doesn’t grant data protection. This means all the safety and efficacy data generated during years of clinical studies is available for referencing and copying, often well before the patent expires.

So even though the semaglutide patent expired on 31 March, its data was available much earlier for Indian companies to reference. As a result, some 50 brands lined up to sell the drug from 1 April.

“Roughly 25% of the cost of developing a new drug goes into invention and 75% in establishing safety and efficacy studies,” said Anil Matai, director general of the Organisation of Pharmaceutical Producers of India (Oppi), which represents global pharma. After years of silence, Oppi is now asking for 6–12 years of data protection in India.

Under the current generics practice, Indian companies use that data to conduct small bioavailability/ bioequivalence studies (usually on 50–100 people) and launch the drug, sometimes even before the patent expires. Incidentally, on 17 August, Zydus lost https://www.business-standard.com/industry/news/delhi-hc-restrains-zydus-from-launching-dabrafenib-till-patent-expiry-126081801249_1.html such a case to Novartis for a cancer drug whose patent does not expire until 2028.

“We launched our myopia drug [in late 2025], which is the first in the world. We didn’t get clinical exclusivity, and within a few months, a big Indian pharma [name withheld on request] came as a second applicant, got approval and launched the drug,” said Nikkhil K Masurkar, chief executive of Entod Pharmaceuticals in Mumbai.
