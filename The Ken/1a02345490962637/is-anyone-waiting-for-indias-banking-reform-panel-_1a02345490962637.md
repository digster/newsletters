The Collection
Fri, 21 Aug 26
Multiple stories, multiple perspectives, one theme worth your time—every week.
Good Morning Ishan,
“India formed a committee to reform banking. The RBI didn’t wait” should have been the headline from this week’s PSB Confluence, hosted by the Department of Financial Services.
Finance Minister Nirmala Sitharaman told bankers that a “high-powered committee for banking reforms”—first promised in this year’s Budget, as part of the government’s Viksit Bharat push to get India to developed-nation status by 2047—will be announced “soon”. The two days of bankers’ recommendations gathered at the conclave will be material for the committee to chew on once it exists.
The panel has seven official themes laid out for whenever it does: deposit mobilisation, banking for youth, supporting the investment cycle and global capability centres (GCCs), agriculture and horticulture value-chain infrastructure, priority-sector lending, reimagining the credit-card business, and one more still to be spelt out.
But committees deliberate before they act.
Over the past few months, the regulator has made decisions across several of those same areas. Some address problems the committee is supposed to think about. Some make the eventual reform harder. And in at least one case, the RBI appears to be moving in the opposite direction. That makes the committee’s eventual job rather interesting. It will not be starting with a blank sheet of paper. It will have to reckon with rules the regulator has already written.
Sorted under the government’s own headings, here’s what we found.
[Credit cards get expensive fast once users fall into the habit of paying only the minimum amount due each month](https://the-ken.com/kaching/credit-cards-are-great-only-until-you-miss-a-payment?utm_source=email&utm_medium=newsletter&utm_campaign=collection), a trap that’s been sitting in plain sight for years, waiting for the kind of reimagining the committee has now promised. Whether that reimagining reaches products that behave like credit cards without technically being one is a separate question, and the RBI has already answered it, badly.[ ](https://the-ken.com/story/the-rbis-revolving-credit-rule-treats-every-nbfc-like-a-bad-lender/)
[A draft proposal open for comments until 28 August would bar non-banking financial companies from offering revolving credit at all](https://the-ken.com/story/the-rbis-revolving-credit-rule-treats-every-nbfc-like-a-bad-lender/?utm_source=email&utm_medium=newsletter&utm_campaign=collection), the same borrow-repay-borrow-again mechanic a credit card runs on, which would wipe out products like Bajaj Finance’s flexi-loans and Tata Capital’s business lines of credit overnight. Stopping an endless debt cycle is a reasonable goal, but treating every NBFC as an equally bad risk to get there is a blunt instrument standing in for actual regulation.
The whole idea is credit reaching the people who need it most, on terms that don’t wreck them. [RBI-licensed NBFCs currently set their own interest rate ceilings, and some have used that freedom to charge annualised rates north of 600%](https://the-ken.com/story/at-rbi-licensed-nbfcs-600-annual-interest-is-board-approved-reasonable-and-perfectly-legal/?utm_source=email&utm_medium=newsletter&utm_campaign=collection). Mutasim’s story last month found a sample of these lenders posting a median 4.5X jump in revenue within a single year, with one case hitting nearly 4,800X. A second draft proposal, open for comments until 11 September, might finally do something about it.

This theme can be read next to a story where the RBI’s own position visibly shifted mid-draft. [When we first wrote in May about lenders remotely locking a borrower’s phone after a missed EMI](https://the-ken.com/kaching/miss-a-smartphone-emi-and-your-lender-can-now-wirelessly-strip-the-smart-out-of-it/?utm_source=email&utm_medium=newsletter&utm_campaign=collection), the draft rule required a lender to wait until an account was 90 days overdue, and clear two separate notice periods, before a full device lock could kick in. The final version, out in August, tightened that specific number to 60 days. But it also added a new, earlier layer of partial restrictions starting at 30 days overdue, alongside fresh borrower protections (essential functions like incoming calls and emergency SOS have to stay on, restrictions only apply if the loan specifically financed the device, and lenders now owe compensation for wrongful restriction) and pushed the whole framework’s effective date back three months, from October 2026 to January 2027.
That’s a regulator actively negotiating with itself over how hard to come down on exactly the young, first-time, device-financing borrowers this theme is supposed to protect.
This is closer to the government’s own poverty goals than to a single RBI circular, and the picture on the ground is just as uneven. [A stray data point this year showed that 40% of re-KYC requests for Jan Dhan accounts in Jammu and Kashmir are still pending](https://the-ken.com/newsletter/make-india-competitive-again/indias-financial-inclusion-project-is-titanic-kyc-could-be-the-iceberg-that-sinks-it/?utm_source=email&utm_medium=newsletter&utm_campaign=collection), and beyond a CAG report flagging discrepancies in direct benefit transfers last year, the ministry hasn’t published fresh progress numbers since 2025. A financial-inclusion drive that can’t account for its own KYC backlog is a failure with a direct line to the number the government actually wants to hit by 2047.
India likes to present itself as relaxed about citizens investing abroad, but it hasn’t actually let go of the wheel. [This year, the RBI sent at least 10 letters to family offices questioning their overseas direct investments](https://the-ken.com/kaching/india-made-it-easier-to-invest-abroad-so-why-are-family-offices-getting-questions-now/?utm_source=email&utm_medium=newsletter&utm_campaign=collection), and vague rules combined with the fear of misreading the asterisks are enough on their own to keep capital from moving in either direction. That’s an odd outcome for a committee meant to make Indian banking more investment-friendly, not less.
There’s a more basic question underneath all of this that the government’s seven themes don’t name: how does anyone’s credit score get calculated in the first place? [Nobody outside CIBIL really knows](https://the-ken.com/story/youre-clueless-about-your-credit-score-for-banks-that-works-just-fine/?utm_source=email&utm_medium=newsletter&utm_campaign=collection). The methodology is guarded like a trade secret, kept opaque even from many of the banks that rely on it to decide who gets a loan. Reimagining credit access for young borrowers, or pricing small loans fairly, is a much harder promise to keep when the scoring system feeding both decisions was designed to stay a black box.
The committee hasn’t had its first meeting yet, and the RBI has already given it a working agenda across most of its own headings, whether it asked for one or not.
