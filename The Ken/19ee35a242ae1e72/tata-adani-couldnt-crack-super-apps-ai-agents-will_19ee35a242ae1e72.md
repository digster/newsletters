Good Morning Ishan,
Here’s a bit of ancient history: China was arguably the first major market where phone payments caught on.
Everyone uses Wechat Pay and Alibaba’s Alipay. As someone who has neither app installed on my phone, I have been turned away from coffee shops, restaurants, stores, and by street vendors. Cash still works most of the time, but anyone who hands it over and waits for change might be met with a raised eyebrow. The fact that you still handle bills and coins marks you as an outlander.
Wechat Pay is embedded in Wechat, which is essential for anyone to navigate all facets of life in China. It’s the defining example of a “super app”, a single application that bundles a wide range of unrelated services—messaging, payments, shopping, transport, healthcare, government services, ride-hailing, news reading, knowledge discovery, and more. It’s all in one place so you don’t need to switch between separate apps.
Alipay is similarly feature-dense and has the same descriptor. Aside from day-to-day transactions, people use it to manage money market funds, get loans, buy insurance, clip coupons, perform currency exchange, buy groceries, book plane and train tickets, reserve seats at cinemas, make doctor appointments… you get the idea.
All of this is functional. All of it works as intended. But it also creates a homepage grid of icons and category tabs, basically an additional home screen that lives within your preferred super app. Now, something new is in the works that make that design make much more sense…

Hi! This is Vidhatri, the producer of Zero Shot.
In November last year, when I was hosting 90,000 Hours, The Ken’s careers and workplaces podcast, we surveyed listeners on how they were using AI and asked them the million-dollar question: was AI making them productive?
We got over 200 responses. People talked about their experiments with tools and described how they were augmenting their work. Some complained about the pressure of being constantly productive and the push their bosses were making for increased AI use.
Tokens did not feature in the conversation. Not once.
Cut to now. It’s impossible to imagine any conversation about productivity at work without mentioning tokens. November last year feels like a world away in the AI conversation, and it’s fascinating to watch people take the tools and run with them.
And the speed is wild, to say the least. Early this year, we started hearing about tokenmaxxing. Engineers were using up tokens because enterprises measured them through leaderboards.
Now, halfway through the year, we’re seeing a flip. The bills are finally here. Uber burned through its AI budget for the year. Microsoft started pulling Claude Code licenses. Meta built an internal tracker to figure out where all the money was going. The question is no longer “Are your people using AI?” It is: “What are we actually getting for it?”
Praveen Gopal Krishnan, the host of Zero Shot, put a name to this shift: yieldmaxxing. The thesis is simple: companies will now try to figure out ways to measure value and not just the volume of tokens used.
On this week’s episode, we brought in Gaurav Mishra, the CEO of Proshort, who’s previously worked with Uber, Meta, and Yahoo, to help us think through what the measuring of “yield” might look like.
