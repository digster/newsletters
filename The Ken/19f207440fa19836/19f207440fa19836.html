Public markets are a two-headed sphinx. Every door you walk into splits into two more doors. Investing or trading. Direct stocks or mutual funds. And within mutual funds, actively managed funds or passive index funds.

Every rupee that you want to grow means choosing between these camps.

Actively managed funds rely on professional managers picking and choosing securities to beat benchmark indices like the S&P 500 or Nifty 50. Passive funds skip the guesswork and simply replicate a benchmark index’s composition, betting on the market instead of trying to better it. That simplicity, built-in diversification, and low management costs have made passive funds increasingly popular.

With the upcoming public issues of NSE and Jio Platforms expected to be among India’s biggest so far—each estimated to raise between 30,000–38,000 crore ($3–4 billion)—passive index funds are once again under the spotlight. Because whenever these companies eventually find their way into benchmark indices, millions of passive fund investors will automatically own a piece of them.

Passive funds have their critics, though.

The criticism isn’t about passive investing itself. It is about the methodology behind how a stock earns its way into an index in the first place. 

A stock must satisfy strict, rules-based criteria set for each index, and maintain high trading frequency and high liquidity. It will then be assigned a weightage based on its market capitalisation.

Indices in India, including the bellwether NSE Nifty 50 and BSE Sensex, base their methodology on free-float market capitalisation, or the number of shares available for trading (total number of shares minus promoter holdings), multiplied by the stock price. The companies with the biggest free-float market capitalisation enter the Nifty 50 and BSE Sensex, and the list is rejigged every six months. 

Every time a large listing comes around, some critics—including some active fund managers—begin to question whether India’s benchmark indices truly reflect the country’s best companies or simply its biggest and most liquid ones. Because the moment a stock enters an index, passive funds tracking the index end up owning it too. While active fund managers might have a vested interest in this debate, their questions are not unimportant.

The scepticism and criticism are despite changes in the regulatory rule-book. In March 2024, market regulator Sebi introduced rules aimed at improving transparency around how index providers include or exclude stocks and assign weightages. However, Indian investors are still exposed to shortcomings associated with passive index investing, like not really deserving stocks continuing to gain entry into the indices. Jio Financial Services finding a place in the Nifty 50 not too long ago was a stark example.

No bias, but really?

India is not alone.

When Elon Musk’s SpaceX, which listed at a valuation of $1.8 trillion on June 12, was preparing to go public, it was in talks to be included in the key indices in the US. While Nasdaq and MSCI tweaked their rules to fast-track the stock’s inclusion, the S&P 500 did not immediately oblige. 

But Nasdaq’s move meant that by early July, a regular index fund investor in the US would automatically own a piece of SpaceX, a company that went public at a reportedly eye-watering price-to-revenue ratio of 141X. It sparked criticism from commentators and market experts who argued that index providers were effectively forcing passive investors to buy into one of the world’s most expensive stocks.

In India, there are hundreds of passive fund schemes that come under the Nifty and BSE umbrella, including both index funds and exchange-traded funds. Equity funds account for the lion’s share, at about 70% of these funds, according to NSE data. Of these, the Nifty 50 is the most tracked index, and represents about 40% of India’s total market capitalisation, consisting of the country’s largest listed companies.

But the problem is that some of that passive money also flows into stocks that may not really deserve to be part of the indices. 

To blame is the methodology used by the index operators to include or exclude stocks. What’s mathematically accurate need not always be logically right from the perspective of an investor. 

The methodology, as it stands, prioritises size and liquidity, not necessarily the quality or valuation of the underlying business.

Take Eternal, the parent of food delivery company Zomato, for example. The food-tech company is part of the Nifty 50 and the BSE Sensex. It currently trades at a PE ratio of over 700X, while the Nifty 50 and Sensex, on average, have a PE ratio of about 21X. 

While Zomato qualifies to be a part of the Nifty 50 thanks to the free-float market capitalisation principle, it also means that investors would be buying into a very expensive stock (in terms of P/E ratio) even if they don’t want to, simply because it is part of the index.

When, in February 2025, higher free-float market capitalisation helped Eternal and Jio Financial Services gain a place in the Nifty 50 index, replacing Britannia Industries and Bharat Petroleum, it also bumped up the valuation of the index itself, making it more expensive.

Then there is Jio Financial Services.

The non-banking arm of Mukesh Ambani’s empire became part of the Nifty 50 index not because the business was inherently strong, critics argue, but because of the treasury book it inherited. This, despite its revenue being a fraction of the median revenue of Nifty 50 companies. 

The Ken had written about this. 

The benevolent parent (Reliance) had given its newest child (Jio Financial Services) a cool $12 billion (about Rs 1 lakh crore) gift in treasury shares—essentially, its own shares that Reliance earlier held in itself—to kickstart Jio Financial Services’ business.

As it stands, these shares currently have a value of over Rs 1 lakh crore compared with Jio Financial Services’ total market cap of about Rs 1.45 lakh crore. It follows that the treasury shares would have significantly bumped up the stock’s free-float market cap, too.

The curious entry of Jio Financial Services into the Nifty 50, The Ken

None of this is new.

In the past, stocks such as Yes Bank and renewable energy company Suzlon continued to remain part of benchmark indices even when their underlying businesses were going through major upheaval, said Sachin Jain of Trigen Wealth. Better-quality companies could have been part of the indices, he argued, but under the current formula, a stock is pushed out only after it falls below certain market-cap thresholds.

Meanwhile, others enter merely because their market-cap is high.

Let’s take Eternal again. Some argue that tech stocks like these have a high PE ratio and lower profits compared with traditional companies such as Britannia, which they believe is a better stock to have in the portfolio. For the last twelve months, Eternal’s profit has stood at Rs 366 crore, compared with a median profit of Rs 11,900 crore for Nifty 50 companies, according to Screener.

But defenders of passive funds counter that a company should be assessed and evaluated on a discounted cash flow basis—that is, based on the cash flows they are expected to generate over the next five to 10 years as they mature. If profitability alone were the deciding factor, they argue, the US’s FAANG companies—an acronym for five of the most prominent US-based tech companies: Facebook (now Meta), Amazon, Apple, Netflix, and Google (now Alphabet)—would never have made it into benchmark indices in the US, nor delivered the returns they eventually did.

“In over-simplified terms, cash flow is like a distant cousin of profits. For a strong but new-ish company (imagine Uber in its early years), eventually both these values are expected to change from being negative numbers to being significantly positive numbers,” says Avinash Luthria, a Sebi-registered investment adviser. 

Besides, for all the criticism, passive funds, specifically large-cap funds, have mostly outperformed active funds, data from the S&P Spiva survey indicates.. 

In a nutshell, defenders of passive funds argue that while these funds may not be perfect, they are still better than many active funds.

Most of the arguments against passive funds, their defenders say, have been propagated by active fund managers whose livelihood depends on promoting their products as better. 

Be that as it may, it does not take away the flaws with passive investing.

The methodology can still keep certain “deserving companies” out of the index while allowing the not-so-deserving ones—companies with lower profits, even losses, and exorbitant valuations—to enter.

This is why some believe index providers should also have qualitative analysis along with quantitative metrics.

Passive funds now account for 18% of mutual fund assets under management and continue to grow. As of December 2025, their AUM stood at Rs 14.2 lakh crore, up more than 30% year-on-year. Their significant size means that the stakes are that much higher for retail investors.

Like passive fund investors in the US who now own SpaceX through passive funds, Indian passive fund investors too own shares of companies they don’t want to be exposed to. Sure, these indices are rebalanced every six months, but such rejigging does not necessarily remove less-deserving stocks from the index unless the methodology itself changes. 

The sphinx would continue asking riddles from the common investor unless Sebi takes a firm stance.
