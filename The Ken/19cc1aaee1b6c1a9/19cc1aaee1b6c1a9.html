I’m not particularly diligent about tracking the stock market.

Like many people who claim to be long-term investors, I mostly check my portfolio when something dramatic happens. But there is one other moment when I reliably open the app. When a war breaks out.

Every time the news fills with missiles and drones somewhere in the world, I find myself glancing at defence stocks—HAL, BEL, Paras Defence, Data Patterns. I don’t actually buy or sell them. It’s more of a strange reflex when human tragedy is unfolding on TV.

The irony is not new. More than a decade ago, an essay in The Washington Post argued that wars, over the long term, have often made societies richer and safer by forcing states to organise and innovate. Whether one agrees with that thesis morally, markets often behave as if some version of it might be true.

The Middle East is once again reminding investors of this uneasy relationship between conflict and capital. As tensions between Iran, Israel, and the US flare up, global defence spending is already climbing. Europe is rearming, the US is replenishing stockpiles depleted by Ukraine and Gaza, and Asian countries are expanding military budgets.

India, meanwhile, is having its moment. Military spending has nearly tripled over the past decade to around Rs 7 lakh crore, while defence production has crossed Rs 1.5 lakh crore. 

Surely, then, defence stocks are one of the hottest trades on the bourses, with some commanding eye-watering multiples for a while now. In fact, at one point nearly one in 10 stocks in the BSE 500 traded at over 100 times earnings, with defence names sprinkled liberally across that list.

Investors believe preparing for war can be very good business. The economics, though, are rarely that simple. Which makes the sector unpredictable and full of interesting corporate stories.

To defence or not to defence

Start with the strange case of Unimech Aerospace.

The Bengaluru-based precision manufacturing company filed for a Rs 500-crore IPO last year. Just in time for the defence boom. Except the business itself is not particularly Indian, and not particularly defence.

Nearly 98% of its revenue comes from exporting aerospace components, largely to clients in the US and Germany. Yet the company benchmarks itself against listed defence darlings like Paras Defence, Data Patterns, and Mtar Technologies—stocks that have been swept up in India’s defence rally. The implication is that in a market where “defence” is a hot label, companies that can plausibly attach themselves to the theme often do.

The funding paradox

Then there is Tonbo Imaging, a Bengaluru-based defence-tech firm building electro-optical systems used by militaries across the world.

For years, Tonbo ran into a problem that sounds mundane but is common in the sector: capital. At one point, the company had orders worth Rs 200 crore but needed just $4 million of working capital to fulfil them.

Defence technology operates on timelines VCs dislike. Development cycles are long, procurement depends on governments, and revenues arrive unevenly.

The incumbents’ execution problem

If startups struggle with funding, incumbents struggle with speed.

Take Hindustan Aeronautics Ltd. The state-owned aerospace giant has one of the largest order books in India’s defence sector. Yet it has long faced criticism, sometimes publicly from the Indian Air Force, for delays in delivering aircraft such as the Tejas fighter jet.

That tension reflects a broader shift underway. For decades, India’s defence manufacturing ecosystem was dominated by public-sector units. Now the government is gradually opening the sector to private companies and foreign partnerships.

All of this leaves investors trying to price a business that rarely grows in straight lines. Defence companies move slowly, and headlines move quickly. Wars, budgets, procurement deals, export orders, technology transfers, and policy shifts.

Somewhere between those two speeds lies the investment thesis. And that, perhaps, explains the strange habit that started this piece.
