Two By Two
Fri, 03 Apr 26
An abridged, narrative version of the latest episode of Two by Two, The Ken’s premium weekly business podcast.
Good Morning Ishan,
It’s that time of the year again. When evenings become something to look forward to. Somewhere, a pub fills up even before the excitement begins. Elsewhere, a phone is propped up on a desk, a laptop is open on the side, and Jiohotstar is running in the background. No work is getting done. A beloved team jersey gets dug out from the back of the cupboard, traffic thins, and group chats come alive. For the next two months, this is just life in India.
This is what IPL is. It is a festival.
So when you see headlines about the Royal Challengers Bangalore and the Rajasthan Royals getting bought for over $1.7 billion and $1.6 billion, respectively, it feels like confirmation. IPL is only going up, this party is never-ending, and everything is great in the world.
Except, it is not that simple. And only looking through a business lens can show you why.
This newsletter is going to be a little longer than usual because I don’t want you to miss anything about this episode, whether you listen to it or not. But of course, you should listen—it is a banger.
For this episode, we had a self-declared cricket noob, a former cricket podcast host, and someone with thirty years of watching matches from the front row.
Rahel Philipose played the I can’t tell a no-ball from a wide card right at the start and took on the most important role in the room: asking every question the rest of us would have wanted to ask if we had the chance. PGK, who used to host and produce a cricket podcast he describes as “a different era of my life” and refuses to elaborate on, took the role of the business expert. And for the inside scoop and the cultural expertise, we had none other than Sharda Ugra.
Sharda is one of India’s best sports journalists—there is genuinely no other way to say it. She has covered cricket since the 90s, was in the room when IPL was first introduced, and wrote one of the earliest and sharpest pieces about what it all meant. Her [2008 article](https://www.indiatoday.in/magazine/sport/story/20080211-changing-the-rules-735310-2008-01-31) in India Today described the first edition as “the ka-ching of cash registers drowning out all other sound or sense”. She was not wrong then, and she is not wrong now.
I have to take a moment to say that all three of us, myself included from behind the glass, completely fangirled over Sharda. She walked in with a little notebook full of notes, said things like “golden goose ka baap” and “if Lalit Modi could monetise air, he would” and delivered every line with absolute confidence and charm.
The studio was buzzing. PGK was getting visibly excited, Sharda was clearly enjoying herself, and Rahel was doing what she does best—making the smartest people in the room explain themselves.
| Sharda’s notebook that contained all the cricket scoops |
When the IPL launched in 2008, it was more about the cool factor than money, or so it seemed. Shah Rukh Khan bought KKR, Vijay Mallya bought Bengaluru, and Preity Zinta was the face of Kings XI Punjab. Rahel described what it felt like perfectly: “rich people with antique cars.” You wanted to be the person who owned the shiny thing, not necessarily the person who made money from it.
Sharda explains what it was really like from the inside. Lalit Modi, the man who built IPL essentially from scratch, was running it like a one-man show. “Every Monday, a new rule would come,” she says. And yet, it worked, because as she wrote in 2008 and still believes: it was never going to be allowed to fail. The institutional weight behind it was too enormous.
Fast forward to today. The same franchises are being bought by the Aditya Birla Group, Times Group, Bolt Ventures and Blackstone. That shift, from Bollywood celebrities to boardrooms, tells you everything. Because private equity does not buy things for cool.
PGK brought up a very interesting fact that around 80% of what a franchise earns every year comes from one source: the BCCI distributing its media rights revenue.
And those media rights used to be contested in fierce bidding wars between multiple media firms. Now, with the merger of Reliance and Star, there is only one serious bidder at the table: Jiostar.
The numbers above explain why everyone in the room was nervous. The value per match has jumped from $1.4 million (2009–2017) to $13.2 million (2023–2027), but the next cycle (2028–2032) is capped at $5.4 billion, and broadcasters are expected to lose around $1.75 billion. This is largely because ad and subscription revenues haven’t kept pace with the soaring cost of the rights.
“Now there’s no reason because there are not multiple people trying to bid,” PGK said.
Sharda put the implications more bluntly: the BCCI and Jiostar need to sort it out between themselves, and every franchise is just sitting there hoping they do.
“It is like being in a family,” PGK said. “You just want your parents to sort it out.”
But in this family, every other franchise is an adopted child. Except for the Mumbai Indians, which is the only biological one, because Jio owns both the broadcaster that controls the money and the team that plays in the league. That is some conflict of interest dressed up as a family dinner.
On the other hand, the crackdown on real money gaming has made it worse. Dream11, Mobile Premier League (MPL), and other fantasy cricket platforms were the most natural advertisers and sponsors for IPL. The target audience came easily: they were already watching the matches, and they either had money or were easily tempted into making more. When the government regulated that model, IPL lost somewhere between 15–20% of its advertising ecosystem overnight. Sharda puts it simply: “It was mad, mad, mad levels. They were everywhere.“
The replacement advertisers? AI companies, fintech, EVs. Fine. But none of them has that natural fit of someone who is already watching the match and simultaneously interested in building a fantasy team to play along.
Sharda watches IPL, she admits, “anthropologically”. She goes in looking for three columns and tries to stay objective about a format she does not particularly love as a fan, and yet:
Her point is not that everything is fine but that the cultural grip is too strong, the money is too entrenched, the BCCI is too powerful, and the players coming through now, like fourteen-year-old Vaibhav Sooryavanshi, who was literally born after IPL was created, have never known a world where this was not the centre of Indian cricket.
So the format has won, and the only question is whether the business model around it can keep up.
The episode closes with a quote she brought in her notebook from a conversation between two legendary cricketers about the IPL. We are not going to reproduce it here, you just have to listen to the episode to get the full feel.
What Sharda, PGK, and Rahel together managed to do in this episode is rare. They took something that feels like pure joy—like my living room during the 2019 Mumbai Indians vs Chennai Super Kings final, half yellow-half blue, everyone screaming—and showed you exactly what is holding it together underneath. It is extraordinary. But also more fragile than the scoreboard suggests.
