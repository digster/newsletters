There are three ways a listed company gets into trouble. First, when investors stop believing its narrative. Second, when lenders stop believing its numbers. Third, when the regulator finally starts asking questions.

Rajesh Exports has finally reached stage three.

[Before we get to it, a quick detour to another company trying to answer a different question from investors.

Yesterday, we published a story on Firstcry, whose stock has fallen nearly 70% since listing despite operating in a growing babycare market. One part of it is surely the impact of quick commerce. But it is also about how, while rivals moved into adjacent categories and platforms like Blinkit and Nykaa to stay relevant to customers, Firstcry increasingly doubled down on stores and private labels. The result is a company trying to extract more value from existing customers at a time when those customers have more alternatives than ever. Give it a read if you missed it.]

Back to Rajesh Exports.

Earlier this month, Sebi (Securities and Exchange Board of India) accused the Bengaluru-based gold refiner of misrepresenting transactions worth Rs 15 lakh crore over five years and barred founder Rajesh Mehta from the securities market pending further probe. The regulator alleges that 97–99% of the group’s reported revenues over five years were attributable to overseas subsidiaries, mainly the Swiss refiner Valcambi, even though Valcambi’s standalone financial statements appeared to show only a tiny fraction of that revenue. 

Sebi’s forensic review also questioned purported investments in African gold mines and identified over Rs 11,000 crore of allegedly fictitious trades with a local broker. Rajesh Exports has denied the allegations and says the regulator misunderstood financial statements.

The numbers are undeniably extraordinary, though. Roughly equal to the market capitalisation of Reliance Industries. Or India’s annual GST collections. Even after Sebi’s order, a state insurer like LIC still owns around 11% of Rajesh Exports. Its Swiss subsidiary is one of the world’s largest precious-metals refiners. This, in no way, was a forgotten microcap hiding in the market’s attic.

Sure, the immediate question is whether Sebi’s allegations are correct. The more interesting question is why so many investors seem to feel they have heard this story before. But not so much about the alleged misconduct.

For years, Rajesh Exports had a peculiar place in Indian markets. It generated eye-watering revenues and razor-thin margins. It attracted sceptics, forensic-accounting enthusiasts, and value investors in almost equal measure. Questions surfaced repeatedly about receivables, subsidiary structures, disclosures, and economics. Yet the company remained institutionally owned and largely untouched by regulators.

Then, after a complaint in 2024 and a forensic investigation, came Sebi.

That pattern, where markets spend years debating something before regulators formally arrive, has become one of the defining features of Indian finance. And it sits at the centre of some of The Ken’s best reporting on Sebi.

Markets speculate. Regulators litigate

For all the criticism Sebi receives, there is one fact: proving market abuse is considerably harder than spotting it.

Take insider trading. Everybody thinks they know what it looks like. A stock moves sharply before an announcement and somebody connected to the company appears to have traded at exactly the right time. The market can draw its conclusions in hours but Sebi can take years. That was the central tension in Easy to detect, hard to prove: Sebi’s uphill battle against insider trading https://the-ken.com/story/easy-to-detect-hard-to-prove-sebis-uphill-battle-against-insider-trading/?utm_source=email&utm_medium=newsletter&utm_campaign=collection. Surveillance systems can flag suspicious trades almost instantly. Courts, unfortunately for regulators, need more than suspicion.

The same problem appears in different forms across modern markets. In We (and Sebi) need to talk about mirror trading https://the-ken.com/kaching/we-and-sebi-need-to-talk-about-mirror-trading/?utm_source=email&utm_medium=newsletter&utm_campaign=collection, technology had enabled investors to effectively replicate another trader’s positions in real time. The behaviour was visible but the regulatory response was less obvious. By the time Sebi decides what a new practice is, things move on to the next one.

And markets have become remarkably good at staying one step ahead.

The battle between Sebi and Jane Street illustrates that better than most. Jane Street scores a point in its high-stakes duel with Sebi https://the-ken.com/long_and_short/jane-street-scores-a-point-in-its-high-stakes-duel-with-sebi/?utm_source=email&utm_medium=newsletter&utm_campaign=collection was ostensibly a story about a single trading firm. It was really about how increasingly sophisticated participants meet increasingly ambitious regulation. The old image of a regulator catching a rogue trader feels quaint now. Modern disputes are fought with armies of lawyers, quants, consultants, data scientists, and economists. Enforcement is now about proving misconduct in a way that survives years of litigation.

That challenge arrived just as the regulator started inserting itself into corners of the market that previous regimes might have approached more cautiously. As we explored in Sebi’s Madhabi Puri Buch and the art of keeping market players on tenterhooks https://the-ken.com/story/sebis-madhabi-puri-buch-and-the-art-of-keeping-market-players-on-tenterhooks/?utm_source=email&utm_medium=newsletter&utm_campaign=collection, the overseer’s ambitions expanded well beyond traditional enforcement. Consultation papers multiplied, and new rules appeared more frequently. 

https://the-ken.com/story/sebis-madhabi-puri-buch-and-the-art-of-keeping-market-players-on-tenterhooks/?utm_source=email&utm_medium=newsletter&utm_campaign=collection?utm_source=ro_hdr&utm_medium=email&utm_campaign=

Entire industries found themselves adjusting to a regulator that seemed less interested in reacting to markets. But market participants were quick to complain about overreach.

All of which is a reasonable defence of why regulators move slowly. It is also why investors become impatient. That is where the idea of investor protection becomes slippery. 

The debate surfaced in Two questions for Sebi on investor protection and market abuse https://the-ken.com/kaching/two-questions-for-sebi-on-investor-protection-and-market-abuse/?utm_source=email&utm_medium=newsletter&utm_campaign=collection, but it has only become more relevant since then. The pressure extends far beyond listed companies. In Sebi loopholes, bull run gave financial influencers wings https://the-ken.com/story/sebi-loopholes-bull-run-gave-financial-influencers-wings/?utm_source=email&utm_medium=newsletter&utm_campaign=collection, we looked at the watchdog’s attempt to regulate a growing ecosystem of personalities who were dispensing investment advice to millions of retail investors.

Investors want Sebi to stop fraud, manipulation and market abuse. Fair enough. Increasingly, however, they also seem to want Sebi to stop bubbles, prevent bad decisions, and rescue people from paying foolish prices.

This expectation to referee games that it does not fully control becomes even clearer in places that sit just outside Sebi’s direct reach.

In How Sebi and 328 shareholders minted India’s priciest stock in a single day https://the-ken.com/long_and_short/how-sebi-and-328-shareholders-minted-indias-priciest-stock-in-a-single-day/?utm_source=email&utm_medium=newsletter&utm_campaign=collection, we examined how promoters used a little-known threshold to keep companies technically unlisted while still creating active secondary markets for their shares. It well captured a recurring theme in Indian finance. That capital tends to find a way around regulation faster than regulation finds a way around capital.

The recent frenzy in unlisted shares is a good example. 

https://the-ken.com/story/sebi-watches-as-investors-in-unlisted-shares-gasp-for-breath/?utm_source=email&utm_medium=newsletter&utm_campaign=collection?utm_source=ro_hdr&utm_medium=email&utm_campaign=

In Is NSE next in line after HDB and NSDL to burn unlisted-market investors—under Sebi’s watch? https://the-ken.com/story/sebi-watches-as-investors-in-unlisted-shares-gasp-for-breath/?utm_source=email&utm_medium=newsletter&utm_campaign=collection, we examined how investors chased marquee names long before their IPOs, frequently at valuations that made little sense. Much of this activity occurred outside Sebi’s direct line of sight. Yet if those bets sour, the regulator will almost certainly be asked again why it failed to protect investors.

A similar contradiction runs through the advice industry. 

India has hundreds of millions of investors but remarkably few registered advisors. 

https://the-ken.com/story/sebi-registered-advisors-are-an-endangered-species-so-who-guides-retail-investors/?utm_source=email&utm_medium=newsletter&utm_campaign=collection?utm_source=ro_hdr&utm_medium=email&utm_campaign=

That imbalance was the protagonist in Sebi-registered advisors are an endangered species. So who guides retail investors? https://the-ken.com/story/sebi-registered-advisors-are-an-endangered-species-so-who-guides-retail-investors/?utm_source=email&utm_medium=newsletter&utm_campaign=collection As compliance costs rose and economics deteriorated, legitimate advice became scarcer. Investors filled the gap elsewhere. Youtube channels, Telegram groups, finfluencers, and anonymous social-media accounts stepped in. Unsurprisingly, many investors now expect Sebi to police that ecosystem too.

Regulators gather evidence, markets form opinions, and cases like Rajesh Exports sit at the intersection. And the distance between those two timelines is where much of the frustration around Sebi now resides.

Is the regulator moving too slowly, or is the complexity of modern markets a genuine excuse?
