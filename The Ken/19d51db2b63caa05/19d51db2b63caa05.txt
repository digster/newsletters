What Willie Walsh inherits as the Indigo CEO. The ex-British Airways chief has to fix the airline without breaking the model that built it, even as costs, competition, and its own design start to push back

Willie Walsh is taking over Indigo at a moment when the airline still looks, from a distance, like the cleanest story in Indian aviation. Investors certainly seemed to think so: the stock jumped about 9% on the day of the announcement.

To be fair, the company is growing, it is profitable, and it continues to dominate the market in a way none of its rivals quite manage. This is also where the picture gets a little less convenient.

In the December quarter, Indigo flew more (capacity rose over 11% year-on-year) and earned more, with revenues up about 6–7%. That is what the model is supposed to do. Keep planes full, keep them flying… as many as possible. But profits did not follow the same graph. Net profit fell sharply to about Rs 5,500 crore, margins compressed, and the gap between revenue growth and cost growth widened.

Some of that is just the airline business being the airline business. Costs are global, including leases, maintenance, and fuel, while revenues are local. When the rupee weakens, they feel it. Immediately. Indigo has kept unit costs broadly in check, but not without strain, and not everywhere. The stock declined roughly 4% the next day, thanks to higher fuel price announcements. 

But some of it is more self-inflicted.

Under Pieter Elbers, who took over in 2022, the airline steadied itself after the pandemic and kept its growth engine running, expanding capacity, pushing international routes, and holding on to its cost discipline. But the model he inherited was already tightly wound. With high utilisation and dense schedules.

That held until December 2025. The infamous scheduling disruptions with delays, cancellations, stretched crews, and a regulatory fine north of $2 million, weren’t a freak coincidence. The system, designed to run hot, started to overheat.

All of this is happening just as the environment around Indigo is getting less forgiving. Which is where Walsh, with experience in leading large airline operations in complex conditions https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/cz6739j210qo, comes in. Not to fix a broken airline, but to run one that works, just with less room for error than before. The question is how long that holds when pilots run short, when pricing gets creative, when competition wakes up, and when passengers start noticing the design a little too clearly. 

All of which The Ken has been writing about.

The airline you complain about, and still fly

Let’s start with the paradox.

Indigo flies nearly two-thirds of India’s domestic passengers, that is, about 9 million people a month, and also manages to be one of its most complained-about airlines. Complaints have doubled year-on-year.

Passengers complain about delays, broken luggage, and, quite frequently, indifferent staff. Employees complain about poor working conditions and a leadership that listens less than it used to. And yet, people keep booking seats.

Because Indigo doesn’t need to be liked. It needs to be unavoidable. With a monopoly on hundreds of routes and two out of every three flyers passing through its system, preference is a function of availability. Annoyance, in that sense, is not a failure.

When the model runs out of slack

The problem with a tightly run system is that it leaves very little runway to spare.

Indigo runs about 2,200 flights a day. That requires precision. Instead, what it got in late 2025 was a slow-building pilot shortage that turned into 4,000–4,500 cancelled flights and a forced 10% cut in schedules.

https://the-ken.com/story/how-indigo-ran-out-of-pilots-gradually-then-suddenly/?utm_source=newsletter&utm_medium=email&utm_campaign=collection?utm_source=ro_hdr&utm_medium=email&utm_campaign=

The triggers were visible in hindsight. New duty-time rules capped pilot hours. Air India doubled its pilot base; Indigo added only a few hundred, and then lost some. A system built to run full had suddenly run short.

We called it Indigo’s “crown of thorns”. A business so optimised that even small miscalculations cascade. At this scale, even a 0.7–0.8% cancellation rate can strand tens of thousands of passengers. Efficiency doesn’t leave much room for recovery.

Paying more, without noticing

Then there is another part of the model: how airlines make money.

Indigo earns roughly 10% of its revenue from ancillary streams of seat selection, cancellations, and baggage. This is far below global peers like Ryanair, but still critical. And some of that is unusually lucrative. Cancel a Rs 7,000 ticket, and the airline may keep 40–60% of it.

When regulators try to limit this, say, by mandating refunds or restricting seat charges, the model adapts. Fees get redistributed and base fares inch up. Bundles appear and choices get automatically nudged.

Indigo has been spotted using the same “dark pattern” playbook as consumer apps. Pre-selected add-ons, guilt nudges, and whatnot. The idea has been to extract a little more, without making it feel like a lot.

When competition finally shows up

For a long time, Indigo’s biggest advantage was the lack of credible alternatives. That is beginning to change.

Akasa, barely three years old, already runs 140–150 flights a day with ~5% market share, backed by deep-pocketed investors and an order book of over 200 aircraft. Air India is rebuilding, even if unevenly.

The uncomfortable trade-off

For all the frustration, something else has happened alongside it.

Flying has become cheaper. Since 2011, average domestic airfares have fallen over 20% (inflation-adjusted). Passenger traffic has exploded from tens of millions to over 200 million annually, with much of that growth riding on low-cost carriers like Indigo.

Planes are newer and routes are denser. Which makes the trade-off harder to dismiss. Because what feels like inconvenience at the individual level, including fees and no-frills service, is often what makes the system affordable at scale. The airline is just pricing comfort.

Under Walsh, unfortunately, the airline has to specialise in the unexpected.
