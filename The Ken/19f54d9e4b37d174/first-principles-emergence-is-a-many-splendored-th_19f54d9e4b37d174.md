Good morning Ishan, it’s wonderful to be back in your inbox and your weekend after a break.
I took a short beach vacation last week. We stayed by the sea. So close that we could hear it at night. Not just through the rhythmic sounds of waves crashing, but also through the low rumble of the sea just being.
During the hot days, the sound duty was taken over by the breeze blowing through coconut palms.
Unfettered wind that blows in from seas or large backwater lakes is powerful and noisy. You cannot ignore it. You can literally feel it on your body. On your legs, feet, hands, face, hair, and ears. When it is inside your ears, you can both hear and feel it as it bombards your eardrums. Whoo. Whoo! WHOO!
And then there was the cawing of the crows—sounds that I haven’t so predictably heard since childhood.
The days were an indescribable mix of hot weather that stayed constant, and a strong breeze that ebbed and flowed. Both mixing at just below the margin where you can feel cool or comfortable sitting on an easy chair under coconut palms.
Ironically, it’s these moments that lead to the most magical naps. It’s so hot that your body wants to shut down. But the wind and the sounds provide just the right amount of distraction for you to doze into a liminal zone where you’re napping, but you’re also awake and can sense everything around you. The crows, the kites, the gulls, the swaying coconut palm leaves, the sea.
I was simultaneously present there and in the past, in the dozen-odd Kerala summers from childhood.
Days where there was absolutely nothing to do. Days where 9.00 a.m. and 10.00 a.m. are indistinguishable from 1.00 p.m. and 2.00 p.m. Time loses meaning. The arrival of anyone becomes an event. It could be a fishmonger, a visitor, a neighbour, or a passing boat. Anything that broke the monotony became a marker of time and a distraction.
In marginal coastal communities, it’s fishing that sets the time. The time the nets are cast. The time they are pulled in. The time the fish are taken to the nearest auction or market. The time the nets are dried, cleaned, and repaired. The time the fish is bought. The time the fish is cleaned. The time the fish is cooked. The time the fish is eaten. The time the day’s fish is discussed. The time the lack of quality fish is bemoaned.
The next day, the cycle repeats itself.
Was I experiencing the present more acutely because of my childhood memories? Or was I using the present as a portal to relive my childhood memories?
With nothing to do during the Kerala summers of the 80s, I fished and read books (mostly obscure and cheap Russian fiction borrowed from an elder cousin). With nothing to do last week, I read books.
I read Careless People by Sarah Wynn-Williams, an absolutely unputdownable book chronicling the kind of company Meta (nee Facebook, perhaps soon “Superintelligence”?) is.
Sorry, let me make that clearer: the kind of people [who run Meta](https://www.theguardian.com/books/2025/mar/16/careless-people-a-story-of-where-i-used-to-work-by-sarah-wynn-williams-review-a-former-disciple-unfriends-facebook). Wynn-Williams worked at Meta from 2011 to 2017, running public policy. She worked directly with Meta leaders like Mark Zuckerberg, Sheryl Sandberg, and Joel Kaplan and absolutely skewers them through her recollection of events.
Meta has turned the might of its legal machine against Wynn-Williams, so much so that she cannot say *a word* about her book. A few weeks ago, she [countersued Meta](https://www.theguardian.com/technology/2026/jun/25/whistleblower-sarah-wynn-williams-sues-meta-attempts-to-silence-her-careless-people).
Please buy and read the book.
I also read three wonderful novels.
Heather, a mystery-thriller by Caitlin Mullen. Slow Gods, a space opera-style sci-fi novel by Claire North. And The Outsider, by the inimitable Stephen King. I’d seen the TV series when it came out, but the novel was much better.

“There is nothing in a caterpillar that tells you it’s going to be a butterfly,” said the late theorist and futurist Buckminster Fuller once. I consider it the best description of [“emergence.”](https://systemsthinkingalliance.org/the-crucial-role-of-emergence-in-systems-thinking/)
Yes, we’ve talked about it in a past edition, back in [March 2024](https://the-ken.com/newsletter/first-principles/emergence/).
I’m bringing it up again because the First Principles System is leading me, and us, in newer directions.
For the past eight weeks, I’ve been running a coaching and mentoring program for a bunch of my younger and smarter colleagues. It’s called “The Greenroom”, a hat-tip to the similarly named [liminal pre-space](https://www.backstage.com/magazine/article/what-is-a-green-room-77433/) for stage performers.
I designed it as a 24-week program that’s divided into 6 blocks of a month each. Each week, there is a 90-minute in-classroom session. Here’s what our first 8 weeks looked like:
1. S1: Thinking in Systems. Set the frame using Donella Meadows’ systems vocabulary (stocks, flows, feedback). “Everything is part of a system. Solve for the system, not the symptom.”
2. S2: Feedback loops in your work. Sketch one real feedback loop in your own work. Critique where the loop works, where it’s broken.
3. S3: Start from ignorance / first principles. Surface one assumption in your work that “everyone knows is true,” then question it.
4. S4: First calibration.
5. S5: Value problems more than solutions. Learn how to reframe problems from different angles.
6. S6: Critical self-evaluation. The editor’s mindset: learning to critique your own work as if a stranger made it.
7. S7: Decision quality vs outcome quality. Learning to make better decisions by separating outcome quality from decision quality.
8. S8: Second calibration.
Did you notice what we did there? The sessions are all drawn from prior editions of First Principles that I’ve written and we’ve discussed.
A quick admission here: I’ve never been a consistent mentor or coach. I’d rate myself a 3/10.
But I’d also rate myself a 7/10 when it comes to impromptu coaching or mentoring, for example when a colleague explicitly sought out my help.
The gap between those two scores was because of the overhead of what I call “admin work”.
The time, discipline, and effort required to create notes and curricula; create recurring calendars with multiple colleagues; context-switch between listening to what they want from me versus what I can reliably offer to them; the effort required to come up with a good mix of concepts, in-room interactivity, homework, assimilation, etc.
Writing First Principles solved a big part of that problem for me. I now had access to nearly 150 editions where I’d talked about the mental models, management concepts, and self-growth prisms that I found valuable. I’d been talking to you about them over the years. Of course, I could go much deeper into them in a room with my colleagues!
Secondly, once I accepted that I was not good at 1:1 coaching, I was free to accept that 1:n would be my only option. I got the confidence to accept that after reading about Nvidia’s Jensen Huang doing so too. We spoke about it [in March 2026](https://the-ken.com/newsletter/first-principles/cybernetic-ai-feedback-loops-and-the-socratic-method/).
Lastly, I was able to use AI to match the interests and ambitions of a diverse set of colleagues with years of writing to design a curriculum that was customised, iterative, and adaptive.
We—my colleagues and I—literally build the topics we want to cover as we’re proceeding through the program, week after week. Already, there’s emergence within the program, as we’ve added newer modules and topics that I’d never think of on my own.
Yesterday I wrote a column that was [quite critical of Anthropic](https://the-ken.com/columns/zero-shot/our-anthropic-fever-has-broken/?utm_source=web&utm_medium=homepage&utm_campaign=con_1_unit_1), but today I’m telling you that without Claude, I’d never be able to manage the sheer complexity of designing the curriculum, structuring the weekly sessions, creating interactivity and homework, observing the differences and nuances across colleagues and changing the structure of what sessions lie ahead as we proceed.
I’m able to run something as ambitious as this (because so many of my colleagues’ careers and work are connected to it now) largely on my own. My colleagues are teaching me what it means to be a better coach or mentor while I’m teaching them how to use Systems Thinking and Mental Models to succeed as professionals.
This is emergence.
There was nothing in August 2022 (when I released the very first episode of the [First Principles podcast](https://the-ken.com/podcasts/first-principles/kabeer-biswas-dunzo/) with Kabeer Biswas) that would lead me (or you) here in July 2026.
I italicised those words because I’ve already written to you, back in March 2024. From the [edition](https://the-ken.com/newsletter/first-principles/emergence/) originally called “Emergence”.

This is Vishnu, with the movie picks for the week.
I was speaking to a couple of my colleagues at the office—or rather, at lunch—and the topic of Anthony Bourdain came up.
The conversation started with Parts Unknown, No Reservations, Kitchen Confidential, and then ended with [his cameo](https://youtu.be/kxN_qPuefrM?si=D6QhtfKGjXDzVy1k) in The Big Short, breaking the fourth wall, explaining what Collateralised Debt Obligations (CDOs) are.
This prompted me to suggest the holy trinity on the 2008 financial crisis, which they were unaware of.
So here’s this week’s suggestions, a [Rashomon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rashomon_effect)-esque list of the crash, through three movies:
1. The Big Short (2015)
Probably one of the most popular finance-based movies, because of how gently it takes you on this (extremely) technical journey of numbers, terms, jargon, and a whole lot of information to take in.
With very rapid pacing and an intelligent use of cameos (one of which was Bourdain), the movie is the perfect place to start the trilogy (in my opinion).
The Big Short streams on Prime Video/Netflix, depending on your location.
2. Margin Call (2011)
If The Big Short was a comparatively ‘outsider’ view into what was happening during the crash, Margin Call takes you right into the bullpen and conference halls of a Wall Street investment bank.
With its claustrophobic, bleak frames and dialogue sharp enough to cut metal, it’s definitely a much heavier watch. But definitely one I’d highly recommend. (Watch [this](https://youtu.be/ISDgcB-J4fQ?si=x9PyHdoEY0ejbOY2), for a taster)
You can rent Margin Call on Youtube.
3. Too Big to Fail (2011)
Unlike the first two, Too Big to Fail drops you right into the middle of the crisis and the aftermath it caused. With no time to spare, throwing around terms like there’s no tomorrow. Hence, my suggestion is to watch this after you have context.
This focuses more on the ripple effects the crisis had on the government and the subsequent bailout of the banks. What sounds like a rather dry affair is carried by a packed cast.
Too Big to Fail, an HBO original, is streaming on Hotstar.

Hello Ishan, this is Tanim writing the section on reading, writing, and books.
The session about “Literature in the Age of AI” held at BIC last weekend was thought-provoking. I walked in expecting the panel to rally unanimously against AI, but there were insightful counterpoints scattered throughout the discussion.
Our panellists were an author (Lavanya Lakshminarayan), a judge (Natasha Joshi from The Deodar Prize), and a publisher (Ranjeet Pratap Singh from Pratilipi). In hindsight, their professional roles shaped nearly everything they said.
The judge was an interesting middle ground: adamant that AI has no place in creative writing but also acknowledging its unavoidable utility in combating AI-generated content itself.
The publisher, though, was all for it. His business thrives on selling stories and making them accessible across languages, and he saw AI as a powerful tool that can lead to faster and cheaper translations.
The author, of course, rejected any AI-generated material to be part of literature (barring research), arguing that creating new meanings that are also meaningful to readers is beyond its reach.
In the end, the moderator called for a poll: would writing die because of AI? I think we can guess what the room decided. Hard no.
There are genuine use cases for AI in translation, but when it comes to crafting something entirely new and unique, the superintelligent tool lacks the one thing that puts the words on paper, and that is our experience. As the author argued, it is our embodied experiences that helps us write what we imagine or write. Yes, like AI, we are influenced by what we read, but the combination of what we have read, lived, thought, and dreamt of can create a literary piece as unique as a person’s DNA.
If you recall a previous [edition](https://the-ken.com/newsletter/first-principles/loss-functions-and-self-authoring/) of First Principles, Rohin asked what makes writing “human”. There were different ways in which you judged what felt “authentic” back then. But the common thread for why you justified that your own writing is not AI-generated was embodiment. Our physical, emotional, and temporal existence shapes our voice in ways no algorithm can replicate.
