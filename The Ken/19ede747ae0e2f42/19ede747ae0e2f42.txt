The Unified Pension Scheme and the Indian art of unintended consequences. The government’s latest pension fix asks people to swim on their own, and it’s not the first scheme to make that trade-off

Governments rarely solve problems. They trade one set of problems for another. The best policies make the trade-off worthwhile. The worst ones simply move the pain elsewhere. The new Unified Pension Scheme (UPS) is the latest example. 

The problems it was trying to solve were the ones created by previous pension schemes. Here’s a quick recap https://www.reporters-collective.in/trc/billboard-governance-under-modi-majority-schemes-faced-funding-squeeze:

The Pradhan Mantri Karam Yogi Mandhan, announced in 2019, was designed to benefit small shopkeepers and retailers. In its first year, it spent just 20% of its promised Rs 750 crore budget.

As it failed to improve over the years, the government blamed beneficiaries for confusing it with the Pradhan Mantri Shram Yogi Mandhan. Though this one was hardly in better shape. Its target was to help 38 crore workers in the unorganised sector build a pension by splitting contributions with the government, but only 46 lakh registrations have been recorded to date. 

Curiously, even with inflation and an increase in registrations, government expenditure has been reducing, dropping almost 50% in 2023–24. 

?utm_source=ro_hdr&utm_medium=email&utm_campaign=View in Browser https://the-ken.com/?post_type=the_collection&p=1714587?utm_source=ro_hdr&utm_medium=email&utm_campaign=

The contributory nature of the scheme makes it hard for daily wage earners to even keep up with the monthly premium https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=2108082&reg=48&lang=2 of Rs 55—200. 

The government again attributed the poor performance to people confusing it with other existing schemes like the Atal Pension Yojana, prompting a think tank to recommend merging the two since both target the unorganised sector. 

Another limitation was the Rs 15,000 monthly income cap, which froze out large numbers of the very workers these schemes were designed to target. 

Periodic contributions and income cap seemed to be the major gatekeepers with existing pension products, especially since the scheme wanted to focus on people with irregular incomes. 

With this problem statement in mind, UPS is being planned. 

The Ministry of Labour and Employment is considering the new scheme to allow workers to contribute any amount at any time, administered by EPFO and open to all workers, irrespective of whether they are employed in the formal or informal sector. 

This means no income cap. Accounts would remain active even if subscribers stop contributing for extended periods, directly addressing the dormancy problem that plagued earlier schemes. 

On paper, that answers every complaint about the old schemes at once. 

Except that the government has removed its own contributions entirely. Which brings you back to the same wall. Removing the barrier to entry doesn’t help if there’s nothing to put in, and now there’s no government top-up to bridge that gap either.

Government schemes and its risky asterisks

Every government scheme carries an asterisk which says: Problems solved might create other problems.

The new data law follows that same pattern, just with more moving parts to break. And like most things the government builds in a hurry, it was laid on an unfinished foundation. 

It was trying to fulfil the basic need of what consent would look like for Indians when it comes to their own data. In trying to give users that control, the Digital Personal Data Protection (DPDP) Act may have handed https://www.ifj.org/media-centre/news/detail/category/press-releases/article/india-new-data-law-threatens-press-freedom companies a new tool to suppress information, reject RTIs, or get away with giving vague answers. Several journalist groups have already filed a PIL flagging curbs on journalistic freedom and heightened surveillance risks. Last month, the Supreme Court issued a notice asking the government to respond. 

Then there’s another problem that surfaced. 

To comply with the DPDP Act, companies need consent managers—neutral parties who sit between users and data processes. But the law is vague enough not to clarify whether KYC companies can fill that role or not. Many think that KYC companies cannot take that role but the firms themselves disagree. In New data law has HDFC Bank, Amazon, Paytm, and others shopping for help https://the-ken.com/story/indias-digital-privacy-law-has-spawned-a-compliance-economy-and-a-turf-war/?utm_source=email&utm_medium=newsletter&utm_campaign=collection, we speak to both sides. 

Predictably, the user ends up further down the priority ladder as companies try to figure out third-party consent managers. This is a result of not defining what consent infrastructure is supposed to look like for big corporations, individuals, or government agencies.

And then there’s some collateral damage too. AI startups are looking at a 10–20% rise in operational expenses to meet compliance requirements, according to NDTV https://www.ndtv.com/business-news/digital-personal-data-protection-dpdp-act-privacy-cyber-security-india-startup-ai-11648440. 

In trying to fix Indians’ control over data, the DPDP ACT is producing a compliance economy that primarily will end up serving corporations and a consent framework that can be weaponised against the press.

Warning: blunders ahead

Most government programmes crawl (currently thinking about the metro lines forever in construction in Bengaluru). Ethanol has been sprinting.

The government has exempted E22–30 fuel blends from central excise duty, and E85 is already in the pipeline. With no speedbreaker in sight, fuel blends are filling up petrol pumps before the vehicles can be modified and stakeholders properly educated. 

In From BS6 to E20—India learnt the wrong lessons. Here’s how to course correct https://the-ken.com/newsletter/make-india-competitive-again/from-bs6-to-e20-india-learnt-the-wrong-lessons-heres-how-to-course-correct/?utm_source=email&utm_medium=newsletter&utm_campaign=collection, The Ken argued that India was racing ahead on blending targets without resolving what higher ethanol concentrations actually do to engines and the environment. 

Even before that, E20 is good for India. But how good is it for vehicles and petrol bunks? https://the-ken.com/greenmargins/e20-is-good-for-india-but-how-good-is-it-for-vehicles-and-petrol-bunks/?utm_source=email&utm_medium=newsletter&utm_campaign=collection highlighted the government’s negligence in communicating these risks to vehicle owners. To this day, they are still not properly aware of the risks of high-ethanol blends or how to manage them. 

And its main argument, reducing India’s oil import bill, might fall flat as the uncertainty of petrol’s composition is pushing people towards EVs.

The former Mercedes-Benz executive lays out the math. “A typical electric car sold in India today uses a battery pack of around 50 kWh. At current global prices of $90–100 per kWh, the battery alone can cost nearly $5,000,” he says. “If India were to eventually sell 400,000 EVs a month, replacing the oil-run cars, the bill for battery cells alone would cross $2 billion monthly. That does not include electric trucks, buses, scooters, or commercial fleets and the import of rare-earth metals.”

India needs Tata, Mahindra EVs to cut the oil bill. But EV owners are stuck at workshops https://the-ken.com/story/india-needs-tata-mahindra-evs-to-cut-the-oil-bill-but-ev-owners-are-stuck-at-workshops/?utm_source=email&utm_medium=newsletter&utm_campaign=collection, The Ken

Ethanol was the fix for the oil import bill. So now somewhere down that chain, a battery import bill is becoming the next problem.

Another area where the government is racing ahead is in planning https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=1989671 for DILRMP 3.0, i.e., the third phase of the Digital India Land Records Modernisation Programme. The main aim is to move towards easier land verification, faster resolution of disputes, better credit access, and stronger infrastructure planning. 

All of that sounds important, yes, but it’s still worth asking if we are ready to move ahead. DILRMP of 2016 still has unfinished goals.

Whose land is it anyway? https://the-ken.com/story/whose-land-is-it-anyway/?utm_source=email&utm_medium=newsletter&utm_campaign=collectiondiscussed its delays. And in the latest available data https://dolr.gov.in/programmes-schemes/dilrmp-2/, the numbers haven’t moved as much. Physical cadastral maps, the base layer of any land record system, have gone from 56% digitised to 68%. Geo-referencing, which makes those maps spatially useful, sits at 49%. Meaning half the country’s land records still can’t be reliably located in space.

Of 700+ districts, only 168 https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=1989671&reg=3&lang=2 have been Platinum Graded, the designation for completing 99% of work across six core components. That’s fewer than one in four. 

Why this is so important is because spatial integration is the foundation on which everything else is supposed to sit on. Credit access, dispute resolution, infrastructure planning, none of it works without knowing precisely where the piece of land is and who owns it. 

Once again, new problems are being created as old ones are solved. The villages that haven’t been digitised yet, that haven’t been geo-referenced, that don’t have Platinum Graded records, won’t show up in the dataset that DILRMP 3.0 will use to plan infrastructure and industrial projects. The scheme designed to bring development to underserved areas may end up routing it around them entirely. 

This problem of always trying to connect tier-3 India to tier-1 India extends beyond infrastructure. 

The PM Internship Scheme was built on a reasonable diagnosis that India’s young graduates, especially from tier-3 colleges in tier-3 towns, are caught between an education that didn’t prepare them and a job market that increasingly demands experiences they don’t have. To solve that, 10 million internships over five years inside 500 of the country’s top companies like TCS, Reliance, IBM, and Coca-Cola sounded good on paper. 

This is how the actual numbers turned out:

Likely because the scheme pays a Rs 5,000 monthly stipend combining the Rs 4,500 from the government and Rs 500 from the company’s CSR fund. There’s no requirement for companies to pay more and no incentive encouraging them to. For an intern placed in a tier-1 city, that stipend doesn’t cover rent, let alone living expenses. And the scheme’s own eligibility cap—household income below Rs 8 lakh per annum—means it’s designed exclusively for candidates for whom family support is not an option. 

The scheme set out to reach precisely the people who can least afford to take it up. 

The stipend is the most visible crack. In Why only 1 in 10 interns join the PM Internship Scheme https://the-ken.com/newsletter/make-india-competitive-again/pm-internship-scheme-another-shaky-start-to-another-shaky-skilling-programme/?utm_source=email&utm_medium=newsletter&utm_campaign=collection, there are other cracks in the foundation that were neglected: how companies participating are largely mass-volume employers looking to fill bottom-of-the-pyramid roles and how getting the intern through the door is just the first hurdle because converting that into employment is a different ball game. These are not newer problems but ones that existed even in other predecessor skilling programmes.

A previous flagship youth training scheme placed fewer than 8% candidates it trained. Another, focused on rural youth, managed 65% placement after over a decade of operation. 

The gap between what the government builds and what reaches people has become an expectation. 

India has over 20K govt schemes, but a family on average uses only 55, shows Haqdarshak data, https://the-ken.com/story/counterthesis-india-has-over-20k-govt-schemes-but-an-average-indian-family-uses-only-55-shows-haqdarshak-data/?utm_source=email&utm_medium=newsletter&utm_campaign=collection addressed this lacuna in detail back in 2022. 

And now as funding https://www.reporters-collective.in/trc/billboard-governance-under-modi-majority-schemes-faced-funding-squeeze for schemes gets progressively squeezed, the reality of a holistic government scheme is further out of reach. For now, the standard is more schemes, less money, half-baked foundations, and new resultant problems.

None of this is inevitable. The fractures are visible enough, documented enough, repeated enough that they are just blunders at this point.
