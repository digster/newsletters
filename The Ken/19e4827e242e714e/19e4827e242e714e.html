I recently realised that there are too many variables in play in our financial decisions lately. And even in our eating choices. 

For instance, earlier this month, Prime Minister Narendra Modi made an unusual request: don’t buy gold, cut down on cooking oil, and use less petrol and diesel. 

After this, import duties on gold and silver were increased to 15% from the existing 6%, while petrol and diesel prices were increased twice within a week. For a country that loves its gold, its fried snacks, dosas, and its SUVs, this was definitely not pleasing to hear.

These are not even local, but mostly influenced by what’s happening around the world.  

According to a report, there are currently 56 active conflicts globally, the most since World War II. Amid the Russia-Ukraine, Iran-US, Israel-Palestine, and Sudanese civil war, over 90 countries are currently involved in conflicts outside their borders.

With the second Indian ship sunk in the Strait of Hormuz in recent weeks, every drone attack on ships, every supply route disruption, and import duties by the US—all of it, eventually, will find a way to our kitchens, our fuel tanks, and bank balances.

For ordinary people, this means inflation could become the main character in our financial story. Households are spending more on essentials, leaving less for mutual funds, gold, or stocks. Needless to say, the Indian rupee’s depreciation to an all-time low at Rs 97 against one dollar has made big tech stocks and gold investments further costlier to Indian investors. 

Overall, the mutual fund industry added Rs 8 lakh crore to its asset base in FY26, marking the slowest rise in three years amid continued volatility in Indian equity markets. The industry also registered 6.4 lakh new investors in 2025, 40% fewer than in 2024. 

New demat account openings fell 33% in 2025, with only about 3 crore accounts opened compared to 4.6 crore in 2024.

Almost half a dozen investors The Ken spoke to seconded the thought that the new tax regime has reduced the incentive for tax-saving investments like Equity Linked Savings Scheme (ELSS), which has contributed to slower retail investment growth in certain categories.

Schemes like ELSS were the funnel for salaried households to save their income and enjoy tax exemptions. But under the new regime, with fewer exemptions available, that behavioural nudge has weakened.

Earlier, the people’s mentality was ‘invest’ to save taxes, now the latest regime has changed this to ‘invest only if expected returns justify risk’

A fintech founder

But it is not the sole or primary reason for the broader slowdown in demat activity. 

Some of the users have also indicated that multiple investment instruments, such as mutual funds, have not been very rewarding lately. As a tower lead at Infosys told The Ken, “Even fixed deposits have yielded better returns than some of my SIPs in the last two years.”

At the same time, rising gold prices and increasing household expenditure are also impacting investments. The pressure of inflation is starting to show.

Restaurants in Bengaluru hiked prices by 10–20% in recent weeks. Some have stopped serving dosas or reduced portions because commercial LPG is in short supply. The government also increased commercial LPG prices by Rs 933. 

This is just the beginning, as the second-order effects have not fully kicked in yet. For instance, diesel prices and fertiliser shortages are due to the war in West Asia. India imports 70% of its fertilisers. “Once the current crop cycle absorbs these costs, there would be another round of food inflation,” an agritech founder told The Ken. 

Even Amazons and Zomatos of the world are feeling the heat. The price rise and LPG issue have reduced the net income of the gig workers, and last week, they went on a nationwide strike demanding a minimum of Rs 20 per kilometre. Amazon has already added a 3.5% fuel and logistics surcharge to its fulfilment fees in the US and Canada. A similar move could be underway for its Indian market as well.

Speaking at an IMF panel discussion on Monday, the RBI Governor Sanjay Malhotra put it plainly: “In a supply shock, we generally try to ‘look through’ the first-round impact, if we believe it is transitory. However, if sustained price increases drive up wages, production, and transportation costs, the ‘look through’ approach is no longer optimal, requiring tighter policy”.

Since the pandemic and particularly after the outbreak of the Russia-Ukraine war, central banks have moved towards a more flexible, meeting-by-meeting approach in policy formulation. They are now dependent on a wider array of information variables, using high-frequency data to make faster and more informed decisions. Moreover, while targeting headline inflation, they are increasingly distinguishing between transitory headline spikes and persistent core inflation trends to avoid any pre-emptive policy tightening that is unwarranted.

RBI Governor Sanjay Malhotra, at the 12th High-Level Conference on the International Monetary System

Further, even the investment industry seems divided on what a good investment instrument is anymore. 

Nithin Kamath of Zerodha* has been consistently voicing that Unit Linked Insurance Plan (ULIPs) aren’t a smart choice for investments. But PB Fintech’s management, which recently received Sebi’s approval for debt trading, in its Q4FY26 earnings call said ULIPs remain among the best investment options, especially given their tax advantage up to Rs 2.5 lakh. Both have their reasons.

See, many times we forget that despite all the discussion about SIPs and mutual funds, after 5 years, the number of active SIPs, varies from 3% to 11% or something. So, after 5 years in our ULIP, the persistency is somewhere in the 70-75% range.

PB Fintech Management in their latest earnings call

What’s constant, though, is that the RBI has, so far, left EMIs untouched. For now, that’s the one variable that hasn’t changed.
