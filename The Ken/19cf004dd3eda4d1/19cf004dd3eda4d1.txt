Instead of pretending to have all the answers, we’re going to break the narrative. 2. A game of ‘multi-tracked moves’

“Play” has been something we’ve talked about many times on Sundays. By that I mean treating work or life as play.

Life can be poker https://the-ken.com/newsletter/first-principles/bad-outcomes-arent-the-same-as-bad-decisions/, an infinite game https://the-ken.com/newsletter/first-principles/the-infinite-game-and-the-player/, or even Calvinball https://the-ken.com/newsletter/first-principles/killing-your-darlings-and-playing-calvinball/.

A few weeks ago my colleague Brady Ng, who leads The Ken’s second newsroom (which houses all our newer, non-traditional, or experimental editorial products) while being based in Hong Kong, gave me pause when he made the following observation on our Slack (emphasis is his).

“To use a chess analogy: good players don’t make one move when it’s their turn; they make a move that is part of a larger set of moves. In chess, it’s (often) linear. In real-life work settings, it’s (often) multi-tracked.”

Absolutely!

The reason Brady’s advice resonated so strongly with me was because one of my most common feedback to colleagues  is sort of a “sibling” to that. “Zoom out a bit and see the bigger picture” is both a cliché and a common tip from me.

Why do I say zooming out is a sibling to planning multiple moves into the future?

Let’s think about that relationship from both sides.

Let’s assume someone is really good at planning multiple moves across multiple threads into the future. This implies an ability to go deep into a few tasks at any given point in time while also managing to stay shallow on many others (remember “A Fourier Analysis of our Lives and Careers” https://the-ken.com/newsletter/first-principles/a-fourier-analysis-of-our-lives-and-careers/?).

How would they do that without occasionally stepping back and seeing the big picture—all the strings they’re holding?

Conversely, imagine someone who’s very good at big-picture thinking. But what good is that if they aren’t able to use that perspective to plan for success better? How many people do you admire for their big-picture thinking but who also suck at multitasking or future planning?

Given I’m a big fan of 2X2 matrices, I’ll try to illustrate it the way I “see” it in my head.

Getting caught in a single task and losing sense of perspective leads us down to another famous 2X2—the Eisenhower Matrix https://jamesclear.com/eisenhower-box. Specifically, the “urgent but not important” quadrant.

Without a sense of the big picture, we end up spending all available time on short-term, urgent things. “I’ll pick up the creative and ambitious things once I’m done with this week’s urgent tasks,” we tell ourselves. Of course, next week gets filled with another set of urgent tasks.

The flaw in our thinking is our belief that getting done with urgent tasks helps create time for important tasks. But in my experience, it’s the other way around.

Setting aside and spending time on important tasks reduces the time available to spend on urgent but unimportant tasks. We end up dropping some balls. And when we do, we realise they weren’t really important at all.

And once we switch our attention back to the multi-tracked game, we have more time to focus on impacts and outcomes. More time to prepare. More time to be creative. We have more agency, more control.

We also have more uniqueness, because there’s a very strong likelihood that you will be the only one thinking and planning about your specific tasks that far ahead, and with that much attention. It becomes impossible for anyone else to compete with you on the very same things you’ve chosen over the very same time periods.

4. Not a recommendation

Hello, it’s Tanim again. 

Do you ever pick a book and immediately regret not choosing better? You can write to fp@the-ken.com mailto:fp@the-ken.com to rant about it so we steer clear, or share your happy memories of a book you recently read here https://theken.typeform.com/FPBooks?typeform-source=the-ken.com. 

I remember how carefully I chose the book Creative Confidence by David and Tom Kelley, largely because I have an inherent dislike for books that fall into the self-help category or promise to teach you how to think. I had picked this book for the January theme of “Reinventing yourself https://the-ken.com/newsletter/first-principles/the-january-chasm/.” If you have read a book that fits the theme and would like to share with our First Principles reader, whether you are someone who has a book to recommend for every theme or just this one, you can share your thoughts here https://theken.typeform.com/FPBooks?typeform-source=the-ken.com.  

Maybe my fondness for alliteration made me overly confident about this one. The prologue was decent enough. But what followed was hard to push through at first. It was like the genius scientist finally realised that they must account for practical realities and that their brilliant theory won’t always translate into purposeful execution.

The book was about how you can consciously be creative while making products, from medical technologies to designer fashion. 

So, the beginning was disappointing, but I persevered. You never know when a book might turn interesting. And to be fair, it had some nuggets: a few good analogies, some real-life incidents the authors encountered themselves. 

But there was hardly anything new for me to take away. Perhaps because many of these ideas had already landed better for me in First Principles, where Rohin weaves his own experiences into mental models in a far more thought-provoking way. 

In one of my early writing tasks at The Ken, PGK’s feedback was that I needed more punch in my writing. Succinct but passionate. I am not quite there yet. But neither, I realised, are the authors of this book. No, it’s not my attention span that needs fixing. (Well, it does, but even if I fixed it, I still wouldn’t enjoy reading this.)

Conveying things clearly and crisply while having fun with them is one way to describe what we aim for in our writing. And by “fun”, we mean fun not just for us, but for the reader. That means respecting your time, your intelligence, and your desire to be surprised. 

If you want to use Creative Confidence as a textbook, I can see it working. Not in terms of jargon—the book is quite plainspoken—but in the way the ideas are explained. The authors are educators, after all, and their writing makes sense in an academic context. But as a First Principles recommendation? No, not really.  

5. Newsroom vs. Editing room

Hello there!

It’s Vishnu with the Movies/TV/Music section for this week.

We are on the fourth and final week of the FP Parallel Oscars, and the categories are: Editing, Production Design, Costume, Makeup, and VFX.

You can vote here https://theken.typeform.com/to/BntCqHnr.

I’ll be honest, it’s been a hectic couple of weeks here at The Ken, with multiple “bets” (as Rohin puts it) being worked on by almost everyone, in the newsroom or otherwise. 

In one of our post-work wind-down sessions at the IDC next to the Bengaluru office (which I call The Ken’s second conference room), a colleague and I were discussing how the outputs of a newsroom and a movie production have a lot in common.

Somewhere during the intense discussion (a running theme here), she said, “A journalism story and a film script aren’t all that different, you know.”

I mean, when you bring it down to its bare bones, both are doing the same thing: tightening the narrative.

And if you look long enough, you’ll be able to split the three-act structure in both of them.

Call it my frequency illusion, but ever since I learned about the setup-confrontation-resolution framework during one of my many free-WiFi-hogging Youtube binge sessions in college, I haven’t been able to see movies the same way:

The Intern, Jurassic Park, Se7en, Nightcrawler, RRR, Aavesham, Ustad Hotel, Vikram, Dead Poets Society, every movie, irrespective of how “big” they are, what their “recreation” budget is (Between Two Ferns reference https://youtu.be/G8y-M5rQiWQ?si=4uW5i0Qld1djXqvU&t=13), or whether they are using the “cool” Mocobot shot, you probably can fit the plot into this structure. 

In case you’re interested: there’s a GREAT video https://youtu.be/kFsI1uK9q44?si=vNBux5jfSx67xrFO explaining the three-act structure through Wes Anderson’s short film Come Together, which he directed for H&M.

Also, please turn on the subtitles, the notes are really helpful, and fun!

Coming back to my point, even a good journalistic piece follows a three-act structure.

For instance, take the hook (or the “lede”) of any story:

While the country is running out of gas, Adani Total Gas shares are going up. 

Private equity firms are now squeezing money out of non-profit schools. 

TISS is starting to look more like an IIM than TISS. 

Amazon is funnelling you to Orange Health Labs for at-home diagnostics. 

(All stories published at The Ken in the last four months.)

The lede must answer one question, above anything else:

“Why should the reader keep reading?”

It’s the same with good movies. You see a cunning, face-painted, eccentric clown out-con and outwit a bunch of criminals to loot a bank in the middle of the day, and get away with it—all under five minutes—and you sit up. Because you want to know what happens next. (Of course, referring to Nolan’s pièce de résistance https://youtu.be/xLcHPsWK5xg?si=H4xoPVm1REpZqgLf.)

It goes without saying that just having a tight script that flows well, reads well and has highs, lows, crests, and troughs is not enough. It needs to be packaged well, and polished well, to make it shine through.

That’s, of course, where the craft of editing comes in—in both the newsroom and a movie production.

There’s a scene in All the President’s Men where Ben Bradlee (Jason Robards) shows the warranted brutal nature of editing:

The newsroom is bustling, papers everywhere, typewriters clacking away, phones ringing, and the frame is partly foggy with cigarette smoke under the grid of white light.

“Okay, let’s have it.” Ben picks up the draft stack, leans back in his chair, foot on the table. All while Redford and Hoffman look nervously at the impending fate. The red pen comes out, and the cuts and border notes start raining down immediately. 

In about 15 seconds, he throws back the draft with a deft “take it inside someplace”, recaps his pen, and walks away, not before the coup de grâce: “get some harder information next time.”

As brutal as it sounds, this edit did result in them digging more and finally cracking the story later on.

You obviously cannot superimpose this onto a movie edit and an edit room. They have to make do with what has been shot, and do not have the leeway (or in most cases, the budget) to go shoot again.

I went back to Making Movies by Sidney Lumet, and he opens his chapter on “Editing” with:

“For many years, the cliche about editing was:  ‘Pictures are made in the cutting room.’ That’s nonsense. No movie editor ever put anything up on the screen that hadn’t been shot.”

Sure, a journalist can always go back and dig deeper, talk to more people, and take another shot at the draft, unlike a director giving his editor the raw footage. 

But having said that, surely the overarching principles of structures, flows, and tempos are the same across both spaces. 

Yes, before you come at me, a disclaimer that I forgot to place before I wrote all this: I am NOT a journalist (stating the obvious here) and am in no position to pass comments. 

These are just observations from a movie-obsessed, letterboxd-obsessed mind who happened to serendipitously stumble into a newsroom, and is trying to connect the dots.
