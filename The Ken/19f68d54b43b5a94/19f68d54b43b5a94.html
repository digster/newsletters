Amit, 21, a computer science student from Mumbai, was clear that he wanted to do a Master’s in cybersecurity. After weighing up dozens of options, he settled on a university in Canada. 

The university mandated a four-year undergraduate degree for admission. Amit only had a three-year Bachelor’s in computer applications. No problem, he thought. He had already found a workaround: a Canadian college was offering a one-year cybersecurity certificate course with enough credits to make up his eligibility shortfall. 

All he needed was the money to pay for it. He rang a friend who took him to a friend who worked at Credila. And just like that, he had secured a Rs 20 lakh loan. 

He was about to fly out when the Canadian government derecognised the college. In one fell swoop, Amit was left picking up the pieces of his shattered plan and Credila was left sweating about recovering its loan.

The derecognition of the college was part of a wider crackdown on institutions luring foreign students with lax admission norms. The North American country is seeking to limit the intake of foreign students and is also tightening visa and immigration rules to that end. The US and the UK are erecting similar barriers to entry.

For students like Amit, this means a narrowing of opportunities to study, and eventually work, abroad. 

For NBFCs focussed on education lending like Credila, it means a fraying business model…
