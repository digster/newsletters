Dear Ishan, we are three days away from autumn! Good morning!
Yes, 23rd September is the autumnal equinox, the day the sun rises and sets exactly in the east and the west, and divides our day into equal 12-hour halves. From then on, the sun tracks progressively lower in our skies, first through autumn and then winter.
As it does, you can observe it in the way its light falls during the day. A phenomenon called Rayleigh scattering causes bluer skies during the day and warmer, golden skies in the mornings and evenings (golden hours!).
Septembers are usually Bengaluru’s wettest month, though this year has been pretty average. Otherwise it’s when our many lakes get filled up, all tanked up till next year’s pre-monsoon rains.
But autumn isn’t the same in the rest of India. Its sights, its emotions, and its music are all different.

I’ve warned you earlier that as The Ken’s 10th anniversary approaches, I cannot help but become reflective and “big picture” in my thinking.
In fact, I may have a special case of the Baader-Meinhof disease, also known as the [frequency illusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frequency_illusion). As in, I’m noticing things more often related to “decades” and “long-term journeys”.
For instance, a few hours ago, I came across a post on social media where a person had put down their thoughts on media business models in India, including The Ken.
They compared Netflix with The Ken, wondering why tens of millions of Indians didn’t mind paying for their monthly streaming subscription even as The Ken, after a decade, was still fighting for a fraction of that.
That’s easy. One is entertainment and the other is business news. The categories themselves are many orders of magnitude apart in scale and interest to the average consumer.
Like, you could replace The Ken with the world’s most successful news organisation and still make the point. Why is the The New York Times, after 175 years, still fighting for a fraction of Netflix’s subscribers (13M versus 325M)?
But there was a line in their post that made me… smile.
“…The Ken’s own decade-long grind shows how hard India’s willingness-to-pay-for-news problem really is.”
Can you guess why? Of course you can, I gave it away in the headline, didn’t I!
Ha ha, sorry.
But seriously, it only looks like a “grind” from the outside. Yes, we’ve worked hard, been through tough, sad, angry, despondent, and even existential crises (multiple times) over the years, but for many of us who have been around for the most part of the decade, these were also our most satisfying and fulfilling years.
The last ten years have been both our purpose and our moat.
From our first stories and subscribers in October 2016, we’ve been tweaking and fine-tuning the two sides of our business model: doing things that will be loved and valued by us and our subscribers. We’ve launched, revamped, killed, relaunched, bundled, and unbundled too many things to remember. Sometimes we did it because subscribers didn’t value them enough, but equally, also when we didn’t enjoy doing them enough.
The grind was the purpose. The hard years weren’t just the cost of the business, they were the business. The difficulty was—is—intrinsic to the worth.
Trusted, original, and independent journalism is hard because gaining trust is hard. If it were easy, it wouldn’t be worth much (to either the one giving it or the one receiving). It wouldn’t be ours.
Thus, over time, the difficulty selected for the people (on both sides) who care.
Seen this way, the last ten years are also our moat. I want to be careful here not to imply any hubris or overconfidence against better, nimbler, or younger competitors we’re yet to see. But I am confident that on the topic of building trust (inarguably the single biggest asset in the news and media space), our decadal “grind” has given us an asset that cannot be replicated using money or tokens.
But I digress. The grind, for me, isn’t something to be glorified and celebrated in hindsight. Not in the way “hustle culture” celebrates it. Hustle writing treats the routine as the price for success. I’m saying it is the asset.
It is the predictable, reliable, stable, and compoundable space you return to every Monday morning. Something to look forward to.
One of my favourite examples of this is from a First Principles podcast episode I did with Amrish Rau, the CEO of Pine Labs, [in November 2022](https://the-ken.com/podcasts/first-principles/amrish-rau-pine-labs/).
If you believe in the theory of the grind merely being the cost of winning the prize that follows it, Rau should be a great example of that.
In 2016 he sold Citrus, the company he’d co-founded, to Naspers-owned Payu for $130 million in cash. By any outsider’s measure, that is the prize. He says the price never bothered him. What bothered him was losing the thing he could go back to every day, and he counts taking the prize as the one regret of his career. That’s your “return” leg in a founder’s own words: the horse you can hug every morning matters more than the race you might win with it.
Rohin: If you could go back in time and change one thing about any of your major decisions in your entrepreneurial career, what would that be?
Amrish Rau: I wouldn’t have sold Citrus.
Rohin: Why do you say that?
Amrish Rau: So again, people talk about it where Citrus could have been valued this or valued that. It never bothered me on that topic. What bothered me till Pine Labs happened is, at the end of the day, you need your own horse, you need your own carriage. And you need to be able to hug it every day and saying, this is mine. When I sold Citrus, I lost that. And that made me so uncomfortable. It’s not funny. Now, when I look at what I’m doing with Pine Labs, I really believe I have been able to get that all over again. And that’s why I’m completely satisfied with where I am. But that’s one decision in my entrepreneurial career, which I will regret forever.
The horse in the race he can hug every day is, to an outsider, the grind. From the inside, hard work done with autonomy and skill feels like play (remember Csikszentmihalyi?).
And remember the Infinite Game?
Finite players play to win, infinite players play to… keep playing.

This is Vishnu with a couple of great documentary picks for the week.
With all the talk of AI planning and plotting to end the world as we know it, I thought it’d be a great idea to watch one of my favourite documentary duos last night—and I did.
This third re-watch cemented my view of both documentaries, and I thought I’d recommend them to you, in case you’re looking for one 🙂
P.S. Both are on Youtube, for free.
AlphaGo (2017)
No documentary (well, apart from Senna) has hit me harder than this one.
I had never heard of the game of Go, and had NO idea what it was, but after an hour and a half, I was utterly shocked and completely invested.
It showed me what humans are capable of, and what we can’t. It showed me what it means to lose, to admit defeat.
You can watch it [here](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WXuK6gekU1Y).
The Thinking Game (2024)
I remember the first time I saw AlphaGo, and how insanely difficult it was to figure out what I was feeling. Up until then, I had never felt such a strong pull to understand what “intelligence”, “humanity”, or “science” actually meant in the context of AI.
Eight years later, I watched its spiritual sequel, from the same team.
But this time, with a mixture of regret, fear, hopefulness, and joy at what Demis Hassabis and his team at Deepmind have been able to figure out.
I hope watching it gives you the drive to get back to learning, reading, coding or whatever your calling is 🙂
You can watch it [here](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=d95J8yzvjbQ).
