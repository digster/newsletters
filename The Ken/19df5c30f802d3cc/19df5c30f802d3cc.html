Are you wearing jeans right now? Which brand is it? What is the first thing you think about while buying jeans? 

Recently, I had an interesting interview with an apparel brand’s founder whose insights made me take a closer look at my closet. 

Ravi Punjabi said that Indian men give “brand” the highest priority, followed by “fit”, and then “price”. For women, fit comes first, then price, and brand comes last.

And sure enough, my husband’s jeans were all from the same brand, but mine were all from different ones. 

Punjabi, founder of Kraus Jeans, highlighted this “dualism” in denim-shopping behaviour between men and women. He has been in the business of selling jeans to women for more than 20 years. 

This insight helped him create a brand aimed at women who never prioritised brands. Making them care about the thing they valued least meant bringing “fit” and “brand” together. 

Denim is the new black

The denimwear market is exploding in India and global brands are eyeing it. London-based Pepe Jeans wants India to become its largest market, while for San Francisco-based Levi’s, India is already its sixth-largest market.

But just being the men’s go-to brand isn’t going to cut it anymore. 

If you flip the pages of history, the share of women’s denimwear has remained low or in “single digits” for the last two decades. 

In fact, in India’s $5.4 billion denimswear market which is expected to reach $9 billion in the next four years, women account for only about a 10th of sales, according to a report by The Knowledge Company, a Gurugram-based research consultancy firm. 

But the myth that jeans are a fading trend has been busted. It will certainly take time, but over the next five years, women could claim a 30% slice of the Rs 30,000 crore jeans market. 

These numbers make sense. That low base means plenty of room for growth. Sure enough, the denimwear industry in India is growing at a compounded annual rate of 16%, against a global average of 2%, and

Punjabi’s brand is taking full advantage of this, and he claims they are now “outselling” global denim powerhouses like Levi’s and Pepe Jeans in this category at large-format stores like Shoppers Stop, Lifestyle, and Pantaloons. From the onset the Thane-based brand spent on building scale via partnerships and now it has over 1,300+. 

This could explain why brands like Pepe Jeans, Wrangler, and Levi’s are opening flagship stores one after another. In 2023, Levi’s opened its largest Asian store in Bengaluru, and a year later it opened another in New Delhi that was even bigger.  

But scale and brand name are not the keys to unlocking higher growth in women’s denimwear. 

The way to a woman’s closet

It’s the fit. And the fitting depends on body type. Western jeans don’t comfortably accommodate Indian bodies, especially women’s. We are much curvier and shorter in height, so the construction of jeans in terms of inseam, hip-waist ratio, and rise needs to be redesigned. 

And unlike the West, where jeans are a workwear staple, in India ethnic wear has been, and still is, very work-wear friendly. 

This was a nuance that Punjabi understood. 

“The next generation prefers comfort over fashion. So we as brands had to change. We had to get out of skinny jeans, and offer whatever they liked—cargos, Korean pants, and so on.”

This understanding proved favourable to the business. 

In fiscal year 2025, Kraus, which primarily sells denimwear to women, claims its total sales stood at about 425 crore. 

Compare that to Levi’s, and Pepe Jeans, their total sales stood at Rs 2,200 crore and Rs 545 crore respectively, per data sourced from business intelligence platform Tofler. Of this, about 10–30% comes from womens-wear per market analysts. That would mean Levi’s reported anywhere around Rs 220–660 crore in sales of womenswear, while Pepe reported anywhere between Rs 50 crore to Rs 150 crore in sales. Both the companies don’t give breakdown of revenue category-wise. 

Ace Turtle, which sells Lee and Wrangler in India, revenues stood at about Rs 300 crore in fiscal year 2024.

For the Thane-based brand, however, most part of its lifetime has been spent on building scale via partnerships. Its 1,300+ relationships with large format and multi-brand stores include big names like Shoppers Stop, Lifestyle, and Pantaloons. 

“We have consistently been the highest selling denim brand for women on Shoppers Stop’s tables.”  

Ravi Punjabi, Founder, Kraus Jeans

With eight stores in fiscal 2024 and a primary revenue of 180 crore, they fell under Kewal Kiran Clothing’s radar. The publicly-listed apparel company that owns Killer and Lawman wanted to enter the women’s segment and Kraus was the perfect fit. Of this, about 10—30% reposted anywhere between Rs 50 crore and Rs 150 crore. Neither company provides a category-wise revenue breakdown. 

Apart from fit, another advantage is the price point. With an average selling price of Rs 2,000, Kraus sits in the middle. Levi’s sells jeans at an average price of Rs 4,000–5,000, and value players like Zudio retails jeans between Rs 500–1,000. 

In a market growing faster than almost anywhere else in the world, and with a generation of Indian women finally being designed for rather than designed around, legacy brands need to alter their 170-year old stitches for the seams are splitting in the challenger’s favour for once.
