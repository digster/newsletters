Good Morning Ishan,
The first diaper I ever had to deal with belonged to my two-year-old niece. For a 22-year-old, it was a surprisingly messy affair. It also makes you briefly wonder whether having kids is really such a great idea.
There was a time when the humble langot—a U-shaped reusable piece of cloth wrapped around infants defined childcare in India.
I never thought a pack of diapers could say so much about how India shops.
Then my two-year-old niece ran out of them. My brother, now based overseas, was puzzled when I said I’d order them on Blinkit. Twelve minutes later, they were at the doorstep.
Those 12 minutes are exactly what’s reshaping India’s diaper market and eating into Firstcry’s diaper business. My colleague Nuha [wrote](https://the-ken.com/story/firstcrys-winning-formula-is-running-out-of-road-it-keeps-doubling-down-on-it-anyway/) about how quick-commerce platforms like Blinkit and Zepto are changing the way parents buy everyday baby essentials, forcing Firstcry to cut prices as more consumers bypass its stores and app for routine diaper purchases.
That raises an interesting question for Swara Baby, India’s largest contract diaper manufacturer with a 37% market share, which has [filed](https://www.bseindia.com/corporates/download/385326/IPO%20Prior/DRHP_20260702074605.pdf) papers for a Rs 1,000-crore IPO.
Unlike Firstcry, which sells diapers, Swara makes them.
Swara’s top ten customers accounted for 80.3% of its FY26 revenue. Over 20% of which is owed to Firstcry. The previous fiscal year was 84.5%, down from 85.6% in FY24. Dwindling, but not by much.
“Any failure to maintain our relationship with these customers which leads to one or more of these customers choosing not to source products from us or reduce the volume of products sourced from us will have an adverse effect on our business, results of operations, financial condition, and cash flows,” it says in its DRHP.
Firstcry is one of those customers in question. And also the one who buys the most diapers from it. But it is also at the other side of the table, where it owns around 77% of it, and is now looking to partially cash out through an offer for sale worth up to Rs 300 crore in the IPO.
The diaper manufacturer’s DRPH states that its transactions with the fellow subsidiary is conducted on an “arm’s-length” basis, though the prospectus does not disclose whether the pricing mirrors that of other customers.
“The question isn’t whether Swara is a good manufacturer. It’s whether investors are buying a standalone manufacturing company or another way to bet on Firstcry,” said a Mumbai-based investment banker familiar with the IPO, who declined to be named as the process is ongoing.
Meanwhile, Firstcry is finding that the diaper business isn’t as absorbent as it once was.
During the December-quarter earnings call, management [said](https://cdn.fcglcdn.com/brainbees/firstcry-ir/Q3_FY26_Earnings_Call_Transcript.pdf) diapers was the category under the most pressure, hurting both growth and margins as parents increasingly shifted to quick-commerce platforms for routine purchases, forcing the company to cut prices. The diapering segment accounts for 15% of Firstcry’s gross merchandise value (GMV).
“Manufacturers don’t fight the retail battle directly, but they rarely escape it. If Firstcry’s diaper volumes come under sustained pressure, that will eventually flow upstream to the manufacturer,” said a Mumbai-based executive working for a baby-care company.
When Swara was incorporated in 2016, it was promoted by founder Alok Birla and a group of individual investors. In 2023, Brainbees, the company behind Firstry got on board, owning over three-fourths while Birla’s stake has fallen to just 1.4%.
The dependency extends beyond equity ownership; the parent company has also extended unsecured loans, amounting to Rs 99.8 crore, to Swara that are repayable on demand.
For now, the relationship has worked well.
India registers [roughly](https://censusindia.gov.in/census.website/data/VSREPORT) 25 million births every year, according to the Registrar General of India—an addressable population of roughly 75 million children under the age of three, the core consumer base for disposable baby diapers. It’s also a market Firstcry wants a bigger slice of.
Instead of merely retailing diapers, Firstcry captures a larger share of the value chain by owning the company that manufactures its Babyhug private-label products. The model offers manufacturing scale, predictable volumes, and greater control over costs, while providing Swara with a dependable anchor customer.
Yet that hasn’t insulated Swara from business realities. Profit after tax declined in FY25 before recovering in FY26, with the company attributing the temporary pressure to higher raw material costs. The business’ sensitivities to commodity prices remain: material consumption accounted for nearly 70% of revenue in FY26.
Swara, however, is betting that its future extends beyond baby diapers. Although the category still accounts for nearly 80% of revenue, the company is expanding into adult incontinence, feminine hygiene, and overseas markets through acquisitions and its newly incorporated US subsidiary.
Quick commerce cuts both ways. “If it pushes more consumers towards Firstcry’s private-label diapers, Swara stands to gain because it makes them. But if it permanently erodes Firstcry’s overall diaper volumes, the pressure eventually travels upstream,” said the previously-quoted executive.
Diapers may have moved from the langot to Blinkit in under 12 minutes. Swara Baby’s challenge is proving that, in a market changing this quickly, being backed by Firstcry is enough of an advantage and not too much of a dependence.
