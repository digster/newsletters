India is growing more opium than it has in years. But it is struggling to find anyone who wants to turn it into a modern pharmaceutical business.
Over the past five years, the area licensed for opium cultivation has nearly tripled, to 15,000 hectares from 5,500 hectares in 2021. The government [increased](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=2165939®=48&lang=2) licensing eligibility by 23.5%, adding 15,000 more farmers. It is also trying to bring private companies into processing, hoping to move beyond its century-old monopoly over the crop.
That has produced an odd outcome.
When the government invited private players in 2023 to process concentrated poppy straw (CPS), a more modern intermediary method of harvesting the crop, the contract went to Sunlite Alkaloids, a company incorporated only that year, sources told The Ken. Its directors, Nikhil Bansal and Paridhi Agarwal, have no pharmaceutical background; their other business is brewing beer.
This is despite the fact that Indian pharmaceutical companies already have experience with the business overseas. Sun Pharmaceutical, India’s largest drugmaker, for instance, owns subsidiaries in Australia and Hungary involved in alkaloid processing and has access to proprietary poppy seeds. Yet when India opened its own CPS processing business to private players, the winner was a company completely new to pharmaceuticals or narcotics processing.
This points to a larger problem with India’s opium revival.
