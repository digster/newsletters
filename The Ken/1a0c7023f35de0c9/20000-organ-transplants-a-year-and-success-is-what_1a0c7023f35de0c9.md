Good Morning Ishan,
In late July, one of Bengaluru’s newer private hospitals performed a double-lung transplant. Immediately after the surgery, the lead transplant doctor sat down with the patient’s family, who had flown in from central India, and told them in a confident, reassuring tone that he was “very happy with the surgery”.
To drive the point home, he said they would all meet at the same place in three months. He even proposed bringing in a neutral observer, and said the observer would not be able to identify the patient as someone who had recently undergone the major operation.
Three weeks later, the patient, still in the hospital all along, died of post-transplant complications. The lead surgeon never visited him after the day of the surgery. The three-month meeting was just a figure of speech.
Around the same time, India had a number to report: 20,000.
On 3 August, India’s 16th Organ Donation Day, the National Organ and Tissue Transplant Organisation (Notto), the apex body under the health ministry that coordinates organ donation and transplants, said the country had reached a record high in annual transplants—nearly 2.5X the number a decade earlier.
India now performs the third-highest number of transplants in the world, after the US and China. The volumes tell a story of progress. They do not say whether any of these transplants worked for the patient.
