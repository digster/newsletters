Good Morning Ishan,
Oceans bring in waves, sand, shells, seaweed, the odd piece of plastic. On some beaches, they also bring the internet.
Rushikonda beach in Visakhapatnam, Andhra Pradesh, will soon be one of them. On an April morning, it’s buzzing with tourists who wade into the surf, ignoring the signboards warning them of the rough water. Nearby, a group of local fisherwomen patiently wait for their husbands to return from the sea.
In just a few years, tech giant Meta’s Project Waterworth—the world’s longest undersea cable—will land at this beach, directly connecting India to the US, Brazil, and South Africa for the first time. It will come ashore the same way the rest of them have: a manhole and a marker, with a nondescript landing station being built in the distance.
On the other side of the country, Mumbai’s Versova beach offers a ready picture of what the new landscape will look like. Here, six of the country’s 17 international cables come ashore through beach manholes, with an occasional warning sign, and sometimes, a low fence. There are no armed guards, no exclusion zone, and no law saying these cables must be protected.
Versova remains a free-for-all public beach. That is to say, undersea cable or not, very little changes in the landscapes where these cables land. And that, precisely, may be the problem.
After all, these cables are critical infrastructure. About 95% of everything you do on the internet travels through them. Critically, India’s over $340 billion services-export economy is dependent on them. When the cables work, nobody notices. But when they break, entire regions slow down.
And that threat is hardly hypothetical anymore…
