Why do India’s gig workers love a job they’re desperate to leave? A new report on gig economy workers of Karnataka reveals interesting numbers and here's how to read them

“There is no pride in this. Kya karoon (what can I do), I’m just a delivery boy.”

Sid Pai, co-founder of Bengaluru-based consulting firm UK & Co, said that to me when I pressed him to explain a couple of surprising and paradoxical findings from his report. Here’s a sample of them:

95% of gig workers are happy with the income they receive.

52% of gig workers have zero savings.

And another:

84% of people surveyed depend on gig work as their primary source of income.

75% of them indicate that they do not wish to stay on for long 

I suppose I must start at the beginning. 

Sometime last week on The Ken’s Slack channel, someone shared a link to a report titled India’s Gig Economy: The Promise and the Paradox. It was created by UK & Co, a Bengaluru-based consultancy, and was based on conversations with 1,355 gig workers across Karnataka. Some of the questions in there are expected: how much money do you earn, are you happy, have you faced accidents or health crises, etc. 

But others weren’t so obvious: stuff like what your screen time looks like, what your long-term goals are, how many people live in your house.

It was such a fascinating report, filled with paradoxes, that Rahel and I decided to invite Sid over for an episode of Two by Two. And well, we had a great conversation. 

Here are some of the best parts from it:

Job creators without the responsibilities of it

One of my biggest frustrations of the whole gig-work conversation is the whole “this is exploitation” vs “but they are getting jobs at least”. I find it particularly tiresome. 

When I asked Sid how he thought about this, he said:

“If you were to set up a factory, you’re looking at the welfare of the people that are working over there. All these questions come into mind—how are they, how are their children, how are they saving, what are they spending everything on, what should be their salaries? We would normally think about all this when you’re setting a manufacturing setup. You would want to ask the same kind of questions to these people because at the end of the day, there’s no difference between the two.”

On educated workers choosing gig work

At one point, I passingly referred to gig-work as “low skilled”, and Sid immediately interrupted me to clarify with a personal story:

“I have somebody working in my office now who was earlier working with Rapido and now is doing high-end ETL transformations for one of the AI things that we are trying to push out. […] It kind of pushes the boundary in terms of why somebody takes up this job. But yes, it also means that there aren’t too many opportunities in the formal economy for graduates. It kind of underscores a larger issue—we are not, we do not have enough jobs in the market for graduates at this point in time. Which is a scarier thing, actually.”

On why there’s no worker NPS

The report had a particularly damning statistic: 

Sixty-one percent of the gig workers said they would not refer their friends or family to join gig work.

Long-time readers and listeners of Two by Two will know my views about NPS, and I decided to talk about it with Sid. So we discussed my favourite topic i.e. product managers and why NPS surveys exist for customers but not for workers. 

“It’s by design. There is enough supply, there is no problem. Somebody or the other will come and deliver. So there is no reason for them to recommend. That same product manager need not worry about this NPS over here. He’s looking at repeatability of customers over there. Not really caring for repeatability over here. It’ll come, it’ll go, no problem.”

But he also illustrated a deeper point.

On pride

“There is no pride in this. There is no loyalty to the platform in any way—they switch apps very often. […] You’re in trouble, you come here, do this for some time. But there is no ‘Oh come here, your future will be secure’. I think it’s by design as well. There is enough supply, there is no problem.”

At the very end, we asked Sid how this report changed him. 

And well, he said something pretty simple:

“I’ve started to tip more. Because what you realise is that they don’t make enough. There is an option to tip—a lot of these apps are being fair and the tips are actually given to the riders themselves. So I think we should tip more. As a country only we should tip more […] I think personally, after seeing this report, that has been my personal change. And I think that is my request to everybody who’s using this.”

I’ve started tipping more.
