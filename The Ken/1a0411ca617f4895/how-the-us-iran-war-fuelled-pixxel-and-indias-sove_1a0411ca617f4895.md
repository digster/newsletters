Good Morning Ishan,
“For every customer who knows how to analyse a hyperspectral image, there are 99 who don’t,” said Awais Ahmed, chief executive of Pixxel.
That is not an easy customer base for a satellite company. Especially one that spent its first five years learning to build satellites that could capture hyperspectral images, and must now convince customers to pay for what they see.
It has made a reasonably good start. Pixxel has raised nearly $96 million from investors including Google and Lightspeed—roughly one-sixth of the nearly $650 million raised by Indian spacetechs since the sector opened to private players in 2020. Its first commercial constellation went into orbit in 2025; its next is due to start launching in early 2027.
It has also built a US business. Pixxel is headquartered in India but has an operating entity in America, and roughly 90% of its revenue comes from outside India. It has also won business from NASA and a major US defence agency.
Then there are the factories: its planned Megapixxel facility is designed to make up to 100 satellites a year in the 300-kg category.
It would be easy, looking at all this, to call Pixxel just a satellite maker. But Pixxel divides itself into Planetary Infrastructure, which builds satellites, and Planetary Intelligence, which sells data and analytics. Satellites, after all, are expensive machines with finite lives, whereas data can be sold repeatedly. And if customers cannot make sense of that data themselves, the company that owns both the sensor and the software has a chance to capture more of its value.
Surely, there is some geopolitical urgency to that opportunity.
