Healthify swallowed its disruptors. But can it digest them? The CEO on the two disruptions he can’t ignore: AI and GLP-1 drugs

Imagine getting fired and broken up with on the same day. Now imagine instead of crying into your pillow, you showed up to work the next morning, started a company with your ex, and somehow made it work. That is more or less what Healthify did. 

And this week on Two by Two, Praveen Gopal Krishnan and Rahel Philipose sat down with Tushar Vashisht https://www.linkedin.com/in/tusharvashisht/, the CEO himself, to hear exactly how a fitness app navigated that hurdle.

“Nobody would want to be the first user on a matrimony website because that means you are signalling desperation. And a desperate matrimony seeker is a poor-quality matrimony seeker.”

That’s how professor Srinivasan https://www.linkedin.com/in/srini108/ from IIM Bangalore explained the cold start problem of a two-sided platform. In this episode, that analogy ended up being about Healthify.

What makes this episode so fun is that it is basically one long analogy competition. A bicycle becomes an e-bike. Rahel rightly calls it an “analogy antakshari.” But beneath all of it lies a genuinely serious question: when two seismic disruptions hit your core business at the same time, do you survive them, or do they survive you?

What actually happened to Healthify

Healthify launched 13 years ago as an app where you could log what you eat, track your body weight, and get a coach. Then AI arrived and made that entire value proposition available for free. Rahel shared how she had been using ChatGPT as a food journal for months, and so had her parents.

Then came GLP-1 drugs: Ozempic, Wegovy, Mounjaro. These drugs suppress appetite at a biological level. They do not require discipline or calorie deficits, and they work. The molecule powering Ozempic has been under patent protection until now. But when that patent expires, generics will flood in, prices will crash, and weight loss will become cheap and biological. That was the second hit, and it struck directly at the heart of what Healthify does.

Now, most companies would need a moment to recover. But Healthify did not wait. It partnered with Novo Nordisk, the maker of Ozempic itself, and built a wraparound lifestyle program to go alongside the drug. The argument: research shows that people who take GLP-1 without any lifestyle support regain significant weight within a year of stopping. So the company is positioning itself as the thing that makes the drug actually stick.

Tushar’s framing for all of this? “These are super exciting opportunities for me. I feel supremely validated and grateful that both these powers are maxing out right now.”

The viva nobody signed up for

This episode felt like watching someone defend their PhD thesis in real time. Tushar is at the front of the room, composed, genuinely optimistic, and defending his product. On the other side, you have professor Srinivasan, who opens by saying he does not think AI is as disruptive as everyone makes it out to be, and then spends the rest of the episode asking 17 follow-up questions for every answer Tushar gives. PGK plays the ruthless examiner who keeps asking the same thing in different ways: okay, but why does it have to be YOU that wins this?

Healthify had been building its AI coach Ria since 2017, so the AI wave came to a company that already had a surfboard. But just because it co-opted the technology does not mean that it gets to be the winner. The hospitals delivering insulin did not win the insulin era. The pharma companies did. So who actually wins the GLP-1 era, and what stops a pharma giant from simply partnering with whoever has the most scale?

Tushar’s answer to this is the most honest thing he says in the episode. “It’s not a God-given birthright. We have to earn it.” He also, by the way, micro-dosed semaglutide on himself for four months to test the product. Dog-fooding his own company’s thesis by injecting it. Unhinged or impressive? You decide.

My honest read

I have never used Healthify. My approach has always been simpler in theory: eat a little less than full, move more, and trust home food. It has not fully worked, but it’s also never required a subscription. 

Something the professor said in the episode stuck with me. He says what ChatGPT actually did was not introduce AI into the world. 

“What the tsunami [ChatGPT] did was increase trust in AI. People did not trust AI before.”

Which is also why I think AI is the disruption Healthify can actually digest. But the GLP-1 piece is harder. Once these drugs become genuinely cheap, the internet fills up overnight with people sharing exactly what to do alongside them for free. Getting someone to pay for a structured program on top of a drug they are already paying for, in this economy, is a harder sell than the optimism in this episode suggests.

Tushar has answers, but he has three very unsatisfied examiners pressing him on each one.

The bicycle analogy starts the episode and ends it. Rahel’s final read is that this is Healthify’s shot at going from the dusty bicycle in the garage to the shiny Harley Davidson parked outside. Maybe. But a Harley still needs someone to choose to ride it.
