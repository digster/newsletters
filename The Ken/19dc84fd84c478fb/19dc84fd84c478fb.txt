1. The Great Churning

If there is one theme this newsletter has constantly adhered to, it’s a belief in the human spirit and our innate ability to create a future that is not obvious. Each week, we approach it from a different angle.

This week’s angle is both pragmatic and hopeful.

We live in times where the prevalent or default mood is somber. Education, jobs, careers, business models, and even knowledge itself all seem to be upended before our eyes, in real time.

It’s hard to feel hopeful about the future.

I agree that this is both valid and accurate. But not in totality. And not for everyone.

I have an interconnected “cycle” of ideas and thoughts I juggle throughout the week. Sometimes they originate as I’m writing First Principles on a Saturday afternoon, but sometimes they solidify from an earlier discussion or conversation I had during the week.

One of the two formative conversations that led to today’s edition was a two-hour conversation I had on Wednesday with Lal Chand Bisu, the indomitable co-founder and CEO of Kuku FM, a subscription-based audio and video platform. I was interviewing Bisu for the latest edition of the First Principles podcast, which releases tomorrow—Monday, the 27th of April.

The second was my lede topic at The Ken’s Friday All-Hands meeting.

Yes, we are living in times of scarcity, disruption, friction, and uncertainty—even chaos.

But Bisu and Kuku FM’s radical embrace of these times, especially AI, showed me how some companies are using this period to dramatically reshape themselves and get ahead.

Perhaps subconsciously drawing from that conversation, my All-Hands lede was titled, “The Great Churning.” I posited, perhaps even implored, to my colleagues that these are the best times for ambitious, talented, and smart professionals to embrace the environment and use it to upshift themselves relative to their peers.

One of the ways I visualise the current wave of AI (which is not even four years old, if we take ChatGPT’s launch to be its canonical founding moment) is as a churning.

As in, concentric spinning circles containing economies and sectors at the outermost edges, then companies and business models, and finally professionals and careers in the innermost circles.

There are centripetal forces pulling the best towards the centre, while centrifugal forces (not really “true” forces https://www.askaboutscience.org/post/centrifugal-force-and-centripetal-force) push away those unprepared or unwilling into a universe of slop and mediocrity.

In such a scenario, economies, companies, and professionals are all forced to redefine and double down on their raison d’etre—reason for existence.

The ones that emerge from this “churn” are significantly better for it—fitter, sharper, more resilient, and more unique.

I quote from a long and thought-provoking research paper by Xiao Ma, Georgi Iliev, Jay Bal, and Andy Yik Yin Chan titled, “Every Staff Is a Superstar and Everyone is an Entrepreneur.” https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/13678868.2025.2563425#d1e291

Mass entrepreneurship: everyone can become a CEO

Artificial intelligence does not merely augment existing roles; it empowers every employee to think and act entrepreneurially. By providing access to previously elite-level analytical and operational capabilities, AI and particularly agentic AI, capable of autonomous decision-making transforms every worker into a potential entrepreneur. Employees at every level can now launch new products, services, and innovations independently within their organisations, redefining leadership and democratising the essence of entrepreneurship.

[…]

Ultimately, the integration of agentic AI heralds a revolutionary shift in the entrepreneurial landscape. By autonomously managing repetitive tasks and sophisticated analyses, agentic AI transforms every employee into a potential CEO, capable of independently driving new initiatives, innovations, and strategic pivots. Organisations that effectively embrace this transformation reshaping their culture, management styles, and structures will position themselves as agile, resilient leaders in a rapidly evolving economy. Yet, as agentic AI becomes commonplace, the future of work will increasingly depend on how effectively organisations democratise entrepreneurial autonomy. Companies that seize this opportunity will not only foster innovation at unprecedented scales but also profoundly empower their workforce ensuring every employee truly has the opportunity, tools, and agency to lead.

But complacency, by both professionals and managers or leaders, could lead to a very real “danger”. Matthew Call, an associate professor in the department of management at Texas A&M University’s Mays Business School, argues that instead of leading to the emergence of new superstars, AI could merely entrench existing ones https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/hrm.70023.

Star Advantage: Employee Value Creation and Capture in the Age of Artificial Intelligence

A critical question facing organizations is whether AI will equalize or exacerbate performance disparities among employees. Although controlled experiments and archival data suggest that AI can disproportionately benefit lower-skilled employees, we propose that in knowledge work contexts, this relationship may be the opposite. Specifically, we argue that star employees—those with relatively high performance, visibility, and social capital—are uniquely positioned to leverage AI in ways that may widen rather than narrow performance gaps.

This counterintuitive outcome stems from three mechanisms. First, star employees possess superior domain knowledge that enables them to assess the quality of AI outputs across both personal and enterprise tools. Such an ability provides stars with opportunities to exploit the capabilities of AI tools for more strategic uses than their peers. Second, stars can achieve accelerated adoption and mastery of AI applications while maintaining relative privacy. Their existing skills and strategic outlook give them autonomy to experiment with a variety of AI applications that other employees may not have the desire or ability to investigate. Third, stars experience multiplicative effects when combining AI with domain expertise in ways that preserve their ability to capture value. These mechanisms build on Choudhury et al.’s observation that AI–human interactions are particularly complementary when an employee has “domain expertise, acquired through specialization and deliberate practice.” Star employees, by definition, already possess this expertise and can therefore realize disproportionate performance gains from AI adoption. In the following sections, we outline how each of these mechanisms disproportionately benefits star employee performance relative to others, and how all three are compounded by what we term the “AI-specific Matthew effect.”

What can and should organisations do about this?

Two things stood out to me in the many articles and papers I read.

The first was for leaders to realise they need to become coaches https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/13678868.2025.2563425#d1e291 (hat-tip to a good friend, who has been hammering this into my head for months).

Cultivating an autonomous culture: leaders as coaches

To effectively foster mass intrapreneurship, organisations must shift their corporate culture from hierarchical command-and-control to a flexible environment emphasising autonomy, experimentation, and learning. Managers must evolve from gatekeepers to facilitators, coaches, and enablers, who trust and empower their employees to act entrepreneurially with AI support. Harvard Business Review suggested AI-Augmented leaders will be a norm in the years to come. AI is not just a tool for efficiency; it is becoming a leadership exoskeleton, enhancing the awareness, wisdom, and compassion of leaders. By automating tactical tasks and providing real-time insights, AI allows managers to focus on the deeply human aspects of leadership: building trust, exercising judgement, and fostering empathy. This doesn’t only empower seasoned executives to lead with greater clarity, it also creates pathways for individuals who might not have considered themselves ‘natural leaders’. Technical experts, engineers, and analysts often valued for their depth of expertise rather than their people skills can now lean on agentic AI systems to augment their social awareness, communication, and decision-making. The result is a broader, more inclusive leadership pipeline, where leadership is no longer confined to the charismatic few but is accessible to those with curiosity, integrity, and the willingness to collaborate with AI. In this sense, AI democratises leadership as much as it democratises entrepreneurship.

Companies successfully embedding mass intrapreneurship explicitly reward innovative efforts. For instance, the multinational tech firm mentioned above established clear incentives for employees whose AI-supported ideas significantly advanced organisational goals. Successful intrapreneurs were publicly recognised, given financial incentives, or fast-tracked for career advancement. In turn, this sent a powerful signal to the workforce: entrepreneurship is not just permitted but actively celebrated, reinforcing an autonomous culture of continuous innovation.

The second is to start reconfiguring their organisation and team structures away from siloed, vertical roles to cross-functional roles. Here’s the (recently) former CEO of LinkedIn, Ryan Roslansky https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/worklab/ai-at-work-ryan-roslansky-on-how-work-is-really-changing.

I keep coming back to this idea that the old org chart is gone—linear ladders, predictable structures, long tenure. If that chart no longer works, what replaces it? How do leaders organize their teams now?

A lot of how work is structured today was designed for the industrial age, when the focus was efficiency and scale. Org charts emerged to bring order to that system and create predictability. But today companies need to operate more like massive startups— adapting quickly and moving people toward new opportunities and challenges. In that environment, traditional org charts can become a constraint.

Microsoft came up with a different way to think about this: the work chart https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/worklab/work-trend-index/2025-the-year-the-frontier-firm-is-born. It’s pretty simple. Organize your organization around the work that needs to be done.

We’ve started experimenting with that framework at LinkedIn. Historically, a product idea has had to move through separate functions across engineering, product, and design, and then separate layers within all those functions before it makes it into the hands of actual members. The complexity and time that takes doesn’t put us in the right position to succeed. But now, AI tools have made it possible for individuals to do work across those functions and silos. So we created a new role called a builder and recruited for it in a very untraditional, but straightforward way: send us an example of something you’ve built with AI. We didn’t focus on credentials like schools or previous companies—just whether you could use these tools to build something.

Six months in, we have a group of remarkable builders, many right out of college, who are already teaching us how to think differently, build differently, and organize around the work differently.

My personal recommendations—exhortations—to younger professionals are:

1. Acquire domain expertise deeply and aggressively.

2. Learn and use AI.

3. Actively seek and solicit mentors and coaches, instead of waiting to “be found”.

Embrace the churning!

3. Reading between the lines

Hey there! It’s Vishnu with the movie section.

Last week, my colleagues Vijay, Vidhatri, and I were in the edit room for the second episode of Intermission https://youtu.be/o64I_q5c7Fk?si=svbVvRjjGdmtW8O2—our newest video business show.

In one of the short breaks we take in between chapters, I asked Vidhatri if she wanted to “see something cool”.

“Did you read the album art piece that I wrote last week?”

“Notice the buildings in the background? Do you know where they shot this?”

Okay, now I am talking to you. Don’t scroll down. Look at the album art, the buildings, the shape of the roof. Try recollecting where you’ve seen this, from a top angle. 

Hint: you’d have missed it if you’re someone who skips the intro and credits of movies.

Remember the Warner Bros. intro https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=RAyro9wpGJc during the Harry Potter movies?

Until I knew what these were, I always thought they were houses. I still remember seeing this intro for the first time when watching Harry Potter and the Philosopher’s Stone with my sister on our home computer.

The wave-effect on the buildings, which transitioned into the rotating WB logo, with the “Hedwig’s theme https://open.spotify.com/track/1n8NKQRg8LVHy7oUhUgbFF?si=7376e0fa2314467b” playing in the background.

Shortly after, in another one of our breaks, Vijay brought up 87North Productions in a different context.

(87North Productions is an independent Hollywood production company founded by the insanely cool stuntman-turned-Director David Leitch [Atomic Blonde, Deadpool 2, Fall Guy] and his wife Kelly McCormick.)

He pulled an Uno reverse on me and asked:

“Have you seen their intro animation? Look it up on Youtube https://youtu.be/VLCO0YxCgeA?si=o7cmgbRxtMnJS3Fm.”

When I played it, I realised I had seen it before, but had not paid enough attention. Because there it was, right there:

“Everything you want is on the other side of fear”

The production house was set up by David Leitch with the strong belief that stuntmen could make technically brilliant, captivating movies—and that the art of capturing action on screen can be better.

(David also has a tattoo of the line on his left arm.)

We promptly went back to editing the episode after admiring the intro animation, but the quote stuck with me and went straight to my home desk board!
