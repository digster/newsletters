I do. I discovered the importance of self-regulation while working out, in the form of “RPE”.
I’ve been working out for many years. Through them, I’ve swung between fads, methods, programming, and injuries. Each time I come down with an injury, I try to understand what caused it so that I can avoid the same mistake.
Only to realise that there are many, many more ways to make mistakes.
My last successful learning was to create an online workout log into which I’d enter sets, reps, and notes in real time in the gym. It worked because I used the notes column to tell my future self what I did and how I felt on particular days that led to pain or discomfort.
Thus, over time, my future and present selves got a relatively decent handle on what patterns were causing recurring issues.
But that wasn’t enough. Because I was missing a column that allowed me to self-regulate at the level of every single set. And that is the RPE, or [Rate of Perceived Exhaustion](https://www.resiliencerehab.com.au/pain-management-blog-training-intensity-rpe/?srsltid=AfmBOoops4027m29eWU-Kc68WT0Hc7AfV2xkBXkMcISv824rxkxbEcV3).
Let’s see how it holds up for me.

Surely you’ve heard of the (by now clichéd and discredited) startup adage, “Strong opinions, loosely held”?
In case you’re lucky enough to not have, it originally implied a decision-making approach that favoured strong viewpoints in advance of strong evidence, which could be dropped or changed if subsequent evidence won. It [isn’t](https://feld.com/archives/2019/05/bad-entrepreneurial-cliches-strong-opinions-loosely-held/) as popular today as it was a decade ago.
The reason this adage survived for as long as it did is because of the contrasting nature of its argument—strong versus loose. It strikes most of us as contradictory, and naturally, we’re drawn to it.
Last week I stumbled into a cousin of this adage at a studio in Mumbai’s Khar suburb. I was in a studio with the AC turned down to unbearably cold levels, making me literally shiver by the end.
Sitting across from me was Riyaaz Amlani, a fellow Generation X-er (ours has to be the generation that ethnographers and cultural scientists decided to skip over) who decided to start a café called “Mocha” in 2001 after returning from the US, where he studied at UCLA.
“I did not want a place that I could feel proud of. I wanted a place that we could feel proud of as a city. We wanted to have something to do in Bombay, which could give it its soul. You went to LA, there were bowling alleys you could go to, there were bars you could go to, there were nightclubs you could go to, there were coffee shops you could lounge around in all day. There were museums that you could go and spend days and days in. I used to work in a museum part-time, and I used to see how people were coming to consume culture. And while they were consuming it, they just didn’t want to see it. They wanted to live it. They wanted to breathe in it. They wanted to soak it up, right? And I found that missing in Bombay. So I always wanted to do things in Bombay that Bombay could be proud of as a city,” Riyaaz said.
And he never stopped. He has been doing the same thing for 25 years, which is building restaurants. Impresario, the company he started in 2001, now runs more than 80 of them across 20 cities.
The full name of his company is Impresario Handmade Restaurants. Naturally, I had to ask him.
Rohin: Your company is called Impresario Handmade Restaurants. What does handmade mean in a country where most restaurant chains have essentially standardised over time? Standardisation is an imperative. What does handmade mean, and why does it exist in Impresario’s name?
Riyaaz: Well, handmade means something that you’ve built or crafted with your hands. Handmade represents some degree of craft and love and attention and a slowness in its making and attention to detail. When I was starting off, handmade seemed to have soul. And now that we have scaled up a little bit, handmade does seem a little bit like a folly or a vanity. But that’s our philosophy. No two Mochas were ever the same. It’s always been part of our DNA and something that’s going to stick around.
As we talked, the temperature kept dropping and the conversation, warmer. Few entrepreneurs take the death of a product or format lightly. Fewer still are comfortable talking about it.
Riyaaz was, though.
Rohin: Can I ask a follow-up question? What does losing a restaurant mean to you? What does it feel like?
Riyaaz: Nothing. It had to be said. It was said. Its utility is over now. Let’s move on. The itch once scratched, it’s scratched. It only then has financial implications, but not emotional reasons. But if we have to walk out of a property, then you have to have the discipline to say, hey, we don’t see potential here, we made a mistake, let’s count our losses and quit. Because if you’re egotistical about it, you’ll keep throwing good money behind bad money. And it’s not an easy decision, but once it’s made, there is no emotional damage.
I’ve rarely come across a founder who espouses both ends of the spectrum—building and killing—with such clarity. Deeply sentimental passion while building versus cold detachment while killing.
Most of us live our lives in the inverse of that contrast. Meaning, we’re deeply sentimental about sunk costs, titles, and past experiences but detached from the daily work we do.
Riyaaz models the harder and rarer combination that is total devotion in the making, zero attachment in the unmaking. But if we can learn to see letting-go not as loss but as hygiene (we can pour ourselves fully into something and exit cleanly), then the two aren’t in tension.
This week, I was also talking to some of my colleagues about how to separate our identities from our work products. Because Riyaaz doesn’t fuse his self-worth with the artefact of a restaurant, it is exactly why he can keep making new ones.
It’s really hard to see non-attachment as a creative-engine angle. But once we do, it’s a counterintuitive insight that opens our eyes. Detachment is what lets us keep building, not evidence that we care less.
If you’re still here with me, here’s another bonus gem from Riyaaz.
Being early is often held up as one of the strongest ways to win, but I’ve met many founders whose earliest businesses failed simply because they were too early. Most of them had the detachment to pivot or move on to newer ones, though.
The killer skill is synchronising an idea with its audience when the time is right.
Riyaaz: We, at the start of our conversation, were discussing the need to have spaces.
Rohin: Yes. When we were discussing Mocha.
Riyaaz: And Mocha was a third space. Mocha went through its ups and downs. We became very closely associated with Shisha. Which was actually just a sidelight, but it became the hero product. Even then, we had more coffee. We had 18 different varietals of coffee, which no one even today has. We were speciality coffee before the third wave became a thing. But it did not matter because people had not. Sometimes the idea comes before the audience, and getting the idea to arrive at the same time that the audience arrives is actually the biggest skill set. There’s no glory in being ahead of your time. But yes, in Mocha, the offering of the coffees was way ahead of its time. And when the shisha got banned in 2008, it almost killed us because nobody really came back for the coffee. Everybody wanted the shisha.
Rohin: Such a strong brand association.
Riyaaz: Yeah, I think. But our coffees are amazing. We were serving Jamaican Blue Mountain, we had Hawaiian Kona. We were serving it in French presses, not in espressos. We wanted you to taste. Now the world is coming around to pour-overs, but we were doing that a long time ago. We figured out, look, people really don’t care so much about things that you care about. You need to care about what they care about and not the other way around.

It’s Vishnu with a movie for your weekend—specifically for a night-in.
This Friday, just as we were packing our bags to leave for the day, a couple of our colleagues and I were talking about ‘Obsession’, which obviously prompted me to ask an unsuspecting Rohin (who was also on his way out) if he’d watched the movie.
Which is when he gave us the (shocking) revelation that he’s not a fan of horror. For everyone else, it was probably just a preference—but for me, it was an opportunity to suggest some great titles that might hopefully change his mind.
So, here I am with three horror movie recommendations for a great watch on a night-in (and hopefully change your mind about the genre, if you’re not a fan).
1. Bhoothakaalam (2022) – Malayalam
Malayalam for “past”, this is, in my opinion, the best horror movie to come out of Kerala, and probably Rahul Sadasivan’s best work (a tall order for someone who also made Bramayugam).
The eerie, tense, nervous plot pulls you in from the first frame, and the impeccable sound design and the editing really make for an exceptional watch (the third act and the climax are just near-perfect!)
Streaming on Sonyliv.
2. Andhaghaaram (2020) – Tamil
Meaning “darkness” in Tamil, this is my go-to title to suggest when people ask for an “underrated” horror movie.
Be warned—this requires your full attention, because it’s a wonderfully non-linearly woven story that’s part psychology, part black magic, and a full sleight-of-hand magic on the edit table.
I remember watching this in college and being in absolute awe of the smart screenplay that closes all the loops cleanly.
Enjoyed best with zero context—go in dark (hehe, intended). Streaming on Netflix.
3. The Wailing (2016) – Korean
Again, one of those movies that made me fall in love with the craft. I distinctly remember watching this alone in my hostel room on my Lenovo laptop with a broken screen, and experiencing horror and thrill like nothing else.
Since then, I’ve watched it at least four more times—on better screens with better headphones—but the eeriness and unsettling feel the movie gave me never changed. On the contrary, with each watch, my jaw has only been closer to the floor.
Probably one of my favourite Korean movies (again, that’s saying something!) Give it the respect and attention it deserves, and it will reward you with a scrambled brain :))
Streaming on Prime and Netflix (depending on where you are).
