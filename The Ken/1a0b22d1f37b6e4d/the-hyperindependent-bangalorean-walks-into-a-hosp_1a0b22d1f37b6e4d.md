Two By Two
Fri, 18 Sep 26
An abridged, narrative version of the latest episode of Two by Two, The Ken’s premium weekly business podcast.
Good Morning Ishan,
“What’s your theory of why all of this crazy stuff is going on?”
I was on our regular post-lunch coffee pickup with Rohin, and we were (like most Bangaloreans) wondering why on earth people were throwing [peptide](https://www.thehindu.com/sci-tech/health/why-was-a-peptide-party-banned-in-bengaluru-explained/article71413555.ece) parties and [raves](https://www.indiatoday.in/trending-news/story/bengaluru-hospitals-music-night-came-with-a-rulebook-internet-wasnt-comfortable-2978295-2026-08-24) in hospitals. Even for a city that’s generally known for doing outlandish things, it felt like someone had thrown a switch, and things had gone to a whole new level.
Why, though? What changed?
There were several explanations we batted around. Maybe it’s because Bengaluru is generally an experimental city. Maybe it’s because there are more startups that are getting more and more desperate for attention. Maybe people were just more bored. Maybe it’s because of the influence from Silicon Valley.
When you have a question with so many maybes, that’s when you know you have a Two by Two episode. Now the problem is that I’m not much of a party person. So, I spoke to a couple of people who live in the city and attend events like this regularly. And the more I discussed why they did what they did, the more I started to understand what was going on.
I call this person the “hyperindependent Bangalorean.”
The hyperindependent Bangalorean arrives alone at most events. They’ve moved to the city for a job at a startup. They live with flatmates they found on an app or by themselves in HSR or Indiranagar, and most of their waking hours go into doing work that they mostly enjoy, but not to the point where it’s their life’s purpose. They are self-reliant to a fault: they use ChatGPT to seek answers to most of their social dilemmas, teach themselves whatever they need, and increasingly treat their own bodies as another system to be researched and tuned without waiting for a doctor.
They are also lonely in a specific way—they want contacts, relationships, collaborators, someone to angel-cheque their idea or join their run club. They also want those people badly. But what they do not want is an unstructured evening, a concert, or a drink with acquaintances who could later become friends. They are hungry for a network but averse to company.
And tellingly, in the words of one of my guests in today’s episode:
So these are people who will fill a form but don’t want to get invited. Their idea of being social or getting accepted is filling a form. I don’t think they see value in getting invited to an event.
And it’s not like, hey, you’re getting an invite for this event only because you’re my friend. You know, it’s very weird because I think when we were growing up, we would love to get invited to social events and things like that.
Right now it’s all become about filling a form or buying a ticket.
And now, brands have noticed the hyperindependent Bangalorean. How they’re perfectly comfortable seeking new experiences, no matter how bizarre they sound. How they seem to show up, and how they talk about what they do.
To discuss what happens when the hyperindependent Bangalorean meets the brands, I had two wonderful guests.
Karthik Balakrishnan runs Underline Center, a community space in Indiranagar that hosts a film club, improv, board games and origami meetups, and which he describes as “a daycare centre for adults”.
Arpan Peter is the founder and CEO of Foreward Lifestyle and Experiences, a culture IP house behind the India Jazz Project and The Shift, a bar-culture platform whose Rs 4,000 Secret Shift evenings sell out before anyone knows where they are.
Together, we try to figure out what happens and why.
We also discuss whether events like this will last. Arpan thinks probably not.
There’s a reason why events and experiences are also called experimental marketing. That’s a term that we use for events and experiences. So I think people are going to continue experimenting. This hyper-independent Bangalorean is going to find a lot more avenues to seek validation or seek community, or however you want to put it. I don’t know if you’re going to see fatigue around this whole space at all because I think the possibilities are endless. And I don’t think brands are going to shy away from associating with it as well. So I think it all comes down to now, what’s going to be the scale of these events? Are they going to continue to be 50-people events?
Karthik agrees, but he’s more forceful about it:
I think they’ll die out. Because I think they fundamentally only work at 50-people scale.
So therefore, the thing to keep it sustaining has to be that there has to be a new set of people who are interested in putting these events together.
Oh, and we settle what a “rave” is once and for all.
Personally, I disagreed—in my mind, the hyperindependent Bangalorean is here to stay, and as long as they are here, the events in this city will only continue to get weirder.
