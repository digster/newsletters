Who exactly are retail investors buying from? As FPIs sell, promoters cash out, and IPO insiders reduce stakes, domestic investors keep absorbing the pressure

Foreign investors have sold more than $23 billion worth of Indian equities this year. Their ownership in NSE-listed companies has fallen sharply from recent peaks. The rupee weakened past 96 against the dollar earlier this year. Global investors, meanwhile, have shifted money towards markets like Taiwan and the US, where the AI trade still looks fresh.

India is taking the hit from several global shifts at once. Oil prices have risen again amid the expanding conflict in West Asia, which matters more to a large oil importer like India than to most major economies. The US Federal Reserve, expected to start cutting rates this year, now looks trapped by stubborn inflation. And global capital itself has started rotating back towards developed markets.

For a market that is already expensive by emerging-market standards, that combination is stressful. And yet, Indian markets still do not quite behave as if there is any serious stress.

Even sharp corrections find buyers surprisingly quickly, monthly SIP inflows remain enormous, and domestic institutional investors now own a record share of Indian equities even as foreign ownership keeps falling. India today has roughly 130 million unique equity investors, more than triple what it had four to five years ago.

One interpretation is that India’s markets have matured. Domestic investors, finally, are strong enough to reduce the market’s dependence on foreign capital.

The more interesting interpretation came from Kotak Institutional Equities’ Sanjeev Prasad in an interview with The Ken last year. “The DIIs are buying,” he said. “Everybody else is selling.”

That one line explains much more about Indian markets right now.

The mechanics are difficult to unsee. While foreign portfolio investments (FPI) have been selling, private-equity investors have been exiting, promoters have been steadily reducing their stakes, IPOs increasingly look like liquidity events for existing shareholders, and the booming unlisted market looks like a place where early investors offload expensive assets onto enthusiastic retail buyers.

One recent Jefferies note made the argument that India’s SIP boom may have become an exit route for foreign investors. It said nearly $78 billion has exited Indian equities over the past two years through FPIs, PE investors, and foreign promoters. Normally, that sort of selling would have caused much sharper market damage. Instead, domestic liquidity absorbed a meaningful part of the pressure. 

The stress showed up elsewhere: the rupee weakened, India’s balance of payments slipped into deficit, and foreign ownership in Indian equities kept falling even as markets remained relatively stable.

That changes how one should think about the great Indian retail-investing boom. About millions of first-time investors systematically buying into markets where many sophisticated investors increasingly seem happier selling.

Everybody discovered a buyer

The mechanics are visible almost everywhere.

Take IPOs. In bull markets, offers-for-sale usually rise because existing investors use strong markets to cash out. But India’s IPO boom stretched this logic much further. Domestic liquidity absorbed dozens of richly valued listings because oversubscription itself started signalling legitimacy.

Or take the explosion in thematic mutual funds. More than 600 new schemes have launched in just three years, many tied to whichever sector had already rallied hardest. Energy after the energy rally. Manufacturing after the manufacturing push. Defence after defence stocks took off.

The flows themselves became the investment thesis.

A generation of investors entered markets after 2020 having mostly experienced one key lesson: every correction eventually reverses. SIPs reinforced that psychology by turning volatility into averaging opportunities. A falling market stopped looking like danger and started looking like a sale.

That mentality had consequences. By mid-2024, one in every 10 stocks in the BSE 500 traded at more than 100X earnings.

Which created an odd inversion. The people most enthusiastic about Indian equities seemed to be households. The people closest to the markets professionally often sounded much more cautious.

The market became part of middle-class aspiration

Bull markets eventually became cultural events, rather than financial ones.

The Ken’s survey of investors captured this shift unusually well. One respondent casually mentioned owning “three cars and two bikes” because stock-market gains had changed how they thought about spending. Others talked about luxury holidays, second homes, business-class flights, and early-retirement plans.

Equities have become aspirational in the way real estate once was. SIPs started getting sold not merely as investment products but as pathways to financial confidence and upward mobility.

And to be fair, some of this optimism is rational. Indian households historically underinvested in equities. The rise of mutual funds and direct investing represents a genuine structural shift in household finance.

But such shifts create their own excesses.

Once investors begin believing domestic liquidity will eventually overpower every correction, valuation stops mattering as much. Expensive markets start feeling normal. “Buy the dip” becomes common sense because, for years, it mostly worked.

Which partly explains why SIP narratives became so emotionally powerful.

SIPs are marketed as discipline and long-term compounding. All true. But they also started being interpreted as protection from valuation risk itself. Sanjeev Prasad pushed back against this sharply in his interview with The Ken, arguing that returns still depend on the price at which investors enter markets.

So, India’s markets have a different kind of vulnerability now.

The problem that India had about not having enough domestic participation in capital markets seems solved. Retail investors and mutual funds now absorb enough liquidity that foreign selling no longer destabilises markets the way it once did.

But that resilience may also be masking a deeper transfer underway. Domestic investors are increasingly becoming the buyers that large investors, PE funds, promoters, and early shareholders sell to when valuations become attractive enough.

If Reuters is right https://www.reuters.com/world/india/india-stocks-set-first-yearly-drop-over-decade-foreign-investors-leave-2026-05-27/ and Indian stocks are indeed headed for their first annual decline in more than a decade, domestic investors have already ensured that everybody else got far better exits than they otherwise might have.
