The Collection
Fri, 27 Mar 26
Multiple stories, multiple perspectives, one theme worth your time—every week.
Good Morning Ishan,
Private-equity (PE) firms don’t typically buy cricket teams. They are supposed to stick to cash-flowing businesses, infra assets, and increasingly, hospital chains. And yet, just as the Indian Premier League (IPL) kicks off tomorrow, a Blackstone-backed investor group has bought Royal Challengers Bangalore (RCB) at a valuation of roughly $1.8 billion ([possibly beating KKR](https://www.reuters.com/world/india/global-private-equity-firms-bowled-over-by-indian-cricket-league-ipl-2026-02-17/) to it).
But is RCB really a sports team? Sure, it isn’t an obvious financial asset. Revenues are in the range of $50–60 million. Its on-field performance is famously volatile. The product depends on form, injuries, and the episodic collapse in the 18th over. And yet investors are willing to pay venture-style multiples for it.
What they are really buying is something else.
The IPL—an $18.5 billion enterprise with [a brand worth](https://cdn.hl.com/pdf/2025/ipl-study-2025.pdf) another $3.9 billion—has engineered a business model that looks less like sport. Media rights worth over $6 billion are sold centrally and distributed across franchises, giving each team a baseline of $50–60 million a year before they sell a single ticket or sign a sponsor. Add to that local revenues, including sponsorships, hospitality, and merchandising, and you get a structure where the downside is limited but the upside compounds with the league’s growth.
This is not how sports leagues usually evolve. Globally, institutional capital has been moving into sports for years—minority stakes in NBA teams, sovereign funds buying into European football, multi-club ownership models spreading across leagues. But even there, private equity arrived late, once the asset class was proven. The IPL, where wages don’t balloon as much and stadium debt is minimal, is [getting there faster](https://the-ken.com/moneyball_edition/chelsea-ac-milan-gujarat-titans-how-private-equity-is-cracking-sports-team-ownership/?utm_source=newsletter&utm_medium=email&utm_campaign=collection).
The tipping point, as it often is, was a transaction. When CVC Capital exited Gujarat Titans at a valuation close to $900 million—reportedly making several times its initial investment—it reframed how investors thought about cricket.
So, the teams are now being valued as assets. To investors, they look more like annuities. Up to 80% of revenues are effectively locked in before a ball is bowled, while salary caps keep costs in check.
Over the past few years, it has been doing something more than just buying Indian companies. It’s been turning entire sectors into financial products, often by taking something fragmented, or “unscalable”… and forcing it into a structure that can be packaged and eventually sold.
Private equity entered education by building systems that look like schools but behave like businesses.
In one case, an education platform preparing to go public revealed a striking detail: a majority of a school’s revenue was being paid out as rent to an entity owned by the same investor. It’s perfectly legal. Financially elegant, even. But it also tells you what the model optimises for.
As one of the first PE-backed edu-infra platforms, Elevate Campuses, prepares to go public, its draft papers offer direct insight into how private-equity firms squeeze money out of schools
In another case, the shift is subtler but just as telling. Spending priorities tilt towards what can be seen and sold, such as new facilities, sports infrastructure, and branding, while the less visible inputs, like teacher training and retention, take a back seat.
Falling budgets, lower salary hikes, and shrinking student numbers—this is what PE acquisitions bring to Indian schools
And then there is the aggregation play.
KKR’s Eurokids story is textbook private equity: take a fragmented sector, consolidate aggressively, expand across formats, and build a national platform. It worked financially. But as the network scaled, so did the tensions, over fees, over quality, and over the pressure to keep growing.
In just five years, the PE titan is setting its sights on a 5X jump in the school chain's value, riding on corporate direction and revenue boosts from recent fee hikes
Education can be structured. The question is what gets lost in the process.
Healthcare follows a similar path, but with higher stakes.
Here, PE is building platforms, stitching together chains through acquisitions, standardising operations, and creating scale across cities. Deal activity has surged, with buyouts accelerating as firms chase what the industry calls “platform assets”.
The logic is to buy Ebitda, improve margins, and benefit from multiple expansions. The execution is not so straightforward.
With 18 buyout deals and counting, PE firms' hunger for Indian healthcare assets will only increase. State and central governments are increasingly choosing to become just payers, but they can’t afford to be onlookers
Sometimes, the tension shows up in more subtle ways. In a newsletter, we looked at a US PE firm preparing for an India IPO, trying, unusually, to signal restraint. The pitch was almost philosophical: can a private equity firm grow without extracting every last bit of value? That question sounds naive. But it exists because the opposite behaviour has become so common.
Tenneco Clean Air proves that conservative pricing can still spark a strong listing
Healthcare works best when it is local and patient-centric. PE works best when it is centralised and optimised. The push-and-pull tends to surface over time, sometimes in pricing, sometimes in care, sometimes in trust. And increasingly, in specialisations.
Take IVF.
Private equity is building national fertility chains like Indira, Nova, Oasis, Ferty9, trying to standardise what has traditionally been a fragmented, doctor-led business. The opportunity is large, demand is rising, and regulation is still evolving.
But the model forces trade-offs.
In a category where outcomes matter as much as access, clinics are pushed to choose what to optimise for—brand or success rates, expansion or depth. And even as capital flows in, some gaps remain oddly persistent, like the lack of focus on male infertility despite it accounting for a significant share of cases.
Is scale possible here?
Infertility, now a public-health emergency, affects one in every six people. Private equity has found its asset-market fit in India and placed a US$1.1B valuation on Indira IVF, making it the world’s second-largest such bet
Pharma, by contrast, is where private equity looks most comfortable. Not in blockbuster drugs, but in niches.
Companies like La Renon have built scalable businesses by focusing on therapeutic segments too small for large pharma to care about. Over time, that strategy compounds, moving up the value chain, expanding exports, and steadily improving margins.
The company’s valuation has jumped 5X in five years by making what no one else is and selling where it works best
It’s exactly the kind of repeatable model private equity prefers.
We saw a different version of this playbook in our newsletter on KKR’s handover and Torrent Pharma’s moves, where control, timing, and positioning matter as much as the underlying business.
In pharma, as in private equity, value is often created between transactions, not just within them.
Markets are euphoric, but the two massive opportunities, semaglutide and CDMO, are anything but straightforward
And then there are sectors that resist being neatly structured.
Innerwear, for instance.
A PE-backed consolidation of brands like Dixcy and Enamor was meant to challenge Jockey with a familiar formula: capital, branding, distribution, and leadership. But consumer behaviour is harder to engineer than balance sheets.
Even with scale and investment, habits don’t shift easily. Not every category lends itself to clean roll-ups or predictable outcomes.
Under the watchful eye of Advent International, Modenik is floundering: missing targets, stumbling through integration, and shuffling leadership
Seen this way, IPL teams don’t look unusual at all.
A scarce asset, predictable cash flows, embedded growth, strong narratives, and enough room for structuring to create upside.
If PE can do it with schools, hospitals, fertility clinics, and pharma niches, why not a cricket league? After all, here, franchises are finally being treated less like billionaire toys.
