Make India Competitive Again
Mon, 18 May 26
What India is doing, will do, and should do—to not just survive but thrive in the chaos unleashed by Trump.
Good Morning Ishan,
In 2025, Trump’s tariff wars exposed how dependent India’s economy remains on exports.
In 2026, the war in West Asia further revealed that the country’s fuel systems were deeply vulnerable to import shocks.
The government is now trying to patch that hole.
On 13 May, Prime Minister Narendra Modi asked people to cut their fuel consumption. On the same day, the Union Cabinet approved incentives worth Rs 37,500 crore to gasify India’s coal reserves to produce fertilisers, chemicals, and feedstock. And the Department of Expenditure calculated that India’s fertiliser subsidy budget will surpass by Rs 70,000 crore.
Three reminders on the same day that India’s self-reliance ambitions still look more convincing on paper than in infrastructure.
India has the world’s fourth-largest coal reserves. To leverage this, it’s been acting on a plan (for six years now) for coal gasification, with a 100 million-tonne target by 2030, under the National Coal Gasification Mission (2020). It’s only achieved 5% of it.
China has already industrialised this playbook at scale, gasifying an estimated 340–350 million tonnes of coal each year.
In simple terms, coal gasification involves converting coal into synthetic gas (syngas) through partial oxidation at high temperatures and pressures. This output is useful for hydrogen production, power generation, and chemical and fuel production.
“[Syngas] is being positioned as a bridge technology in India’s coal transition pathway,” said Debajit Palit, head of the Centre for Climate Change and Energy Transition at the Chintan Research Foundation, and former director at TERI.
The first proper budgetary allocation for coal gasification came four years later in 2024, when the government allocated Rs 8,500 crore. Jindal was one of the companies to receive Rs 1,000 crore under this scheme.
From then till now, Jindal’s coal gasification plant, located within its steel plant in Angul, Odisha, is the only one with healthy numbers. The entire thing is a full-scale industrial operation, running at 80% capacity and posting more than Rs 3,000 crore in overall profits last year.
Every other project—from Talchar in Odisha to Trichy in Tamil Nadu; from Bharat Heavy Electricals Limited (Bhel) to Coal India Limited (CIL) to private companies like Western Coalfields—is yet to take off at scale.
But brighter days might be around the corner for syngas.
New Era Cleantech, an energy transition company, is developing the country’s largest coal gasification and carbon capture plant in Maharashtra. It had also received an outlay alongside Jindal in 2024. The current project has an investment of [Rs 20,000 crore](https://www.business-standard.com/amp/companies/news/new-era-targets-closure-for-20-000-cr-coal-gasification-project-by-dec-126051401443_1.html) over three years, starting in 2026. Once fully operational, it will have the capacity to produce 5 million tonnes of syngas per year.
Adani Enterprises, too, is pushing ahead with a massive coal-to-chemicals complex in Odisha, with investments of up to Rs 70,000 crore.
Why has one private company cracked this, while India’s coal gasification push still struggles to leave the ground?
Hint: Jindal may have solved the Jindal problem. India still has to solve the India problem.
