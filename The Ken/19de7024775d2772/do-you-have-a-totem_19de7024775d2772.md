Good Morning Ishan,
Arthur
So, a totem. It’s a small object, potentially heavy, something you can have on you all the time…
I’m not much of a Christopher Nolan fan, but the more I’ve been playing with AI, the more I find myself thinking about Inception, and of course, The Matrix (of which, like most millennials, I’m an outright fan, even more so after the AI revolution).
Now, I’ve been using AI for a while—in fact, I wrote about my [experience](https://the-ken.com/the-nutgraf/the-machines-are-here/) when I caught a glimpse of GPT-3 in 2020, way before it entered our lives.
However, until recently, I realised that I’ve been using it wrong.
For a long time, I used AI to expand the possibility space, i.e., more options, more ideas, more direction. It did this (mostly) well. Of course, it’d occasionally hallucinate or say random things, but I’d be able to catch it. That’s because I was the expert who’d evaluate the answers critically and mentally decide which ones were plausible and which were nonsense. Sure, I added context, memory, and all the other things at the start to give it direction, but even those were stuff I mostly used AI to help construct.
Ariadne
What, like a coin?
I suspect many people use AI this way. But I think I’ve encountered a problem with this approach…

Hi everyone! This is Vidhatri, the producer of Zero Shot. Hope your long weekend is going great! And if you are in Bengaluru, I hope you are enjoying the sudden and unexpected respite from the heat.
I want to tell you about a conversation we had last month. Avinash Raghava, the CEO of SaaSBoomi (now rebranded AIBoomi), stopped by for a recording and told us something startling: at least 40–50 companies in their network had to reinvent themselves entirely and change their models.
I remember everyone in the studio did a double-take after this. This was how SaaS companies were grappling with AI. These figures ground that in reality.
This conversation made us wonder: who are the founders who have reinvented themselves, transitioned smoothly into the AI space, and are playing the long game?
Sumanth Raghavendra, the co-founder of Presentations.AI and also the co-founder of The Ken, was the perfect example.
His story actually starts in 2005, long before ChatGPT and AI became household conversations. That year, he founded Instacoll, an online office suite in Bangalore. The product stuff was figured out, but they had no distribution. That was the first dead end. In 2012, he narrowed the bet to one thing: presentations. That became Deck, a mobile-first app. It was built on a simple insight—that people know what they want to say, but just need help saying it visually. Except they didn’t. People froze while looking at the slides.
Then came the hard work. Years of designing data and working with traditional machine learning. But hey, as the product was getting better, GPT arrived.
But instead of panicking, Sumanth’s team rebuilt everything. They learned from the past and the technology available to them.
The result: Presentations.AI now tops every search for “AI presentation maker”.
Sumanth tells us how they pulled it off.
