Before you stepped into a role, how did you imagine playing it? If you are a parent now, what did you think you’d be like, versus how you actually are? Or how did you think you would be as an employee versus how you are now?

Funnily, this question hit me on my first reread of the Mistborn series because the neighbourhood board game café got the Mistborn: The Deckbuilding Game (2024), and I wanted to pressure my partner into buddy-reading the series so I could play with him. 

I love board games as much as reading, but lately the former has preceded the other. There’s a community and a friendly competition to them that reading alone doesn’t give me, with the added fun of  engaging your mind to make the best possible move. 

Anyway, back to my question. In book one, Vin spends a lot of time worrying about what kind of Mistborn she will be, what kind of spy , and later, what kind of leader and wife. Hardly any of her fears come true. Neither do her hopes. Things just happen, snowballing her into whoever she becomes. 

We can tell ourselves, if ‘x’ happens, react with ‘y’. But that’s all it is, just telling. Unless ‘x’ and ‘y’ are already wired together in us, most of us won’t connect them when we are actually in that situation. 

I would like to think most of you can’t relate, because I am early in my career and early means it gets better, right? 👀

Willpower matters but only so much. It works when everything is perfect, when we can shut out every variable that we didn’t account for. A slight imbalance tips it over the edge if we are not careful. Because we are all inside a system, and that system isn’t always ours to control. 

Here’s a recent example. I somehow miss every other event The Ken hosts, including yesterday’s, always thanks to an impromptu trip home. Silly to be peeved about it, but here we are. 

Rohin and Vishnu have earned some downtime after a good Saturday at “Intelligence Independence,” so this edition is you, Sakshi, and a survey from The Ken’s editorial team. You are going to want to participate in this one.

2. From the FP community’s bookshelf

What are you reading this weekend? Why did you choose to read this book out of so many hundreds and thousands? Tell us here. 

Hareesha Rao – Illusions by Richard Bach

Illusions is my antidote for a day when I feel disillusioned. There is a reason for every season 🙂

Apaar Singhal – Source Code by Bill Gates

A lot has already been written by Bill Gates, but the book provides an interesting narrative on how it all started at Microsoft, what shaped Bill Gates in his childhood, and all the challenges Bill Gates and Paul Allen had to undertake to build their enterprise. Definitely a worthy read!

Sai Tejas – Dawn by Octavia E Butler

An excellent sci-fi book that deals with the concept of a benevolent alien species. It is also very layered, and does not paint the characters (or species) as black or white, but instead portrays them as they are: uncontrollably themselves. The first in the trilogy, it would be hard not to pick the second and the third books up once you’re done with the first. 

PS: you can think of how ethical pet ownership is, once you’re done with the trilogy (or even the first book).

(Sai Tejas)

Ajay P – Changel: The Biography of a Village by Arvind N. Das

Read this book to get a glimpse of rural India and its realities. It provides us with a view of India and its history from the perspective of a remote village, while portraying the intertwined and complex dynamics of caste, class and economics. 

Anuradha – Sea, Salt and Spice – a history of Mumbai through food by Meher Mirza

We have all heard and have opinions on Mumbai street food and think it’s just vada pao and frankies. However, in this book, Meher Mirza digs deeper and finds untold stories to make us want to discover Mumbai all over again. Mumbai is not just about the Portuguese or Gujaratis; it’s much more, and there are many more stories.

(Anuradha)

Raman Ramachandran – The Correspondent by Virginia Evans

A story told through letters written by the author to various friends and relatives. A unique way of telling the story.

Chitrang Negi – Demon Copperhead by Barbara Kingsolver

It’s a fast-paced, raw coming-of-age story of Demon (Damon), who is born in poverty to a single teen mother in rural America in the 90s. It’s a story of resilience, love, loss, and addiction. It’s set against the backdrop of the opioid crisis, institutional poverty, social stigma, and class divide. It’s a loose modern-day retelling of David Copperfield.

(Chitrang Negi)

And last but not least, if you still haven’t read Anxious People but have always wanted to, here’s a sign. 

Priti Gohel’s thoughts on Anxious People by Fredrick Backman

It’s funny, and it’s got a soul… It’s like Fredrick Backman sees people and understands emotions… He talks simply, and it leaves you with a warm feeling despite the plot.

(Priti Gohel)
