I sometimes use VPNs to watch Netflix shows that aren’t available in India. But until last month, I had never used one for Youtube.
As someone who spends an embarrassing amount of time on the platform, it was surprising that I’d missed a tech controversy. One that was purposely made invisible. Only after a video from British-Indian creator Arun Maini appeared on my feed did it catch my attention.
The controversy centred on AI+ smartphones, launched by deep-tech firm Nxtquantum, founded by former Realme CEO and co-founder Madhav Sheth. The company had marketed the phones as India’s privacy-first “Made in India” solution to rival Chinese brands. But content creators Techwiser and Techbar questioned AI+’s privacy claims by highlighting that the phones came with pre-installed Chinese-linked apps.
| Chinese apps pre-installed on AI+ phones with backend data sharing | Source: Arun Maini (Mrwhosetheboss) Youtube channel |
|
AI+ dragged them to the Delhi High Court, which restricted the creators, who have a combined subscriber count of over 8 million, from posting “disparaging content”. Their videos were even blocked in India.
But soon after Maini’s exposé, where Sheth admitted to [acting](https://www.freepressjournal.in/tech/indian-smartphone-brand-ai-faces-scrutiny-after-youtuber-questions-chinese-links-made-in-india-claims) in haste, the creators’ videos were back online.
Although the legal dispute continues, online noise has settled down. The phones have launched, and Techwiser seems to be even [endorsing](https://x.com/rghvgrv/status/2067924845220995355?s=46&t=0zutXLrieS5zt9bRR6QQNQ) them for affordability.
But the episode raises a broader question: why is it easier, now more than ever, for companies to approach courts when faced with criticism online?
