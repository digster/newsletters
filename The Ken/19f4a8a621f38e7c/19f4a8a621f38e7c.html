A few weeks ago, there was a headline I almost skipped because it looked incredibly boring. The National Payments Corporation of India (NPCI) had announced that Phonepe and Google Pay together processed 79% of India’s UPI transactions. 

That’s down from 86% two years ago, and, for the first time since anyone started measuring this stuff, the two biggest apps now account for less than four out of every five UPI transactions.

The change looks even stranger when you throw in Paytm*, the company many people had assumed would spend 2026 being a case study rather than a competitor. The top three have gone from controlling 95.2% of UPI transactions in January 2024 to 87% today. Paytm, against almost everyone’s expectations, has actually gained share over the past year, climbing from 6.8% to 7.9% (that’s a small comeback story for another day).

But before any of you get carried away, this isn’t exactly the fall of Rome. Phonepe still processes 46% of all UPI transactions. Google Pay does another 33%. UPI itself now handles around 23 billion transactions worth Rs 30 lakh crore every month, which means we are talking about two companies that still sit in the middle of the overwhelming majority of digital payments in India. The duopoly has just become… slightly less of a duopoly.

The reason that is interesting, though, isn’t the number itself. It’s that, if you had asked almost anyone two years ago how this duopoly would eventually weaken, they probably would have pointed to the regulation specifically written to weaken it.

The funny thing is that regulation has never actually regulated anybody.

Back in 2020, NPCI decided that no single UPI app should account for more than 30% of transaction volume. That is a fairly straightforward way to stop two companies from swallowing the market. Except the cap never arrived. It was supposed to come into force in 2021, then December 2022, then December 2024. Finally, on 31 December, 2024—the exact day the previous deadline expired—it pushed the deadline again, this time to 31 December, 2026.

That is a slightly absurd situation. That deadline is now less than six months away and both companies remain above the limit they have theoretically been preparing for over the last six years.

That is the part I can’t stop coming back to. The rule that is supposed to create competition hasn’t actually done anything, because it hasn’t ever been enforced. But competition showed up regardless.

It’s not that consumers suddenly woke up and decided they wanted a more pluralistic payments ecosystem. Markets almost never work like that. They moved because a bunch of companies, almost all at the same time, decided it was worth burning money and buying users again.

Look at who’s actually taking share. NPCI’s own Bhim came back with a Rs 1,500 crore relaunch and MS Dhoni plastered across billboards nationwide, all of which has so far translated into… about 1% market share. Which isn’t a criticism of Bhim so much as a reminder of just how expensive it is to move even a single percentage point in UPI.

The companies doing the damage are also a little unexpected. Sachin Bansal, best known for building Flipkart, whose former fintech arm Phonepe now dominates UPI, has turned Navi into a real challenger, growing its share from 0.21% to 3.6%. Flipkart’s own Super Money has gone from not existing to 1.8% in under two years. 

Whatsapp Pay has finally been let off the leash and now has Kunal Shah, arguably India’s best-known fintech product builder, running Whatsapp’s global business. Throw in RuPay credit cards, and suddenly the duopoly is fighting half the fintech ecosystem.

That is also the irony. NPCI spent six years trying to manufacture competition through a market-share cap that has, so far, capped absolutely nobody. The companies actually chipped away at the duopoly the old-fashioned way: cashback, credit cards, marketing budgets, and enough incentives to make switching payment apps feel worthwhile again.

The Ken has covered almost every one of those developments over the past few years. They never felt connected at the time. Looking back now, they weren’t separate stories at all.

Go back to 2019, when Google Pay first passed Phonepe to become the single biggest UPI app, and NPCI got nervous enough about a foreign platform owning India’s payment ecosystem that it began sketching out what would become the 30% cap. For everyone, not just Google. There was already something a little strange about the whole setup: NPCI itself, the entity worried about concentration, controlled almost none of the ecosystem it had built, while the RBI was separately worried that NPCI had become too systemically important. Everybody was worried about somebody else’s dominance. Nobody had a plan that survived contact with an actual deadline.

By 2022, Phonepe wasn’t waiting around to find out if the cap would ever bite. It started building out wealth management and RIA licenses, aiming for a future where 40% of revenue came from finserv rather than payments. It was a hedge that only made sense if you believed some version of the cap was actually coming for the core business.

While the cap didn’t come, Phonepe built the hedge anyway, which in retrospect looks like reading the room correctly, just on the wrong axis. The threat was Navi and Super Money and not the regulator.

By 2024, Google Pay’s own share had already drifted from 44% to 37%, entirely without regulatory help, which is the detail that should have told everyone how this was going to go. Concentration in UPI was never a one-way ratchet that only a rule could reverse. It was always going to erode on its own timeline, as more apps and more incentives and more government-adjacent pressure piled in.

The cap assumed it needed to force something that was already happening.

Then, NPCI stopped waiting on its own rule and just entered the race itself. In March, it relaunched BHIM with real money and a cricket legend behind it, targeting a 5% share within three years.

A month after that, we laid out the odd position this puts NPCI in: referee of a cap it has twice postponed, landlord collecting rent from the rails everyone else transacts on (Rs 3,270 crore of operating income in FY25, not nothing), and now a player on the field as well. 

One of our guests put it as the referee blowing the whistle and then jogging over to join one of the teams, which is about right. So the sub-80% number is not really a vindication of NPCI’s policy.

It is NPCI-the-competitor chipping into NPCI-the-regulator’s own problem, while NPCI-the-regulator’s actual tool sits in a drawer for a third straight year.

And there’s a decent chance the whole cap becomes irrelevant before it’s ever enforced, for a completely different reason. 

In April, in a piece imagining Indian e-commerce in 2028, we described a scenario where AI agents just initiate UPI payments straight through banking APIs, skipping the Phonepe/Google Pay app layer entirely. At which point, as we put it then, the market-share cap NPCI had deferred to December 2026 becomes moot. That was written as a speculative exercise two months ago. It now reads more like an early description of the same erosion showing up in last month’s data, just arriving through a different door.

The pattern across five years of our own coverage is that every proposed fix for UPI concentration has landed either too late or beside the point, and the actual de-concentration, when it finally showed up, came from a scatter of companies NPCI didn’t build and can’t fully take credit for. 

Which leaves the actual open question for this edition: is 79% the start of something structural, or a one-month number that Phonepe reverses with the next feature update? My take: NPCI will get to take a bow for a shift it mostly just stood next to.
