I have been thinking about a strange fact for the past few days.

Sure, NEET is the exam a renowned 59-year-old engineer and education reformer from Ladakh is currently starving himself over, and one that is bringing Delhi and other parts of the country to a rolling boil, with a growing chorus asking, politely and then less politely, for the education minister’s resignation. But it is also the exam that…

…a state government once tried to abolish on equity grounds.

…turned an edtech giant into a unicorn. 

…dragged another edtech giant into the ground. 

…made a lesser-known company one of the few profitable businesses in Indian edtech. 

…a global tech giant built free mock tests for. 

Normally, when something generates this much anger, it’s because it’s failing at its one job. NEET generates this much anger and still manages to be one of the most commercially productive pieces of infrastructure in the country. Those two things aren’t in tension. I think they are the same fact, described twice.

I only noticed this by going back through five years of our own stories about NEET, one at a time, and seeing how often a different business showed up on the other side of the same crack.

https://the-ken.com/story/neets-switch-from-pen-and-paper-to-computer-damned-if-you-do-damned-if-you-dont/?utm_source=email&utm_medium=newsletter&utm_campaign=collection?utm_source=ro_hdr&utm_medium=email&utm_campaign=

Start with supply, because supply explains most of what comes after. India has around 100,000 government MBBS seats for 2.1 million students who sit NEET every year. We wrote about this gap in 2023 https://the-ken.com/story/100k-mbbs-seats-for-2-1m-aspirants-yet-indias-fix-is-the-opposite-of-what-youd-expect/?utm_source=email&utm_medium=newsletter&utm_campaign=collection. Even a near-perfect score doesn’t guarantee you one, and the government’s fix for that scarcity was to make it easier to open more private colleges rather than build more public capacity. One woman in that story scored 359, missed the cutoff, and did her MBBS in Russia for a third of the price a private Indian college would have charged her.

https://the-ken.com/story/100k-mbbs-seats-for-2-1m-aspirants-yet-indias-fix-is-the-opposite-of-what-youd-expect/?utm_source=email&utm_medium=newsletter&utm_campaign=collection?utm_source=ro_hdr&utm_medium=email&utm_campaign=

That is what the exam actually is: a filter so narrow that a chunk of every year’s applicants gets squeezed out of the country before the exam even starts.

In 2024, we looked into the NRI quota https://the-ken.com/story/when-the-merit-doors-to-mbbs-shut-rich-indians-use-the-nri-window/?utm_source=email&utm_medium=newsletter&utm_campaign=collection, a set of seats reserved for children of non-resident Indians and filled at a much lower bar than the general merit list. We found families posing as siblings of a candidate who had scored 180 out of 720, a quarter of the marks, still finding brokers who could walk them through buying a seat via that route.

So while the protests rage on over a leak that let some students gain a small unfair edge, our own reporting shows a separate, technically legal loophole being used to just buy the outcome merit was supposed to guarantee.

Go back further. You will find the exam’s basic existence being contested, for reasons that have nothing to do with leaks. In 2021, we wrote about https://the-ken.com/edsetgo/no-neet-ends-in-the-central-exam-debates/?utm_source=email&utm_medium=newsletter&utm_campaign=collection Tamil Nadu’s attempt to abolish NEET altogether. The state’s own committee found that after NEET arrived, the share of Tamil-medium students getting into medical college fell from about 20% to 2%, while English-medium admissions rose from 80% to 98%. First-gen grads securing seats dropped from roughly a quarter to one in seven.

Not only was the exam logistically fragile, but also fixing who becomes a doctor in India, by language and by class, years before this year’s leak made that fragility a national headline. And the counterargument in that same piece is worth reading too: a coaching veteran told us that handing the decision back to states just moves the corruption into mark inflation at the state level instead of removing it.

Essentially, there is no version of this exam, centralised or not, that anyone has actually solved.

Then there is the industry that grew fastest because the exam is this brutal, and its story over the last few years is really a tale of consolidation followed by collapse.

Physicswallah became edtech’s youngest unicorn in 2023 https://the-ken.com/story/indias-youngest-edtech-unicorn-physics-wallah-is-making-an-audacious-gamble/?utm_source=email&utm_medium=newsletter&utm_campaign=collection on the back of NEET and JEE coaching, taking share directly from older players like Aakash. That same month, we wrote about the rot inside Aakash itself https://the-ken.com/story/aakashs-sales-force-has-no-love-lost-for-byjus/?utm_source=email&utm_medium=newsletter&utm_campaign=collection, whose own sales force had turned against its new parent, Byju’s, after the acquisition brought in a wave of misselling that was poisoning the brand from within. By 2024, we were writing that Byju’s had one escape route left https://the-ken.com/the-nutgraf/byjus-has-one-escape-route/?utm_source=email&utm_medium=newsletter&utm_campaign=collection, and it ran straight through Aakash, because the NEET-JEE coaching arm had become the only part of the company still worth saving.

“Whatever was happening at PW, it was no longer conducive to teaching. We were not comfortable with a lot of things that we were asked to do. I won’t give details since PW is my former employer, but the focus shifted to business expansion.”

Tarun Kumar, PW’s eighth employee

Not every business built around NEET is fighting for its life, though, and this is the part I find most telling. Earlier this year, we wrote about Daily Rounds https://the-ken.com/story/the-most-successful-edtech-you-havent-heard-of/?utm_source=email&utm_medium=newsletter&utm_campaign=collection, a company almost nobody outside medicine has heard of, which has built one of the few genuinely profitable edtechs in the country by doing the opposite of everyone else.

Instead of chasing the huge undergraduate NEET market, it went narrow, focusing entirely on NEET PG, the postgraduate entrance exam. Roughly Rs 340 crore in profit on Rs 590 crore in revenue in FY25. A 45% margin, five years running. Physicswallah, for comparison, had posted exactly one profitable year since it was founded.

https://the-ken.com/story/the-most-successful-edtech-you-havent-heard-of/?utm_source=email&utm_medium=newsletter&utm_campaign=collection?utm_source=ro_hdr&utm_medium=email&utm_campaign=

If a national exam this troubled can still produce a company that profitable, the exam clearly isn’t broken for everyone. Just for the people actually sitting it.

The fiascos have consequences beyond the coaching industry too. We wrote in 2024 https://the-ken.com/edsetgo/neets-fiasco-shows-why-edtechs-have-to-pay-real-attention-to-results-now/?utm_source=email&utm_medium=newsletter&utm_campaign=collection about how NEET’s earlier paper-leak scandal forced edtechs to stop pitching “we teach well” and start actually proving their results, because once a national exam is caught leaking, parents start asking questions about everything downstream of it. A leak first embarrasses the government and next resets what an industry has to prove just to stay in business.

Then, one of the largest companies in the world noticed the same opportunity, from a different angle. We wrote about Google rolling out free Gemini-powered mock tests for JEE https://the-ken.com/story/google-geminis-free-jee-mock-tests-experiment-or-trojan-horse/?utm_source=email&utm_medium=newsletter&utm_campaign=collection, with NEET and CAT next, in partnership with Physicswallah and Careers360. It made a land grab for the 2 million students who take NEET every year, using free AI as the wedge.

https://the-ken.com/story/google-geminis-free-jee-mock-tests-experiment-or-trojan-horse/?utm_source=email&utm_medium=newsletter&utm_campaign=collection?utm_source=ro_hdr&utm_medium=email&utm_campaign=

Whether that is a real public good or a Trojan horse for locking in a generation of anxious test-takers before they have even chosen a college, the fact that Google thinks this is worth doing tells us something about how large and how emotionally loaded this exam’s audience has become.

Which brings me to this year’s leak, and the story we wrote in May https://the-ken.com/story/neets-switch-from-pen-and-paper-to-computer-damned-if-you-do-damned-if-you-dont/?utm_source=email&utm_medium=newsletter&utm_campaign=collection, when the government’s answer to NTA’s second major leak in three years was to move NEET onto computers. What we found while digging into that story was more specific than just the fact that the testing agency is disorganised. A parliamentary committee had already flagged, back in 2024, that NTA had grown dependent on outsourced vendors, without the in-house capacity to secure an exam at NEET’s scale. Switching from paper to screen moves the same outsourcing problem onto a more digitally exposed surface.

“TCS Ion, Eduquity, and NSEIT are our partners,” said an NTA official. “We are currently focusing on the rescheduled exam. The decision on who is the partner for the next NEET will only be taken after this.”

In a country where passing a competitive exam is the only path out of wretched livelihoods for millions, NEET has become a lightning rod for students’ and even states’ anger.

The government’s assumption seems to be simple: if the problem is paper leaks, eliminate the paper altogether. But it’s not like online exams are foolproof. NTA’s own computer-based exams have faced disruptions before.

Which is why, even within the official circles, there are conflicting opinions on what the right way to conduct the test is.

NEET’s switch from pen-and-paper to computer: damned if you do, damned if you don’t, The Ken https://the-ken.com/story/neets-switch-from-pen-and-paper-to-computer-damned-if-you-do-damned-if-you-dont/?utm_source=email&utm_medium=newsletter&utm_campaign=collection

All in all, despite being India’s biggest, highest-stakes exam, NEET isn’t really failing. Instead, it is generating a layered economy because it is this broken.

The leak was never the anomaly. It’s just the one failure loud enough, and cruel enough, to put someone in a hospital bed and someone else on a cap table.

The situation in Delhi is moving by the hour. If you have got a read on it, or a crack in NEET’s economy we haven’t found yet, leave a comment, or write to me at sumit@the-ken.com
