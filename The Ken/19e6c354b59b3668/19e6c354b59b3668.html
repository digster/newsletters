Rapido rides make for some of the most insightful conversations I’ve had in this city. 

Naresh is forty, recently moved to Bengaluru for work, and not particularly at ease with technology. When I got onto his bike, he had a new phone propped on the dashboard mount, still wrapped in factory plastic on the back.

First day with it, he told me. The previous one had died on him about a week ago, something to do with the memory, he wasn’t entirely sure. Seven days without a phone meant seven days without rides, and seven days without income. He had borrowed money to buy this one. 

Naresh didn’t dwell on it, but you could tell. For him and for millions moving through India’s gig economy, a working smartphone is almost a precondition for livelihood. 

What I did not know then was that until 2024, some of India’s largest lenders had turned that same dependence into a recovery tool, and one that worked unusually well.

Large consumer lenders like Bajaj Finance, Cholamandalam, and DMI Finance had found a way to digitally collateralise what was otherwise an unsecured smartphone loan. 

Phones came pre-loaded with software that could partially or fully disable the device if the borrower missed that EMI.

The problem was that the borrowers most likely to default were also the ones with the most to lose if their phone was disabled. People like Naresh for instance, who depended on it to earn in the first place and could only repay through the same device that the lender had switched off. Complaints piled up, and the alarms raised by advocacy groups reached the regulator.

So the RBI banned the practice in 2024, amid mounting unease over data privacy and the ethics of disabling someone’s primary digital device.

Now, after nearly two years, it has returned with a second-draft amendment that once again gives lenders legal cover to remotely disable smartphones, but this time with several caveats.

Three evils

The RBI’s reversal is a real-time illustration of a trade-off it has had to weigh. The coercive nature of the remote-disable on the one hand, versus the financial inclusion it uniquely enables. 

Small-ticket smartphone loans are a near-nightmare for a lender to underwrite. Small amounts, borrowers with no credit history or asset collateral, and no real way to repossess the smartphone after default. The remote-disable mechanism eased the risk calculus just enough for lenders to start saying yes to borrowers they’d otherwise turn away.

Today, smartphones make up roughly half of all consumer durable loans in India, with one in three consumer electronics purchased on some form of small-ticket financing. In fact, between 2020 and 2024, when the remote-disable mechanism was at its peak, borrowing for smartphones and home appliances jumped from 1% of all borrowing in 2021 to 37% in 2024. Part of this seismic shift, explains Ajay Sharma, was very much driven by the remote-disablement practice.

Across two decades in the Indian smartphone industry, Ajay has steered the launch of brands like Micromax and Panasonic into India. He makes clear who remote-disable actually makes lending possible for: buyers picking up entry-level phones, typically under Rs 15,000. Most are new-to-credit borrowers, and “the point-of-sale of these phones is also the point where India’s financial inclusion agenda finds some of its most direct traction,” he added. 

This market is also unique in scale. Unlike a car or fridge, where a household typically owns one and holds onto it for a decade, smartphones come in multiples per family and get replaced every two to three years. That compounds into a far higher volume of loans and far more people stepping onto the credit ladder through this single product.

The 2024 ban proved the point. Delinquency rates on overall smartphone loans drifted from around 2% pre-ban to 2.7% after. But at the lower end of the price spectrum, where the remote-disable had done its heaviest lifting, Ajay saw delinquencies steadily climb to over 4%. And that’s a problem because:

“The delinquency rates normally acceptable to financing companies in this segment are around 2%.”

Ajay, industry expert

Anything past that, and NBFC unit economics begin to wobble.

Another senior executive at one of India’s largest consumer lenders shared sharper numbers: lending against phones that had previously been protected by the lock fell by about 80%, and on the loans still being written, delinquencies doubled.

The downstream damage cascaded through the entire chain. With recoveries slipping, NBFCs leaned on retailers who had originated the loans. Counters that kept producing defaulters got blacklisted by lenders. “If too many of the dealer’s customers default, lenders stop financing from his counter, which also affects his sales for the other devices,” Ajay said.

Growing  NPAs meant that lenders had to spend much more on recovery, and retailers turned very conservative in pushing financing for lower-end phones. Ajay saw loan application rejection rates climb close to 50% following the ban. 

There are quieter trade-offs too. A meaningful share of new-to-credit borrowers in this segment are women, and for many, remote-disable was the less invasive of the two recovery options on offer. The alternative, a recovery agent at the door, carries its own well-documented history of intimidation and public shaming in non-urban India.

Thus, the regulator faced the tough task of picking the least of the three evils: complete exclusion from credit, a recovery agent’s knock, and remote disablement. 

The latest proposal is their answer.

‘Low tech, low intelligence, and worryingly, low ethics’

The draft regulation also arrives in the middle of a bad supply chain shock to smartphone manufacturing globally. Memory prices surged through 2025 because data centres swallowed an outsized share of global DRAM and high-bandwidth memory output.

The pass-through has hit hardest at the bottom of the smartphone stack. Budget devices saw production costs rise by 20 to 30%, and several India-market models have been repriced upward by Rs 1,500 to Rs 2,500 or discontinued. Global smartphone shipments are now expected to fall close to 12% in 2026, its steepest single-year decline on record. While in India, the entry-level segment fell 59% in the first quarter of 2026, year on year.

The 2024 ban had made credit harder to come by, and the memory crisis, as Ajay points out, has pushed the device itself further out of reach. Both have pulled in the same direction: away from smartphone access for the segment that needs it most.

And so with all that, the RBI has delivered its verdict on the said trade-off, concluding that a blanket ban on phone locking was perhaps doing more harm than good. The new draft regulation puts the tool back in lenders’ hands, but with guardrails, and on most of the contested questions, the RBI has erred on the side of the borrower.

Under the revised draft, a lender cannot completely brick the phone, as was common earlier. The restriction has to be partial and gradual, and essential services such as “internet access, incoming calls, and emergency SOS features” must keep working. But it stops short of specifying what exactly can be restricted. The formalised rules will likely take shape following a round of public consultations, which are open until 31 May.

The other safeguard is that now the lender has to wait. Disablement can only be triggered once the loan is 90 days overdue, during which the lender shall give multiple notices to the borrower. Such a mandatory grace period did not exist previously. And none of it kicks in without prior consent built into the loan contract. Once the borrower pays, the phone has to be restored within an hour.

The smartphone-disable rules are only one slice of a much wider regulatory turn. The same May 2026 draft tightens recovery practice across the board, mandating certified agents, recorded calls and more. Across the document, the centre of gravity has shifted slightly closer to the borrower. 

For Anant Shroff, founder of DPDZero—a Bengaluru-based collections platform that handles loan recovery for banks and NBFCs—this is the moment three years in the making. He built the company on the bet that debt collections in India, in his words, an industry that is “low tech, low intelligence, and worryingly, low ethics”, could be done differently, with empathy as the core product instead of pressure. “We had built all of this,” he said, “no one wanted it.” 

Lenders ranked their priorities in a fixed order: recovery first, cost second, compliance somewhere at the bottom.

With this renewed regulatory posture, Anant is starting to see this order flip in real time. “Now it is compliance, recovery, and cost,” he added. The regulation has arrived for him as something like a prophecy. 

But the prophecy is also unfinished. Several of its load-bearing questions still remain open, such as the point that bars lenders from accessing data on a locked phone but says nothing about the third-party vendors who build and run these locking systems. We will find out what the final cut looks like in October. 

Either way, this draft is the latest sign that NBFC-led lending to the underserved—long operating in regulatory shadows—is slowly moving into the regulator’s fold.
