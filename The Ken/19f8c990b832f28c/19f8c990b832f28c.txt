Earlier in July, several SBI Card customers reported an anomaly in their credit card statements.

Their payments were tagged https://www.moneycontrol.com/news/business/personal-finance/sbi-credit-card-users-report-wrong-overdue-status-on-july-bills-credit-scores-fall-13972322.html overdue, impacting their credit scores almost immediately. This, even as some of them had paid their dues on time, while some others still had time before their due dates.

It may have been a technical glitch, but the episode raises an important question: if paying on time can’t always save you from trouble, what happens when a credit card bill isn’t paid in full?

For one, the moment a borrower chooses not to pay the entire due, they lose one of the biggest benefits of owning a credit card: the interest-free period. And then, the interest meter starts ticking on the unpaid amount, including any new purchase made thereafter, until the entire outstanding balance is cleared.

Essentially, the plastic money that once gave interest-free credit, now starts charging high borrowing rates.

“Paying just the minimum will attract lots of charges,” said a former audit assistant with Deloitte.

The numbers don’t lie.

Say you bought a laptop for Rs 1 lakh using your credit card. The bill arrives at the end of the month, and you have 15–20 days to pay it off. If you pay the entire Rs 1 lakh before the due date, well and good. You just borrowed the money from the bank and repaid it without any charges.

But, say you don’t have enough to cover the entire amount. The bank has a solution.

Every credit card statement carries two numbers: a “total amount due”, the money that a borrower owes their bank at the end of the cycle, and a “minimum amount due”, which is typically 5–10% of that. The latter provides relief for someone who can’t clear the entire bill in one go, keeping the late fee and the “defaulter” tag away.

In reality, though, it’s doing a lot of harm to one’s finances.

The math behind the trap

Say, your card attracts an annual percentage rate (APR) of 40%. Without taking compounding into account, that translates to a monthly interest rate of over 3.3%.

Assuming the borrower pays only the minimum amount due every cycle for six months, and makes no new purchases during the period, she would’ve paid over Rs 15,000 in interest alone, with over Rs 86,000 more to close the debt.

If she were to continue that way, it’d take her years to pay it off completely.

“Considering the simple interest [of 40%, annualised] itself accounts for so much opportunity cost,” said Srinath Ragunathan, a Mumbai-based finance professional.

In reality, however, interest on credit card bills is compounded daily. “That way, the effective annual rate works out to over 49%, since compounding makes you pay interest on previously accrued interest,” he added. “Effectively, the borrower will then be paying nearly half of his original due amount as interest alone.”

Transunion Cibil, a credit-information company, explains https://www.cibil.com/blog/six-ways-you-can-use-credit-card-for-optimum-credit-score? it in simpler terms: “When revolving money (that is, when paying only the minimum due), a borrower continues to accumulate interest charges from the first date of their purchase.”

Now assume the borrower, after paying just the minimum due in the first month, spends another Rs 10,000 on groceries and fuel in the next cycle. Had the earlier bill been paid in full, the new purchase would’ve enjoyed an interest-free period until the next due date. Since it was not, the bank levies interest on the item from the date of purchase.

In the long term, the borrower could end up paying more interest than the actual debt. This way, “[they] would end up blocking their available credit limit, too,” Ragunathan added. That results in a higher credit-utilisation ratio, which is the percentage of a borrower’s available credit they use at a given time.

A credit-utilisation ratio of over 30% can hurt one’s Cibil scores https://the-ken.com/story/youre-clueless-about-your-credit-score-for-banks-that-works-just-fine/, too. That three-digit number, which ranges between 300 and 900, can make or break one’s loan application. (Anything above 700 is generally considered a good credit score.)

Paying the minimum due may buy time for a borrower, but it doesn’t make what they owe any smaller. The option should, at best, be considered an emergency valve.

Skipping a payment is even worse because, as mentioned earlier, the interest is compounded daily. The account would then be treated as overdue, attracting even late-payment charges. Such an activity, or rather the lack of one, stays on the user’s records forever, hurting their Cibil scores over many years.

Users in India have seen their credit card debt grow multifold over the years. In the 10 years to March 2026, the credit card outstanding in the country has gone up https://www.transunioncibil.com/content/dam/transunion-cibil/corporate/documents/Beyond-The-Swipe-Report-Digital.pdf over 8X to Rs 3.1 lakh crore, according to Transunion Cibil. On the other hand, the number of live cards only grew about 5X to 107 million.

Put simply, debt has grown faster than the number of cards that people carry around.

The case for the card

Credit cards remain one of the most useful financial tools in an individual’s wallet. Even as they’ve become less visible. Today, they sit inside UPI apps, and sometimes, work with a tap of a phone. In effect, paying with credit is as simple as scanning a QR code now.

But that convenience doesn’t mask what a credit card represents: everything is still borrowed money.

That said, none of it makes a credit card the villain either, provided the user is financially disciplined.

“I use my credit cards for the money I have,” said the former Deloitte auditor quoted above. “That way, I don’t overspend and get full benefits of the cards.” The 27-year-old has been using two Axis Bank credit cards for over 18 months now. While he enjoys cashbacks from online purchases made through one card, the other offers him better reward points for booking flights and hotels.

And his approach isn’t that complicated: “When paid in full, I don’t incur any extra charges, and can use my cards better.”

Miss that one habit, and credit cards become double-edged swords.

They may offer several advantages, including cashbacks, access to airport lounges, and reward points. But the biggest of them all is the interest-free credit period.

That advantage vanishes the moment one chooses complacency over discipline. Everything after that is the bank charging them for the privilege of forgetting that a credit card is, after all, a loan.
