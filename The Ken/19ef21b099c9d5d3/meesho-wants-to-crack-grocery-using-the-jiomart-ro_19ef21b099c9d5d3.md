Tue, 23 Jun 26
A weekly newsletter about the biggest changes in commerce—focusing on shifts that matter to you.
Good Morning Ishan,
Last week, Meesho announced it would acquire B2B platform Kirana Club for Rs 202 crore. The deal, set to close by March 2027, gives Meesho access to over[ 4 million](https://entrackr.com/news/meesho-to-acquire-kirana-club-in-rs-202-cr-deal-12031243) kirana retailers across tier-3, tier-4, and rural India. On paper, it looks like a big grocery bet. In practice, it’s not entirely clear what that bet is.
At the start of the pandemic, Kiranatech became a legitimate term as everyone tried to connect and digitise kiranas, including Meesho, but within a few years, it [shut](https://m.economictimes.com/industry/services/retail/meesho-shutters-superstore-grocery-business-in-india-300-lose-jobs/articleshow/93802818.cms) the vertical down.
Today, as an e-commerce company, Meesho has two cards to play.
Either it could go the B2B route, which its Kirana Club acquisition signals, or play the B2C card.
The B2B card is roughly what Jiomart [attempted](https://the-ken.com/tradetricks/jiomart-ups-its-b2b-service-but-kiranas-arent-quite-convinced/), positioning itself as the new distributor to kiranas, thus competing with other distributors. Meesho is adding a layer of benefit to that competition by promising digitisation to gain an edge. The e-commerce company believes that it can acquire retailer relationships through Kirana Club’s backing.
Now, if Meesho wants to focus on B2C through kiranas, customers would order on Meesho to buy groceries, and the nearest kirana would fulfil the order, thus cutting the cost of dark stores and logistics out of the books.
Jiomart has also tried this version, and it struggled even with Reliance Retail chipping in.
What has changed now that Meesho wants to renew these bets?
India’s grocery market is valued at roughly $658 billion, with kirana stores and general trade accounting for about 91% of sales.
Roughly 98% of the space is offline, while ~2% is online. It doesn’t take a lot to guess where Meesho wants to play.
Right now, it is running a grocery pilot in Nagpur under a separate grocery vertical, said one Meesho manager.
“That region gives us a proper flavour of tier two (which is where Meesho has a strong suit), you can run a lot of experiments both on the business and on the consumer side,” he added.
The pitch for tier-2 and tier-3 is also different from what quick commerce is selling.
“If you look at these two groups, customers who want speed and convenience and customers who want affordable groceries, which cohort do you think dominates India? It’s the latter,” said Abhishek Maiti, from 1Lattice, a market intelligence platform.
The e-commerce company is trying to redefine the grocery market around affordability rather than speed, focusing on basket prices, value packs, scheduled deliveries, and community buying contracts.
“Right now, metros are almost saturated, and tier-2 and -3 and value-conscious households are the ones where the next phase of expansion is going to be,” he added.
Quick commerce, the argument goes, may not be a viable model in smaller cities beyond a certain point.
The Kirana Club acquisition is meant to accelerate this process.
“The current model of kirana will take very long to expand. The idea is to see how exactly you integrate this Kirana club with it,” said a second Meesho manager.
Currently, Meesho has a marketplace model and a separate grocery model.
“The marketplace also sells grocery items. It could be either a branded grocery, but again in limited categories, mostly beauty, makeup, and some health and wellness-related products like protein powder, breakfast cereals,” added the manager.
But this grocery subsidiary, mentioned in its DRHP in October 2025, has been loss-making for three consecutive years, though the platform believes it can change its trajectory.
According to a source close to the company, Meesho has referenced building a local logistics network, an early-stage pilot for daily essentials and groceries. It is tagged as an H2 (Horizon 2) initiative, which is what Meesho identifies as exploring new opportunities at an early stage.
Through these initiatives, the company tests product-market fit, scalability and unit economics before making further investments. Its successful H2 initiatives, Valmo and content commerce, were scaled into core H1 (Horizon 1) businesses.
Even though it was an old bet, it is still slotted under new bets because the e-commerce company wants to take it slow this time, keeping profitability in mind.
So far, there are no specific numbers for grocery, but its New Initiatives segment—which houses digital financial services, grocery, and AI services — [contributed](https://scanx.trade/stock-market-news/companies/meesho-limited-approves-audited-fy26-financial-results-board-clears-up-to-100-crore-investment-in-payments-subsidiary/39620432) around Rs 120 crore in revenue in FY26, up from Rs 40 crore in FY25.
Meesho has a stronger financial position to experiment from than it did last year. Consolidated revenue rose 34.5% to Rs 12,626 crore in FY26, and net losses narrowed sharply to Rs 1,358 crore from Rs 3,941 crore. There’s a safety net if the bet fails immediately.
And all that depends on what it wants to be. Does Meesho want to be a B2B technology layer for kiranas? A B2C grocery platform that uses kiranas for last-mile delivery? Both? The Kirana Club acquisition points toward the former. The Nagpur pilot points toward the latter.
The numbers will tell in a few years.
