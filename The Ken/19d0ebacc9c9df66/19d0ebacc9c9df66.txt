I’ve been around long enough to know that layoffs usually follow a predictable template at most companies. First, once it becomes evident that the company is struggling financially, it freezes hiring, cuts marketing budgets, kills investments, and then announces layoffs. The CEO then issues a contrite but somewhat anodyne statement that says, “This is a difficult decision. We know you are sad. We are sad too. What to do? The market is hard. The company is losing money. Sorry. Go back to work.”

But AI has changed things. Including layoffs.

“Oracle is planning to axe thousands of jobs, among its moves to handle a cash crunch from a massive AI data centre expansion effort,” reported https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-03-05/oracle-layoffs-to-impact-thousands-in-ai-cash-crunch Bloomberg earlier this month. 

Oracle isn’t the only company that’s simultaneously firing people while investing in data centres. There are reports https://www.cnbc.com/2026/03/14/meta-planning-sweeping-layoffs-as-ai-costs-mount-reuters.html that Meta is weighing chopping off 20% of its workforce to “offset costly artificial intelligence infrastructure bets and prepare for greater efficiency brought about by AI-assisted workers”. 

Earlier this week, Amazon announced it would be laying off 16,000 employees in the same month as its CEO, Andy Jassy, proudly claimed that AWS revenue is expected to grow at twice https://www.reuters.com/business/amazon-ceo-sees-ai-doubling-his-prior-aws-sales-projections-600-billion-by-2036-2026-03-17/ the rate they had previously anticipated. In an internal town hall, Jassy made it clear why he was on a spending spree in the short term:

“The faster we grow in ​AWS, the more capex we ​have to spend shorter ⁠term, because we have to lay out all that capital for land, power, buildings, chips, servers, networking gear. We have to lay all that out a couple of ​years in advance of when we’re going to monetize.”

As I said, I’ve been around long enough to know that CEOs doing layoffs don’t usually talk like that. 

So what changed?

Hi! I am Vidhatri, the producer of Zero Shot! 

Working in a journalism organisation gives you access to different time capsules that capture and save for posterity what it’s like to be living, working, and existing in those specific periods. 

One major thread in our coverage over the last few months—to nobody’s surprise—is the chaos caused by AI across teams within organisations. Earlier this month, my colleague Suprit wrote https://the-ken.com/story/ai-just-broke-brands-google-ad-strategy-the-answer-is-the-ad/ about how marketing teams are wrestling with the “ghost in the machine” that is “haunting their ability to reach customers”. 

The good old days of search engine optimisation (SEO) are over, and the race to crack AI search is on. It’s a battle for visibility, except the rules are entirely new. 

A couple of days later, I read something that took this in another direction. 

Indiamart, India’s largest B2B marketplace, is trying to figure out the new order of staying visible. In the process, though, it has realised that it is being excluded by ChatGPT. So, what does it do? Sue OpenAI for “selective discrimination”. 

The Calcutta High Court, where the case is still being heard, did note that Indiamart was being “selectively discriminated and unjustifiably excluded without any logic”. 

Now, there are layers to this case, including a US federal agency taking note of the counterfeit goods on Indiamart. 

Those specifics aside, this case captures the tension between incumbents jostling for space with new and powerful players. 

Who competes with whom? Who should win? Will the market absorb them all?  

To answer all of these questions, we spoke to Samir R Gandhi, the co-founder of Axiom5, a boutique law firm specialising in competition law. 

Samir has a ringside view of how competition law and markets have evolved in India, having represented regulators as well as private parties. 

He did what lawyers do best: give context, connect the dots, talk about earlier precedents, while also acknowledging that there are a lot of things for which we don’t have answers yet.
