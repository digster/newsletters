Good Morning Ishan,
Every monsoon, the engineers at Khadakwasla dam, just outside Pune in Maharashtra, have a tough calculation on their hands. They have to decide when to open the gates and how far to let the Mutha River flood the city.
Whatever they decide, parts of Pune will go under, as the last three monsoons have shown. In July 2024, the dam released 35,000 cubic feet of water a second, displacing over 4,000 people across the city. This July, residents of Ekta Nagari, a neighbourhood situated right on the riverbank, received warnings at night to move their belongings off the ground floor, hours before some of the dam gates were opened.
Keeping the gates shut is, unfortunately, not an option. Rainfall in the hills to the west fills the reservoirs along the Mutha River and its tributaries—the very ones which supply Pune’s drinking water. The engineers need to keep it as full as possible to last the city until the next monsoon. But once the dams fill up, the gates have to be opened, and the river downstream swells almost immediately. The calculation is about getting the timing just right.
This is exactly the kind of problem a city’s digital twin is designed to solve.
