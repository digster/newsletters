A weekly newsletter about the biggest changes in commerce—focusing on shifts that matter to you.
Good Morning Ishan,
Box-office numbers aside, I know a film is a veritable phenomenon when my dad wants my Netflix or Amazon Prime password so that he can watch it dubbed in Tamil because he missed it in the cinemas.
That’s exactly what he did over the weekend. He had serious FOMO over the second instalment of Dhurandhar, and he wanted to stream the original first on Netflix so that he would be all caught up on the spy thriller, which is exactly the movie you would get if you threw Fauda, The Kashmir Files, and a pulsating soundtrack in a blender.
Within 11 days of its release, the sequel has reportedly grossed more than the first film’s Rs 1,300 crore receipts during its entire theatrical run.
These are the kind of numbers Sanjeev Bijli lives for.
“All thanks to Dhurandhar, Q4 is looking strong now,” PVR Inox Ltd executive director Sanjeev Kumar Bijli said. “We were a little apprehensive after January. Border 2 did reasonably well but it wasn’t a blockbuster and February was completely muted with films not working. Dhurandhar 2 has changed the outlook for this quarter substantially.”
Dhurandhar delivers: PVR Inox to end FY26 on a three-year high, says promoter Sanjeev Bijli, [Moneycontrol](https://www.moneycontrol.com/entertainment/dhurandhar-delivers-pvr-inox-to-end-fy26-on-a-three-year-high-says-promoter-sanjeev-bijli-article-13871493.html)
But you can’t run cinemas on blockbusters alone. That’s like Amazon’s existence being tied to the Black Friday Sale, or Zomato and Swiggy’s to New Year’s Eve.
PVR Inox, despite being India’s largest movie chain, or maybe because of it, goes from a standout quarter to a terrible one so often that even the best projections on growth and profits look like shots in the dark. The Dhurandhar-induced high is sure to wear off soon.
Then it’s back to being all about how to thrive despite movies. Or at least despite bona fide hits.
In 2022, PVR chose to merge with its nearest competitor, Inox, in order to have more bargaining power with studios. The move also came right after the Covid-era existential scare. It was about size being an antidote to the inherent volatility of the movie business.
Then, the combined entity became as much a [fast-food company](https://the-ken.com/tradetricks/if-you-think-pvr-inox-is-just-a-multiplex-giant-youre-wrong/) as a movie chain. In 2023, the average moviegoer spent half as much on food and beverages as she did on the ticket. (The figure is still largely the same.) But I argued in my piece that PVR Inox could squeeze a lot more out of its customers.
For AMC Entertainment, the world’s largest multiplex chain, F&B spends account for 65% of the average ticket price. For some of its rivals, it’s as high as 75%. Which means PVR Inox can do a lot more to get moviegoers to loosen the purse strings.
And what PVR Inox has that fast-food brands don’t is this: there’s no real alternative. For Domino’s, there’s Pizza Hut. For McDonald’s, there’s Burger King. Sure, you may be devoted to one of them, but you at least have options.
But PVR Inox is now the first or only multiplex chain we think of. And whenever we go there, we’re probably going to order something. PVR Inox has to make its expensively-priced foods just tasty enough for us to not feel too bad about splurging on them.
If you think PVR Inox is just a multiplex giant, you’re wrong, [The Ken](https://the-ken.com/tradetricks/if-you-think-pvr-inox-is-just-a-multiplex-giant-youre-wrong/)
Later in 2023, PVR Inox launched a subscription [plan](https://the-ken.com/story/pvr-inox-stacks-the-odds-against-its-own-subscription-service/?share_code=U2QrelNVZEV6RkMrWnBHUklybEVQL1QrSXM0NmN2WVV3MmRBT08ySkcwYz0%3D&t=260330073737). It was the company’s way of saying, “We can’t guarantee great movies, but maybe we’ll make it cheap enough for you to watch crappy ones.”
“Our patrons tend to visit the cinemas about once or twice a month,” said Gautam Dutta, the company’s co-chief executive officer, in an emailed response to The Ken. “We wanted to transform this pattern by encouraging them to experience the big screen more frequently—ideally around five to six times a month.”
Now, PVR Inox wants to run its multiplexes like hotels, as my colleague Aakriti [wrote](https://the-ken.com/story/pvr-inox-wants-to-run-its-cinemas-like-hotels/?share_code=SDJsalNDcTZUOHNMeCtSYXJHQUwzbjJ6WjJNZDFqTXJkZnhadUNHMHRlOD0%3D&t=260330111721) a few months ago.
It’s turning to the tried-and-true strategy of hospitality giants such as Marriott International, Hyatt Hospitality, and Indian Hotels Corporation, and furthering its partnership with franchisee partners. Marriott, for instance, has been able to expand to 143 countries through its “management contract” model, where it operates the hotels owned by others in exchange for a fee. Marriott, the world’s largest hotel chain, owns just 1% of its properties.
In PVR Inox’s case, its franchisee partners would bankroll the screens while the cineplex chain serves popcorn and operates the theatre.
PVR Inox wants to run its cinemas like hotels, [The Ken](https://the-ken.com/story/pvr-inox-wants-to-run-its-cinemas-like-hotels/)
One of their top executives even has a cute [quote](https://www.storyboard18.com/media-and-entertainment/pvr-inox-to-expand-foco-screens-to-2000-as-india-box-office-rebounds-ws-l-93592.htm) about the parallel with hotels: “For us, it’s a return on intellectual capital, and for them it’s a return on capital.”
So, as I said earlier, these are different ways of making sure that there is a business even without blockbusters. Maybe there is such a business. But how valuable will it be?
PVR Inox, whose market capitalisation is just over Rs 9,200 crore, is trading at less than half its all-time high in 2022. This is after two consecutive quarters of almost Rs 100 crore in profits, the highest in two years. Sure, this is partly to do with the knock-on effects of the Iran war, but the stock’s troubles predate it.
Here’s the blunt truth: success doesn’t beget success for a company like PVR.
That’s far from ideal for an investor who has to pay now for future growth.
