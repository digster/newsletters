Dear Ishan, good morning! Do you also, like me, find yourself frequently in the twilight zone between “next time I’ll start earlier” and “this time it’s too late”?

If I touched a nerve, sorry! 

But because I have you to keep me honest, I’m starting earlier this time. For real.

For the last two years, we’ve been creating an annual Gift Guide. It’s entirely subscriber-made. It features no ads, no sponsors, no affiliate links—and, deliberately, no links to Amazon or Flipkart. Instead, just genuinely interesting gifts, and the reasons real people love them.

In 2024, I released it on 17 November. Too late, obviously.

Last year, I managed to pull it back to 19 October. Still too late, many of you told me. 

So, this year I’m starting even earlier. But I’m not stopping there.

Instead of a once-a-year “holiday season” scramble, the Gifting Guide is going to be always-on. I want us to make it a living, curated list we can bookmark and come back to whenever we need an idea, refreshed throughout the year.

A quick catch-up if you’re new: 2024 leaned toward books and desire-objects; 2025 went deep on coffee, health, and AI. But both proved the same thing, that the best gifts aren’t the priciest, but the most thought-through.

So this year we’re starting with the thinking. Today’s survey isn’t what to buy. It’s how you give. And, in an incredible example of taking the scenic route to figure it out, we’re calling it The First Principles of Gifting. A few of the questions:

– Finish the sentence: “A great gift is one that __.”

– A gift you’d love to receive—but would never buy for yourself. Why not?

– What separates “treating yourself” from just… buying something?

– The hardest person you have to buy for—and what makes them impossible.

– The best gift you’ve ever given—and the look on their face.

It takes a few minutes, nearly everything’s optional, and if your answer is sharp, we’ll feature it with your name, if you like.

Do you have a personal philosophy about gifting? A theme that connects gifts, people and occasions.

1. Compounding horizontally

Most of us know or have experienced first-hand how AI is dismantling decades-old vertical career growth models. In the latest edition of the First Principles podcast, Mukesh Bansal—the founder of Myntra, Curefit, Meraki Labs, and Nurix AI—explained to me how he views compounding differently. 

Here’s a lightly edited excerpt where he talks about it.

Mukesh Bansal: If you look at the track record of venture studios as a whole around the globe versus track record of venture funds, venture funds also have a pretty poor track record. But on the whole, more people do venture funds; very few do venture studios, because building one company is hard, and more than one is extremely hard—you just multiply the difficulty by 10. So why do that? I think it’s a culmination of two things for me. One is that I’ve been around startups for 30 years. When Myntra became a fairly large-scale company and I was at Flipkart, there was a choice to continue at Flipkart and play out the compounding for the next 10 years.

But somewhere along the way, it was becoming too much of the same, and I was yearning to try something else, learn something new, which health and fitness provided at the time. Now Curefit is a leader in this category. I could very easily be the CEO of Curefit and run it for the next 10 years, happily compounding. But again, the curiosity part is somewhat limited there. So that’s one part—just my own inner desire to want to get involved in something else. 

Rohin Dharmakumar: Very interesting. I want to go back to that point that you made about Flipkart, you had the opportunity to compound for 10 years but didn’t, chose not to. Then at Curefit, you also had the opportunity to compound, but chose not to. So what I’m actually seeing is a rejection of compounding—

Mukesh Bansal: Not compounding; I love compounding as an investor. I want my Curefit equity to compound 30% forever. So it’s not a rejection of compounding, it’s a very different—

Rohin Dharmakumar: To go back to an earlier stream of thought, there are 2 paths—one is the individual contributor path, and the other is a people manager path. In careers, the dominant stream of thought is that once you enter a sector and build experience, you get better at it, so you stay, build on it, and compound. As opposed to the generalist or eternal zero-to-one person like you, who is comfortable leaving sectoral expertise after four or five years, moving to a new sector and then moving on. In that sense, compounding isn’t happening in the traditional way.

Mukesh Bansal: Well, there are 2 levels of compounding. What I am compounding is my entrepreneurial knowledge and skills. I’ve personally seen failure up close. I’ve seen a lot of other startups fail from the sidelines. That’s now a common pattern—a learning that has, in some ways, compounded for me. If I really understand why startups fail and have some mechanism to avoid that, it gives me an unfair advantage at Meraki Labs. And that’s what I’m betting on, that I will make fewer mistakes than a typical startup.

Rohin Dharmakumar: So you’re horizontally compounding rather than vertically compounding.

The concept of horizontal compounding relates to many other career models we’ve discussed. Portfolio careers. Career lattices. The return of generalists. T-shaped professionals.

2. Save Bellandur Lake

Bellandur Lake has seen better days. For years, it was mocked for its dirty water, weeds, and combustible foam. These days it isn’t even a lake, but just a dry bed, reduced to this state by indiscriminate desilting operations and a poor monsoon. 

But even with its hands tied, Bellandur Lake remains an absolute giant in Bengaluru’s ecological and urban ecosystem. Before we can restore it, we must save it.

For those living near Bellandur, Yemalur, Kempapura and the surrounding areas, this directly affects the environment, flooding, water systems and quality of life around us. But even if you live elsewhere in Bengaluru, Bellandur is part of the city’s larger lake, wetland and drainage system. What happens there affects Bengaluru beyond the immediate neighbourhood.

The goal is to eventually protect the Bellandur Lake, ensure restoration work is properly completed, prevent further damage to the lake and its ecological systems, and make sure the process is transparent and accountable. We are doing this by first trying to prevent the Bund roads from being taken in the hands of private companies and industrialising the area. Using this petition, the cause may be brought to higher legal courts and brings us 1 step closer to solving this short-term goal.

3. An essay that you must read

Dear reader, this is Tanim with the books section. 

Once in a while, a book (usually non-fiction) that blows your mind wide open. For me, that book is a collection of essays by Jean Améry, a Holocaust survivor and Austrian essayist, called At the Mind’s Limits: Contemplations by a Survivor on Auschwitz and Its Realities. It’s free to borrow on Archive.org. 

I read an essay after a long time. Do people even write essays anymore? Most of the essays that I have read are always at least a decade old. In college, reading one every couple of days was routine. Somewhere along the way, I lost that habit.

Not every essay has to be academically dense. And that is very true for Améry’s writing. He’s describing his suffering under the Third Reich, and yet the prose never once feels like a lecture.

Have you read any essays that made you sit with something you would never have considered before? Share it here. 

Even if you don’t read the whole collection, read the first essay that’s titled “Torture”. 

I have encountered “torture” in many places, from fiction, news reports, and TV to Foucault’s Discipline and Punish. None of it hit like Améry. His torture is real and present because it’s mixed with the mundane. There’s no cinematic build-up. It just happens, described the way one would describe eating breakfast. 

Maybe that’s what torture becomes when it’s your default state, not a single event. And how many people even survive long enough to tell their tale. 

In that mundanity, Améry asks deceptively simple questions about what we mean by “humanity”, and why some people have to fight every single day just to qualify for the word.
