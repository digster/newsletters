Three years ago, my colleague Seetharaman and I [wrote](https://the-ken.com/story/why-mrf-nestle-and-other-pricey-stocks-are-happy-being-exceptions/) about why Indian tyre maker MRF and other “pricey” stocks are happy being exceptions. They stood out for not making their highly priced shares affordable for retail investors. Generally, listed companies want to have more retail shareholders. So they make their shares cheaper by issuing bonus shares and doing stock splits.
But nearly a dozen-and-a-half stocks that traded above Rs 10,000 a piece had done none of this in years. Even now, nearly 40 names including Bosch, 3M India, and Shree Cements, continue to trade above that price. A chunk of India’s retail investors can only watch them from the sidelines—wanting to buy and sell them, but unable to.
Several countries like the US, Japan, the UK, Canada, and Singapore have solved this through “fractional shares”—essentially, a portion of a share less than one unit, which costs a fraction of the price of one share. For example, in the US, where a full share of Apple stock trades at roughly $310, for $10, you can own about a thirtieth of the share. Indeed, many Indian investors have bought fractional shares in names such as Meta, Google and Apple.
But Indian stocks are a different story.
