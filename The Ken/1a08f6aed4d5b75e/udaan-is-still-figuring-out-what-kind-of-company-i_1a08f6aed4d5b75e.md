Good Afternoon Ishan,
Udaan is buying Lynk, Swiggy’s B2B distribution business, for roughly Rs 500 crore. Lynk gets FMCG companies such as HUL, Marico, and ITC into more than 100,000 kirana (mom-and-pop) stores, mostly in four cities. This business is something that, on the face of it, Udaan should have understood years ago.
Except that the Udaan of a few years ago was trying to do big things.
Udaan was going to organise India’s fragmented wholesale trade, connecting manufacturers, distributors, and retailers on one giant marketplace. The presumption was that this would be enormous. Investors liked the idea enough to put more than $2.3 billion into it. Udaan became India’s fastest unicorn in 2018, just 18 months after launch.
Then the company discovered that wholesale trade could be, among other things, a fairly good business for losing money.
So it started doing the opposite of what a heavily funded startup would do. It cut cities, categories, people, and even businesses. It moved away from the marketplace model and towards something much closer to conventional distribution.
And now, having spent several years making itself smaller, it is buying things again.
There is an irony in this. Udaan’s business now looks close to the business it might have built if nobody had given it billions in the first place. It is focused on groceries and staples, concentrated in some markets, interested in private labels and, crucially, much more concerned with whether each piece of the business makes money.
Udaan [says](https://www.business-standard.com/amp/companies/start-ups/udaan-secures-160-million-in-funding-simplifies-capital-structure-126071401346_1.html?utm_source=chatgpt.com) revenue grew at roughly 25% CAGR between Q4 CY23 and Q1 CY26, contribution margins improved by nearly 500 basis points and Ebitda burn fell by about 70%. Its largest operating cities and clusters are now Ebitda profitable.
So perhaps Lynk is not really about getting bigger. It is about getting better at being the particular sort of B2B company Udaan has decided it wants to be.
The Ken has been watching a startup learn the hard way that having a lot of money and having a good business are two different things.
The Udaan of today is restructuring, trying to get the economics right, and asking investors for patience. Just weeks before the Lynk deal, Udaan was working through a restructuring involving $170 million of convertible notes and fresh financing from existing backers. It was also abandoning the marketplace model that had been the centrepiece of its original plan.
(In July, Udaan’s global creditors filed for insolvency proceedings in the Singapore High Court against the company’s offshore holding entity, Trustroot Internet.)
There is something circular about Udaan spending years figuring out how much less of a marketplace it can be.
The B2B e-commerce company is treading water. And it finds a $160 million lifeline, including from the world’s largest asset manager
The easiest way to see what Udaan keeps buying is to look at its acquisitions. In 2025, it got Shopkirana in an all-stock deal. Info Edge, which owned just over a quarter of Shopkirana, ended up with a stake in Udaan that it valued at $23 million. Shopkirana brought another network of kirana stores and, more importantly, another way of getting FMCG products from suppliers to those stores.
The Lynk deal may not be identical. But clearly, Udaan, which used to want to be the software layer connecting India’s wholesale market, now wants to own more of the plumbing.

The first signs that the original plan was in trouble came much earlier. In 2020.
Udaan had laid off about 3,000 people and needed a $280 million funding top-up. Inside the company, the restructuring had names borrowed from video games: Pacman and Mario. Pacman, a logistics business, was eventually shut down because it was losing money. It was just becoming clear that ambition had a carrying cost.
3,000 people laid off. An FMCG-food category culture clash. A stagnant Mario. And finally, a $280 million funds top-up. Here's how 2020 shook up India's leading B2B unicorn
At the time, the plan was to cut the excess to get profitable, and then go public. The IPO did not happen. That was the gap between the company’s slew of fundraises and its operating reality. Udaan had raised enough money to become one of India’s most valuable startups. By March 2023, GMV had fallen more than 40% from its earlier level, to about Rs 5,600 crore.
Udaan has laid off over 500 employees over the past six months and plans to sack more while it puts its house in order by streamlining its operations.
Just next door, Jumbotail had raised a fraction of Udaan’s money. Yet by March 2023, its GMV had doubled to Rs 2,260 crore while Udaan’s had fallen. Jumbotail’s strategy was offensively unglamorous. Turns out, it was the lesson Udaan needed to learn.
Smaller than its two rivals, seven-year-old Jumbotail is certain it doesn’t need the one thing considered essential in FMCG distribution: feet on the street
This is a funny place for Udaan to end up. From wanting Indian wholesale to be more like technology to itself being more like wholesale.
