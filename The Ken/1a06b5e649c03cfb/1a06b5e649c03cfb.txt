All that glitters in Augmont’s IPO is not (digital) gold. Shares popped 22% on listing day. But there is the other side of India’s gold rush

By Sumit Chakraborty https://the-ken.com/writers/sumit_368/

Earlier this week, Prime Minister Narendra Modi asked us Indians, again, to please stop buying so much gold. It is his second such appeal this year. This time, the trade deficit and a weakening rupee are doing the asking, with gold imports having become expensive enough to worry a government that is also, reportedly, weighing whether to cut the very import duty it hiked in May to make gold less appealing in the first place.

Nobody seems to have told Augmont Enterprises, whose shares listed two days before Modi’s speech and were, by then, already up more than 20% on a stock market that had not gotten the memo either.

Augmont listed on the bourses on 31 August at Rs 961 a share, a 22% pop over its issue price, after an Rs 825 crore IPO that was subscribed more than 100X. Anand Rathi Research, one of the brokerages telling investors to subscribe, had said the issue was already valued at around 20.6 times FY26 earnings at the top of the price band. Fully priced, in its own words, it was a reasonable long-term bet on India’s gold market getting more formal and more digital over time.

The stock has held most of that pop since, valuing the company at close to Rs 7,900 crore.

To be fair to that enthusiasm, most of it probably has nothing to do with digital gold at all. Augmont’s actual business is overwhelmingly SPOT, the B2B side that sells bullion to jewellers and dealers, and that business is genuinely humming: a return on net worth near 50%, almost no debt, and a gold price rally fat enough to lift the value of every transaction passing through it. None of that needs a retail customer to open an app.

What feels strange to me is the thing below that trade business, because a meaningful part of what Augmont actually sells doesn’t have a price the market can discover at all, not in any way that resembles how a listed share works. 

Its consumer arm, a platform called Gold For All, sells digital gold. And digital gold is in a category that the market regulator has gone out of its way to say it won’t touch. Back in November, Sebi left little room for interpretation: digital gold products are neither notified as securities nor regulated as commodity derivatives, they sit entirely outside the regulator’s jurisdiction, and none of the investor protections that come standard with the securities market extend to them. The chairman confirmed days later that a new framework wasn’t even on the table. 

So, Augmont’s shares became a regulated security, but the gold in its customers’ digital wallets did not.

All of this did not arrive out of nowhere. We covered Sebi’s circular in detail back in November https://the-ken.com/kaching/digital-gold-in-regulatory-no-mans-land/?utm_source=email&utm_medium=newsletter&utm_campaign=collection, and noted, almost as an aside, that it landed on the same date that has marked demonetisation and a US presidential election in years past. Nobody who pays attention to how these announcements get timed found that convincing. Digital-gold apps got a warning label that week but no rulebook, and the industry has since stepped over Sebi’s line and started regulating itself.

“There has to be some collaborative effort among digital gold players to ensure that customer gold balances are maintained in the form of physical gold bars. The gold stock, its purity and quantity must be audited, matched with books and certified every month by an independent auditor. These should be done to ensure that they don’t get into some regulatory problem that they have to shut down.”

Ramkumar RS, founder and CEO of gold-savings company Milligold

The India Bullion and Jewellery Association put together a body called the Digital Precious Metal Assurance Council of India, tasked with auditing vaulted gold, verifying that platforms actually hold the physical metal they claim to, and running some kind of grievance process. More than 80 million digital gold customers now fall under its watch. 

It is all a bit like asking the fox to install the locks on the henhouse, only the fox genuinely seems to want the chickens to survive this time.

If you want a sense of how lopsided the arrangement really is, look at what is right next to Sebi’s own regulated alternative. Electronic gold receipts, the fully overseen, exchange-traded way to hold gold electronically, have managed something like Rs 100 crore in total trading volume since they launched back in 2022. Unregulated digital gold platforms moved almost Rs 4,000 crore in a single month earlier this year. 

And “regulated” does not mean “safe” in this sector either. Sometimes it just means someone catches the problem later.

I wrote in June about Rajesh Exports https://the-ken.com/the-collection/rajesh-exports-and-sebis-trouble-of-being-right-eventually/?utm_source=email&utm_medium=newsletter&utm_campaign=collection, a listed, Sebi-regulated gold refiner that the regulator eventually accused of misrepresenting transactions worth Rs 15 lakh crore over five years, barring founder Rajesh Mehta from the securities market while it investigates. Sebi’s own forensic review found that 97–99% of the group’s reported revenue over that period was attributed to overseas subsidiaries whose own standalone accounts showed only a sliver of it, alongside more than Rs 11,000 crore in trades the regulator now calls fictitious. That is Sebi eventually being right, after five years.

Augmont isn’t even the first gold platform to run into a governance snag on its way to the public markets. We wrote in October about Malabar Gold https://the-ken.com/story/malabar-gold-rose-to-the-top-with-an-army-of-investors-to-stay-there-it-has-to-leave-them-behind/?utm_source=email&utm_medium=newsletter&utm_campaign=collection, India’s biggest jeweller by sales and 5X the size it was six years ago, which is also perpetually getting ready to list, and also had to untangle its own ownership first: family members and more than 4,000 individual investors scattered across 43 subsidiaries. 

Augmont’s version of the same problem wears a different shape but lands in roughly the same place. Its promoters are expected to hold zero shares once the dust settles on this IPO, a complete exit that one reviewer flagged as an unusual governance signal worth paying attention to. Layer on a related entity, Riddisiddhi Bullions, which supplied more than a quarter of FY26 revenue, and the top 10 customers accounting for 52% of revenue with no long-term contracts behind any of it. That’s where the pattern is.

And tucked into the same prospectus, almost as a footnote, is a detail that undercuts the whole trust-us pitch a little further. Augmont has itself been the target of a cyber-fraud case, one that got a company bank account frozen at Rs 1.4 crore before the freeze was eventually challenged. It is worth being fair here: Augmont was the victim in that story. But the platform is asking customers to trust it with money for gold they will never physically hold, even as its own disclosures show that it has already been on the other side of a money-losing cyber fraud.

And it isn’t just storage that is in that gap. Some of these same platforms have gone further https://the-ken.com/story/gold-leasing-apps-assure-up-to-21-returns-its-a-p2p-play-escaping-rbis-crackdown/?utm_source=email&utm_medium=newsletter&utm_campaign=collection, leasing out customers’ digital gold to jewellers as working capital and advertising “assured” returns of 15–21%, well above the roughly 11% average annual appreciation on gold they cite in the same breath. One financial adviser put it like this: there is no such thing as an assured return on gold, and calling it that is itself misleading. The structure behind it looks a lot like peer-to-peer lending, an area the RBI has been tightening its grip on, except this version was built specifically to sit outside that framework too. 

Then there is a growth story that has been playing out for years, largely without much of a governance conversation attached to it. We reported last January on Jar and its digital-gold peers https://the-ken.com/story/jar-and-its-digital-gold-peers-are-sparkling-until-regulators-come-knocking/?utm_source=email&utm_medium=newsletter&utm_campaign=collection, Safegold and Gullak among them, and just how easy these apps have made it to start investing. A phone number, an OTP, and a daily micro-savings habit is live before there is time to think twice. 

Jar’s own annual recurring revenue climbed from Rs 23 crore to Rs 270 crore in a single year, almost entirely on the strength of that frictionless onboarding. We had flagged the same tension even earlier than that https://the-ken.com/story/us300m-jars-golden-run-strikes-a-jarring-valuation-note/?utm_source=email&utm_medium=newsletter&utm_campaign=collection, back in 2022, when Jar’s valuation was already running well ahead of any regulatory clarity anyone actually had. Four years later, the clarity finally arrived.

Now, I absolutely don’t mean to say people who bought into this IPO made some kind of obvious mistake. The SPOT side of things, bullion trading for jewellers and dealers, is real, already scaled, and now disclosed the way a public company is supposed to be. 

But the part of the pitch built for retail India is still running in a space where the regulator has said that if it goes wrong, don’t come looking for them.
