It was raining IPOs in India until Trump decided to start a war in West Asia. A temporary truce is underway. If the war storm really passes, it could start pouring public issues again. Yet, a long-running problem will likely continue—most folks don’t read initial public offering (IPO) documents.

When a company is preparing to list on a stock exchange, the draft red herring prospectus (DRHP) is the first complete look at everything the entity entails: its business model, risks, financials, promoters, history, litigations, etc. The document, which can actually run into hundreds of pages, should ideally inform an investor’s decision. But in reality, it doesn’t—simply because most retail investors hardly ever have the time, inclination, or wherewithal to pore through the tome. 

Investment decisions vary person-to-person. Some follow the advice and footsteps of superstar investors like the late Rakesh Jhunjhunwala. Others pile on because global gurus had invested in the company—I know dozens of people who bought Paytm’s IPO because Warren Buffett’s Berkshire Hathaway had bet on it earlier. 

Then, there are also others who turn to Telegram channels and Whatsapp groups, where every Tom, Dick, and Harry can pretend to be a finance expert. Many of these “finfluencers”, in fact, don’t meet the Securities and Exchange Board of India’s (Sebi) criteria to give out investment advice. 

While Sebi has been doing its best to block these channels, it has now found another way, one that plays to how investors behave. 

Last month, the market regulator pushed a bunch of amendments to the issue of capital and disclosure (ICDR) regulations, which govern how Indian companies raise capital through public offerings. A highlight: a new mandatory abridged prospectus.

“Finally, Sebi is admitting that retail investors don’t read 500-page legal documents,” said a Sebi-registered advisor. “The abridged prospectus gives you the overview. What does this company actually do? Who runs it? How much debt does it have? What are the top risks?

Everything is optimised to be succinct and clear. The business overview summary is capped at 500 words. The summary of the industry can’t be more than 250 words. Details about the promoters and each object of the issue get 100 words each, max. The abridged prospectus includes pre- and post-offer shareholding tables, and also key financials like share capital, net worth, revenue, Ebitda, earnings per share, return on equity, net asset value, top 10 risk factors, among others.

Additionally, the regulator made it compulsory for application forms and public advertisements to carry QR codes and links to their red herring prospectus, abridged prospectus, and price band details.

“The topline. The bottom line. All in one place,” the advisor added. “You won’t need to Ctrl+F through a PDF.”

It’s perfect for people who want to invest carefully, knowing a fair bit about the company, but don’t have the interest or the capacity to read hundreds of pages. 

But there’s a catch. There’s still going to be a lot of legalese. 

“It is going to be written by the same lawyers and book-running lead managers who drafted the DRHP,” the advisor said. “I hope it does not defeat the very purpose of keeping the abridged prospectus: to be clear, simple, and easily understandable.”

He also identified another gap. The prospectus is not required to have a peer comparison. A mandatory field in a DRHP and RHP, this part compares the IPO-bound company’s financial and operational metrics with its industry peers and helps justify the offering price. 

According to the advisor, “it’s a must-have for retail investors.”

Also, whether the issue’s price and valuation are right or expensive forms a key part of an investor’s decision matrix, along with other factors in the abridged prospectus. There can be no shortcuts here.

While the investors are breezing through their lighter reads, Sebi has moved to address another issue on its hands—IPOs dressed up but not ready to go. Some 40-odd firms are sitting on approved IPOs worth nearly Rs 50,000 crore. 

Take Herofincorp, the Indian motor company’s non-banking associate, which aimed for a public issue of Rs 4,000 crore. It filed its DRHP in July 2024 and, in May 2025, got Sebi’s approval to list. Almost a year later, it’s yet to file its final prospectus. Zepto, the quick commerce company, submitted its confidential IPO papers last December and received Sebi’s feedback in February, and yet there has been no tangible movement since then. 

The markets, especially being put through Trump’s various tantrums and war wringers, have just not been welcoming enough for companies looking to go public. Investors have turned cautious, and companies, in turn, are reluctant to take the risk. 

But companies cannot sit on their approvals indefinitely waiting for better weather. Sebi approvals, known as observation letters, come with an expiry date. If companies fail to file their IPOs within that window, which is typically 12 months, the approval lapses and they have to refile their DRHP and restart the whole process. 

Citing current wartime conditions, industry bodies like the Associated Chambers of Commerce and Industry of India (ASSOCHAM), the Confederation of Indian Industry (CII), and the Federation of Indian Chambers of Commerce and Industry (FICCI), flagged the issue with Sebi.

And the regulator complied with a one-time six-month lifeline.

On Tuesday, Sebi issued a relaxation on the validity window of observation letters. Any approval that was set to expire between 1 April and 30 September 2026 has until the end of September to file an IPO without the hassle of redoing the approval process. 

Considering the representation of the Industry Body, the prevailing uncertain market conditions due to ongoing geopolitical tensions and subdued investor participation, SEBI has decided to grant one time relaxation to extend validity of the SEBI Observations letters, expiring between April 1, 2026 – September 30, 2026 till September 30, 2026 

Sebi circular dated 7 April 2026 

It’s a small but graceful move. And with the West Asia war hitting a two-week snooze and the Sensex rallying nearly 3,000 points on the temporary ceasefire announcement, companies that are waiting in the wings might finally start feeling hopeful again.
