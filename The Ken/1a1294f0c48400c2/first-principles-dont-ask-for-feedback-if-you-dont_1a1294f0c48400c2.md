There’s never been an easier time (at least in my lifetime) to ask for feedback. There are books on how to ask for feedback or give it. AI tools will help you create wonderful questions and approaches. Survey tools are slick and sophisticated. And behind every customer order or digital product is a retinue of embedded services, generating feedback for everything that happens.
And yet, or perhaps, we’re also living in the absolute worst time to be overwhelmed and misguided by feedback.
So many of us have it backwards. We think getting feedback helps build conviction.
Nothing could be further from the truth. If anything, asking for feedback before developing your own conviction is a guaranteed way to cripple your conviction. I see this quite often. A colleague will spin up a survey form to ask users for feedback. Another will write up a proposed product feature and ask for feedback. Yet others will invite their colleagues into a meeting to provide multiple options—A, B, C, etc.—and ask them for the best one.
A common pattern in many of these cases, and one that my colleagues know I’ve warned them against, is not knowing why you want feedback. Or what you’d do with it. Or what kind of feedback you’d simply ignore.
“Tell me, what will you do with the answers you’ll get?” is something I’ll ask back. To which, often, the answer is: I’ll only know when I see the feedback.”
Fair, so I ask again, “If you don’t know what you might get, or what you might do with it, then why are you asking for it?”
I’m sure you can guess the most common answer? “Well, because feedback is good.”
Except, it isn’t. Feedback that isn’t helping refine and shape your existing conviction (or hypothesis) is feedback that is not just pointless, but actively harmful. It distracts you toward what someone else thinks, and away from what you believe (or ought to).
Another of our collective theories is that feedback contains useful information, and that this information is the magic ingredient that will accelerate someone’s learning. Again, the research points in the opposite direction. Learning is less a function of adding something that isn’t there than it is of recognizing, reinforcing, and refining what already is.
…
What findings such as these show us is, first, that learning happens when we see how we might do something better by adding some new nuance or expansion to our own understanding. Learning rests on our grasp of what we’re doing well, not on what we’re doing poorly, and certainly not on someone else’s sense of what we’re doing poorly. And second, that we learn most when someone else pays attention to what’s working within us and asks us to cultivate it intelligently. We’re often told that the key to learning is to get out of our comfort zones, but these findings contradict that particular chestnut: Take us very far out of our comfort zones, and our brains stop paying attention to anything other than surviving the experience. It’s clear that we learn most in our comfort zones, because that’s where our neural pathways are most concentrated. It’s where we’re most open to possibility, most creative, insightful, and productive. That’s where feedback must meet us—in our moments of flow.
[The Feedback Fallacy](https://hbr.org/2019/03/the-feedback-fallacy) by Marcus Buckingham and Ashley Goodall (HBR)
In 2009, Google’s first design lead quit the company after a few years. He wrote an honest blog post about why. The emphasis is mine.
When I joined Google as its first visual designer, the company was already seven years old. Seven years is a long time to run a company without a classically trained designer. Google had plenty of designers on staff then, but most of them had backgrounds in CS or HCI. And none of them were in high-up, respected leadership positions. Without a person at (or near) the helm who thoroughly understands the [principles and elements of Design](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Design_principles_and_elements), a company eventually runs out of reasons for design decisions. With every new design decision, critics cry foul. Without conviction, doubt creeps in. Instincts fail. “Is this the right move?” When a company is filled with engineers, it turns to engineering to solve problems. Reduce each decision to a simple logic problem. Remove all subjectivity and just look at the data. Data in your favor? Ok, launch it. Data shows negative effects? Back to the drawing board. And that data eventually becomes a crutch for every decision, paralyzing the company and preventing it from making any daring design decisions.
Yes, it’s true that a team at Google couldn’t decide between two blues, so they’re [testing 41 shades between each blue](http://www.nytimes.com/2009/03/01/business/01marissa.html?pagewanted=print) to see which one performs better. I had a recent debate over whether a border should be 3, 4 or 5 pixels wide, and was asked to prove my case. I can’t operate in an environment like that. I’ve grown tired of debating such minuscule design decisions. There are more exciting design problems in this world to tackle.
[Goodbye, Google](https://stopdesign.com/journal/2009/03/20/goodbye-google.html) by Douglas Bowman
I don’t want to make this an argument about designers or “HiPPOs” (Highest Paid Person’s Opinion), but to restrict it to the fact that conviction was a worthy muscle to build back in 2009, and still is in 2026.
But since I used the Google example, it’s only fair I contrast it with the Apple example, clichéd and stereotypical though it might be today.
“Don’t let the noise of others’ opinions drown out your own inner voice. Have the courage to follow your heart and intuition.” – [Steve Jobs](https://www.inc.com/jeff-haden/one-of-steve-jobss-most-famous-quotes-still-applies-but-with-a-catch/91180628)
There are usually two gaps in most instances of bad or lazy feedback-soliciting I’ve come across, and they both reinforce each other: low conviction <-> vague feedback.
People with strong convictions built the hard way (research, experience, conversations, time, reflection) either shy away from feedback questions in the early stages (“zero to one” convictions are most easily killed with misdirected feedback) or device questions that help them invalidate their own beliefs (disproving the hypotheses).
That’s right. The best feedback questions are the ones that elicit responses that could kill your conviction. Thus allowing you to either abandon or alter it.
It’s not easy.
…this dynamic of asking for feedback — but not really wanting it — is very common, whether among family members, friends, or colleagues. From my experience, there are two underlying dynamics for this seemingly contradictory behavior: On the one hand, we’ve been taught that feedback is a good thing — we want to hear others’ perspectives since they might help us enrich our thinking. In addition, asking for input is a way of engaging other people and getting them involved. On the other hand, asking for input means that we might have to change plans or do something differently. Change can be difficult and takes time, so we often resist it.
Naturally, this inner conflict is not always conscious. That’s why we might say one thing (“Please give me feedback”) but act as though we don’t really mean it (getting upset with the feedback). In other cases, we consciously make a decision that we don’t want feedback (“The decision is already final”), but feel obligated to ask for it anyway because it’s socially or culturally mandated. Then we can at least check off the box about getting other perspectives, and proceed to do what we wanted anyway.
…
Think carefully and consciously about whether you really want feedback, and why. If you truly think that you could benefit from someone else’s thinking, then ask for it. But if you feel confident that what you are doing or thinking is already good enough, then it’s okay not to ask. In other words, don’t ask for input as social convention. Do it only if you mean it.
[Don’t Ask for Feedback Unless You Want It](https://hbr.org/2012/11/dont-ask-for-feedback-unless-y) by Ron Ashkenas (HBR)
A mea culpa first. A few weeks ago I switched the Gifting List to “always-on” and asked you to send in gifts whenever the mood struck. Well, many of you did—and they’ve been… lovely. Safe. Recommendations you’d land on by Googling “good gift ideas”. Smartphones. Perfumes. E-ink readers. Luggage. Coffee beans. Fitness trackers. All perfectly nice, but none of them surprising.
Which is a problem, because surprising was what I was aiming for.
This list was meant to be the opposite of every other gift list on the internet. As in, not the obvious stuff everyone already knows, but the thing only a thoughtful, slightly-obsessed person would ever tell you about. Right now, we don’t have enough of that. And the honest truth is I can’t fix it. Only you can. This list is a community thing. It’s our list. It is only as good as what we put into it.
So here’s a direct, slightly desperate ask. Help me save it.
Not with another gadget. Not with a perfume or a book. We love them, but everyone already knows them. Send in the other kind:
- the gift you’re almost evangelical about
- the thing you gave that made someone gasp (not in pain)
- the niche obsession you’d happily force on a friend (in a good way)
- the object with a story behind it (not the manufactured ones)
- the thing you can’t believe you ever lived without
- the local, the weird, the specific. Ones you’d never find in a marketplace.
One genuinely great idea beats ten safe ones (or was it 20?). If each of you sends just one—the one—(em-dashes inserted by me) we’ll have a list worth bookmarking for years, not weeks.
