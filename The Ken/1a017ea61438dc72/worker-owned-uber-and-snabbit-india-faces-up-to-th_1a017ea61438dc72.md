Good Morning Ishan,
Sushil has spent years on Delhi’s roads, driving for various taxi companies. Now he thinks he might finally own a piece of one.
“When Uber and Rapido make money, it stays with them,” he says. “With this, if it makes money, I get a share too.”
He means Bharat Taxi, launched by Amit Shah, India’s home minister who moonlights as the head of the five-year-old cooperation ministry, in February. The cab aggregator, Shah informed the parliament on 5 August, had already onboarded nearly 800,000 drivers across more than seven cities and earned them a cumulative Rs 84 crore.
The pitch is that Bharat Taxi is a cooperative operating on the same stake-holding and profit-sharing principle as Gujarat’s Amul or Karnataka’s Nandini. It vows to return 80% of profit to drivers, retaining just 20% as cooperative capital.
It’s the ministry’s most ambitious bet yet outside of agriculture. Ride-hailing is nothing like the government’s other big national initiatives, like seeds and organic produce, which are in sectors where cooperatives are commonplace. But it’s in line with the National Cooperation Policy, 2025, which calls for cooperatives to enter “new and emerging sectors, in both urban and rural spaces”, such as water distribution, biogas, waste management, and transportation. A cooperative life-insurance company is also on the anvil. And the ministry, The Ken has learnt, is exploring a cooperative version of quick-service companies like Instahelp and Snabbit, in addition to cooperatives for plumbers and electricians.
It’s a tacit acknowledgement by the government that gig workers will account for a growing chunk of the workforce, and it doesn’t want to leave their already precarious fate entirely to market forces.
As it stands, though, the math is more modest than the mission.
