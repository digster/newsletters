Jio Financial Services announced its Q4 FY26 results after trading hours on 17 April, and the numbers were…underwhelming. Or at least that’s what the market thought.

Come Monday, while the benchmark Nifty 50 index held flat, the stock fell 3%, likely spooked by the 14% fall in the company’s quarterly profit compared to the year-ago period. The full-year profit was down 3%, too. 

That’s possibly not what investors expected from a business whose parent sports the motto “growth is life”.

In 2023, JFS was demerged from Reliance Industries (RIL) in a characteristic display of the latter’s financial-engineering expertise that The Ken had written about. JFS ended up walking away with Reliance shares then worth Rs 1 lakh crore, ring-fenced from any regulatory threat. 

The mood all-around was understandably buoyant. 

“Jio Financial Services Limited is uniquely positioned to capture the growth opportunities in the financial-services sector and play a crucial role in transforming the landscape of digital finance in India,” announced chairperson Mukesh Ambani in Reliance Industries’s annual report that year. 

The market responded in kind, and JFS’s stock price jumped by 45% in less than a year. 

But nearly three years since its listing, JFS is realising that finance, unlike telecom or retail, can’t be disrupted with deep pockets and low pricing alone…

On the power challenge, the article’s concern is well-founded. Modern AI racks with NVIDIA and AMD’s latest architectures (GB200/GB300s) are now pushing 120–150kW+ per rack, and dense AI clusters are extraordinarily power hungry at scale. What’s telling is that Google had to secure its own power distribution (discom) license. This model of hyperscalers becoming their own power utilities may become a precedent across India.

On the talent question, unlike US data centers tucked away in rural Eastern Oregon or remote Virginia, Vizag is a major port city with a growing IT presence. The talent challenge may be less acute than it first appears, especially with the government projecting 5,000–6,000 direct and 20,000–30,000 indirect jobs. My hope is that this investment catalyzes a real ecosystem, not just buildings and power lines, but a local talent pipeline, supplier base, and cooling/thermal management industry that positions India’s east coast as a genuine global compute hub.
