Look at your fingers. You grip a coffee mug without thinking. Button a shirt. Pick up a phone. A humanoid’s finger doesn’t look much different.

Yet when asked to name the most frustrating part of a humanoid robot, a robotics engineer pointed immediately to its hands. 

“Humans take dexterity for granted. Without it, a robot is just an expensive mannequin.” 

Robotics engineer

That seemingly mundane ability—to grip, balance, manipulate objects, and navigate the world—is precisely what robotics companies are racing to teach humanoids. Bernt Børnich, CEO of 1X, an AI and robotics company backed by OpenAI, calls dexterity “the final boss of robotics.”

Building the robot is only half the problem. Building the intelligence behind it is much harder. 

That requires data. Vast amounts of it. Unlike large language models, which were trained https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/scirobotics.aea7390 on the equivalent of hundreds of years of human reading scraped from the internet, robotics companies are working with barely a fraction of that in video.

Worse, the internet is a poor teacher for robots. They can’t be trained on just any footage. What they need is meticulous, first-person recordings of humans interacting with the physical world, carefully collected, annotated, and painstakingly structured.

So the industry turned to India. 

Across the country, workers are recording https://the-ken.com/story/tesla-and-figure-ai-are-building-robots-to-act-like-humans-indian-workers-are-teaching-them-how/ themselves doing everyday chores for data-collection firms, which then sell that footage to companies such as Tesla, Figure AI, and Agility Robotics to train their humanoids. Indian startups see this as a moment to claim a seat in the global AI value chain. 

The country has over 26o https://tracxn.com/d/explore/industrial-robotics-startups-in-india/__AlisTT9OisX6tlAYQyqJ6_1BW5Rlkc9czvaB8R8NIR8/companies robotics startups, and investors are beginning to pay attention. 

Last week, Addverb Technologies, an Ambani-backed robotics startup, said it aims to raise more than $100 million to double down on its operations in India. Unbox Robotics recently raised $28 million from ICICI Venture and RedStart Labs—one of the largest bets on the sector in recent years.

Others are not as hopeful. Madhukar Yarra, CEO of a Bengaluru-based company that annotates training videos for robots, thinks that this is just a flash in the pan https://timesofindia.indiatimes.com/business/india-business/its-a-race-to-capture-real-world-ai-training-data/articleshow/131671305.cms. 

But even as India celebrates its progress in AI—with Meity recently saying https://economictimes.indiatimes.com/tech/artificial-intelligence/20-foundational-ai-models-created-under-indiaai-mission-5-released-meity-secretary/articleshow/131690791.cms startups under the IndiaAI Mission have produced 20 homegrown foundation models—the next frontier may be shifting from language to the physical world. And unlike AI, India still has no comparable strategy for robots. It’s still trying to succeed in a layer whose value has already begun to depreciate.
