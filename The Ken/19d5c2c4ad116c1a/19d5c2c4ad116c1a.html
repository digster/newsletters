Of course, I’ve been bribing them with treats every day, so that helps.

Bribo got his fifth and final rabies shot this past week after that bad fight last month. He’s made a new friend, Tommy, who’s about six months old too. Gabru doesn’t like him a lot because he jumps around too much in excitement (yeah, Gabru doesn’t like moves that are too fast or too random).

We’re also in Bengaluru’s golden quarter for swimming. March to May is the best period to swim, because it’s too cold before that, and the rains start after.

2. The perils of ‘metadata creativity’

A few weeks ago, in a fit of frustration, I told some of my colleagues, “Guys, for God’s sake, please don’t do ‘metadata creativity’.”

I felt bad about it later. Because I realise that creativity is hard, and that it often follows from learning, which requires experience, that younger professionals simply haven’t had enough of yet.

Once I got over that (pretty quickly), I refocused my attention on the phrase I’d made up in my head in that moment: “metadata creativity.”

What did I mean? Let’s start with the first word, “metadata.” It’s defined as “a set of data that describes and gives information about other data.”

When you write a document in, say, Microsoft Word (until Microsoft decides to rename it to, I don’t know, “Copilot Write” or something), metadata could be the size of the file, its format, the date when it was created, its author, and so on.

Data about other data.

Now think about creativity that is designed to be creative. It doesn’t start from the inside—from the seed of an idea, a belief, or an observation. Instead, it starts from the outside, from the structure of creativity. It’s a checklist approach that tries to figure out the markers of a creative output, and then sets out to “check” each of those markers.

Guess who does this job brilliantly? AI large language models.

Because while the tools may contain the “sum total of human knowledge” within them, they are empty and bereft of any idea, belief, or observation as a seed or core. Every response they give is designed and fine-tuned to satisfy their users.

Hence, they are unbeatable at “checklist creativity”. Show the output of a good model for a creative problem to a layperson, and they will be wowed by how creative it “looks”.

Except older and more experienced professionals aren’t as easily impressed. Because they see through the structure and checklists to arrive at the lack of anything truly novel at the core.

Now don’t get me wrong. Seeing the world outside-in, through a structure, is a valuable skill. But it is a skill of abstraction. And before you get to practice abstraction, you must plod your way through detail.

I call this problem premature abstraction, borrowing a concept from computer science. In programming, you are warned never to abstract too early, before you understand the underlying patterns. The same principle applies to learning. AI allows students and professionals to jump to high-level outputs before they have done the low-level work that makes those outputs meaningful. The product looks the same. The person behind it is fundamentally different.

AI-Proofing your Future: How to Learn, What to Study, and Where the Jobs Will Be (Part 1) by Mohan Sawhney

Please take the time to read the full piece by Prof. Sawhney. It’s worth it.

Metadata creativity is premature abstraction. It’s an attempt to be creative by imagining the perfect result, then creating a scaffolding for it, then filling in words, pictures, and meaning last of all.

It’s the difference between coming up with an answer to a hard problem and offering an “answer-shaped response” (what LLMs do).

Of course, when enough of us do it, we see the effects at a societal level. The simultaneous collapse and revival of “taste”.

The mainstream conversation about AI and taste is boring. Can machines even have taste? Will AI replace creativity? Is generative art real art? These are À rebours questions. Consumption and curation. They miss the point entirely.

What is actually happening is obvious, and painfully human. We gave the world a generative engine for taste and 99% of companies, brands, and people produced slop with it. Then we blamed the engine. This is like handing someone a Steinway and cursing the piano when they play “Chopsticks.” AI didn’t create slop. It merely revealed that most of the people who claimed to have a taste level were actually just good at moodboarding and copying Dieter Rams. The moment they had to generate instead of curate, the emptiness was right there on the screen. And it stung.

[…]

All this matters because the stakes are decidedly not aesthetic. They’re civilizational. For the first time in history, the constraint on what gets built is not resources, nor distribution, nor technical capability. AI is dissolving all three simultaneously. The bottleneck is now taste itself. The limiting factor on what gets funded, what gets made, and what gets remembered is whether the person holding these new tools has anything worth saying with them at all. It’s clear today that most don’t. Yet.

We’ve spent the last 20 years promoting pattern-matchers, rewarding reference-collectors, and building an entire curatorial class whose philosophical capacity tops out at “frictionless design.” But taste is abrasive, and rarer than ever. This should concern you more than any doomer scenario about artificial general intelligence enslaving the human race. We wonder why the culture is flat. Why we produce slop at work, eat slop bowls for lunch, and scroll through slop for leisure. The danger was never that the machines would develop taste. It’s that the people directing them never had any in the first place.

Taste Test: Encrusting the Tortoise by Douglas Brundage

And we end up living in  “The age of average”.

Creativity is hard. Coming up with original ideas is hard. It’s also frustrating and unpredictable.

It’s much easier to refocus our energies on the “processes” instead—brainstorming sessions where the monkey is socialised to everyone else’s backs, workshops, pixel-perfect mockups, mood boards, and long Claude documents that look impeccable. It’s safer to propose a pastiche of the best work that already exists around the world than to risk something truly untested.

I’ll quote from “The age of average” again.

 But it’s not all bad news.

I believe that the age of average is the age of opportunity.

When every supermarket aisle looks like a sea of sameness, when every category abides by the same conventions, when every industry has converged on its own singular style, bold brands and courageous companies have the chance to chart a different course. To be different, distinctive and disruptive.

So, this is your call to arms. Whether you’re in film or fashion, media or marketing, architecture, automotive or advertising, it doesn’t matter. Our visual culture is flatlining and the only cure is creativity.

It’s time to cast aside conformity. It’s time to exorcise the expected. It’s time to decline the indistinguishable.

For years the world has been moving in the same stylistic direction. And it’s time we reintroduced some originality.

Or as the ad agency BBH says.

When the world zigs. Zag.

The age of average by Alex Murrell

4. Of numbers in titles

Hello, dear reader. This is Tanim taking over for the books section. 

When we think about books with numbers in the title, the most generic and obvious choice is 1984. If you are past that and have moved to newer books, there’s Fahrenheit 451. If you are a young adult fiction fan, it’s Six of Crows. If you like old classics, it’s probably A Tale of Two Cities. For those who enjoy adventurous novels, Twenty Thousand Leagues Under the Sea or The Three Musketeers. Five Point Someone if you first started reading during your engineering degree. And Catch-22 if you like to challenge yourself. That one is a roller coaster. 

The book I read under this theme falls into none of these categories. The author has written some issues of Wonder Woman, but her books belong to a totally different genre. Heart-wrenching family drama situated within a societal issue is Jodi Picoult’s favourite genre. Nineteen Minutes, my choice for the number-in-the title book, is about school shootings, friendship, and of course, family drama. A quick Wikipedia search will tell you that it was her first book to hit the New York Times bestseller list. But My Sister’s Keeper is the one you see most often on roadside bookshops, and it’s also the only one adapted into a feature film. 

Before I tell you more about Nineteen Minutes, let us know if you have any books with numbers in the title to recommend here.

This one is definitely a tear-jerker, and like most of her books, deals with complicated relationships. It gives you a different perspective on guns and shooters, and on how trials become so focused on the shooting itself that they minimise the context that led to it. True, it’s hard to focus on anything else when you have to appease grieving family members of victims, but that’s exactly what makes the conflict so rich, and what gives the book its power to move you. Our gray matter likes the grey in stories. At least mine does. 

You might punish the shooter, but do you take preventive measures to make sure that this never happens again? That question gets lost in the hurt of mourning the deceased. 

On a tangent, this reminded me of a quote in my colleague Sudeshna’s story from about ten days ago: “Detection is a technological event, but prevention is a behavioural challenge.” To relate this to the present context, let me reframe it to: Punishment is an appeasement event, but prevention is a societal challenge.

5. Okay, but did you see that tattoo though?

Hey there! It’s Vishnu with the Movies/TV/Music section.

I was rewatching Nobody (2021) last week (which also happens to be one of my favourite action films in recent memory), when a rather familiar scene came up:

Bob Odenkirk’s character, Hutch, walks into a tattoo parlour. A bunch of guys are about to rough him up. One of them glances at the cards tattooed on Hutch’s wrist:

A 7 of Spades and a 2 of Diamonds.

Why this tattoo, you ask? In a game of poker, it’s the worst starting hand you can be dealt, basically, a fold-immediately-if-you-don’t-want-to-lose-everything situation.

Without giving you any spoilers, let’s just say you don’t want to deal with Hutch’s bad (or good) hand.

And yes, I said this was a familiar scene because I had asked about it on a quiz I’d hosted sometime back in Bengaluru. Once a quizzer, always one 🙂

So naturally, when I sat down to write down this week’s section, I thought of doing some digging on tattoos from movies and TV. And here are a few that got me, and you might have missed: 

The Matrix (1999)

Neo is told over the phone by Trinity, to “follow the white rabbit”.

Moments later, he gets an invite to a club from a woman with a small white rabbit tattoo on her shoulder. The tattoo is on screen for maybe five seconds.

He follows her to a club. That’s where he meets Trinity. That’s where everything begins.

Btw, it’s apparently also a nod to Alice in Wonderland, and a Jefferson Airplane song of the same name, which also features in the trailer for The Matrix Resurrections!

(And no, this segment was not sponsored by Rohin.) 

John Wick (2014)

I mean, who can forget the Baba Yaga/Boogeyman scene!

Right before he dresses up for ‘work’ in his all-black, you get a glimpse of this tattoo sprawled across his back: “FORTIS FORTUNA ADIUVAT,” which roughly translates to “Fortune Favours the Bold,” or “It is only the strong that fortune comes to save,” depending on your interpretation.

Side bar: In 2021, Matt Damon starred in an ad for Crypto.com around the same motto. Well, we all know how that turned out, and like always, South Park made sure that Good Will HODLing never forgot about it. 

Deadpool 2 (2018)

In a blink-and-you’ll-miss-it scene, Zeitgeist, one of the X-Force members, has “116” tattooed on him.

That’s a reference to X-Force Issue #116, the comic in which Zeitgeist first appeared.

It’s the kind of thing only a comics nerd would catch on first watch, which, let’s face it, is exactly the Deadpool audience.

Another sidebar (it’s not a sidebar anymore, is it?): Zeitgeist is also an exceptional documentary film that covers a lot of conspiracy theories across religion, history, world order, and even the 9/11 attacks. It’s streaming on Youtube. Definitely worth a watch!

The Departed (2006) + Breaking Bad

Here’s an interesting one: Billy Costigan (Leonardo DiCaprio, The Departed) and Jesse Pinkman (Aaron Paul, Breaking Bad) have the same tattoo:

It’s a Borneo Scorpion (a ‘Pantang Kala’), which stands for protection in battle. As far as anyone can tell, it’s a complete coincidence. But let’s just say the extent of ‘protection’ was different for Billy and Jesse, iykyk.

The Secret Life of Pets (2016)

In the film, a tattoo artist practices on a pig before working on humans: 

But why a pig? Because apparently, pig skin is the closest thing to human skin in terms of texture and thickness, and real tattoo artists do practice on it.

Also, the dotted lines on the pig are the different cuts of pork!

Now, I have to be honest with you, this list is almost entirely Hollywood.

This is ironic, given that the first time I remember tattoos playing a real role in an Indian film was in Ghajini (the Hindi one, not the OG). As a kid watching it, it looked so badass and cool (which I still think it is).

Another sidebar (last one, I promise): the phone number written on Aamir’s body as a prop turned out to be a real number belonging to a woman in Mumbai. She spent weeks after the film’s release being flooded with calls. In the end, the production team had to get her a new number!

But beyond Ghajini, I’m drawing a blank on Indian films that use tattoos with this kind of intentionality, and I suspect that’s my ignorance more than anything else. 

If you’ve got one, or if I missed a tattoo from any film or show that you think deserves a spot on this list, I genuinely want to know.

Drop it in the comments. Consider this an open invitation.

Have a great week ahead!
