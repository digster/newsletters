A weekly newsletter about how finance is getting supercharged by tech in India, and how you can make money work for you.
Good Morning Ishan,
I have a personal-finance quirk. Every quarter end, I check what the government has done with the rates on small savings schemes. Old habits die hard, and old friends can’t stop joking about it.
A lot of people consider these investments boring and cumbersome. Why bother with staid schemes run by the post office and a few banks? Too much paperwork for too little reward, or so the argument goes.
But if they took a closer look, they would probably change their mind. A boring form masks a cool substance. The effort may be worthwhile for those looking for safe choices—and there are many seeking that now.
For the eighth straight quarter, the government left the rates on small savings unchanged. So, for the April to June 2026 quarter, the Public Provident Fund (PPF) will earn an annual rate of 7.1%, the Sukanya Samriddhi Yojana and the Senior Citizen Savings Scheme (SCSS) will get 8.2%, and the National Savings Certificate (NSC) will earn 7.7%. Other schemes, ranging from recurring deposits to fixed deposits, will fetch between 6.7% and 7.5%.
It’s safe to say that safe fixed-income investments are back in vogue. With good reason. As it stands, the Indian equity market’s return is near zero over two years. The war in West Asia continues to take a heavy toll on investors’ animal instincts.
Fixed-income instruments serve a purpose, no matter what critics say about their post-tax returns not beating inflation. Sure, their long-term returns may be low compared to equity, but here’s the thing: return of capital is more important than return on capital for many. Especially in times like these. While equity has its place in an investor’s portfolio, so should fixed-income instruments. The former promises higher returns and growth with higher risks; the latter offers lower returns but stability with lower risks.
The ideal mix of equity, fixed income and other assets—the asset allocation in a portfolio—depends on the investor’s circumstances and risk appetite.
The bank deposit is by far the most popular fixed-income investment in India. The trouble is, most banks have slashed their deposit rates over the past year or so. Only a handful, mostly small finance banks, currently offer reasonably good deposit rates of over 7% to 8%, and even those are mostly around the middle of the range.
There’s a better alternative: small savings schemes.
To start with, all investments in such schemes, without any limit, are as safe as they get, being guaranteed by the government. This is better than bank deposits, which are covered by deposit insurance only up to Rs 5 lakh per account. On returns too, many small savings schemes score higher, even if they come with longer tenures.
For instance, the PPF—a long-term (15-year) instrument open to all—now gives 7.1%. Sure, this is lower than what some banks offer. But unlike bank deposits, the interest on PPF is exempt from tax. This edge makes its post-tax return much better than bank deposits for many investors. The PPF is a safe and steady long-term compounding instrument for anyone looking to build a nest egg. It’s best to invest at the start of every financial year, before 5 April, to get the most bang for the buck.
The hitch? The maximum investment allowed in PPF in a financial year is Rs 1.5 lakh. But there are other avenues too.
For parents with daughters, the Sukanya Samriddhi Scheme, which runs until the daughter turns 21, is a great choice. Here too, the maximum investment allowed in a financial year is Rs 1.5 lakh per daughter, for up to two daughters. The interest rate though, is much better at 8.2% currently, and it is tax-exempt too—a neat way to build a tidy corpus with a compounding instrument for your [little girl](https://the-ken.com/kaching/a-sukanya-samriddhi-account-is-a-little-girls-best-friend/#:~:text=The%20interest%20offered%20by%20the,is%20highly%20tax%2Defficient%20too.). The interest rates on the accumulated corpus of both the PPF and Sukanya Samriddhi Scheme are variable, that is, they can change every quarter when the government announces rates on small savings schemes.
In contrast, the SCSS and the NSC are fixed-rate instruments. So the rate at the time of investment holds until maturity. At 8.2% currently, the SCSS, with a five-year tenure extendable by another three years, is a good choice for senior citizens seeking regular quarterly payouts. Every senior citizen can invest up to Rs 30 lakh in the SCSS. The interest is taxable, but the post-tax return is still better than what most bank deposits give.

For others, the five-year NSC at 7.7% currently is a better choice than most bank fixed deposits. There is no maximum investment limit, the interest compounds annually, and it is taxed. But the real attraction of the NSC flows to another instrument that derives its rate from it. The Floating Rate Savings Bond (FRSB), issued by the Reserve Bank of India on behalf of the government, carries a mark-up of 0.35 percentage points over the prevailing NSC rate. That brings its current return to 8.05%, better than what banks offer today. This instrument, open to all and without investment limits, pays out interest every half-year. As and when NSC rates change, the payout on the [FRSB](https://the-ken.com/kaching/the-names-bond-floating-rate-savings-bond/) will also change.
The hunt for safe instruments with decent returns could intensify in the coming months, especially if the war drags on. Boring can be a superpower in this quest.
Meanwhile, some fixed-income platforms are aggressively advertising ‘fixed returns’ of 10–14% and 8–15%. These are being pitched as much better than fixed deposit (FD) returns, with ‘zero market volatility’. But these instruments with higher returns invariably come with higher risks, a detail conveniently buried in the fine print, if mentioned at all. It’s caveat investor, as always.
