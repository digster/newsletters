On Wednesday, junior IT minister Jitin Prasada called India “a very fertile ground” for hyperscale data centres and said the government is preparing a data-centre policy https://www.business-standard.com/amp/industry/news/govt-to-roll-out-data-centre-policy-as-concerns-grow-over-power-use-126101400508_1.html, one aim of which is to keep data centres from raising consumers’ electricity bills, as they have in other countries. 

While Prasada did not say what the policy would contain or when it would be ready, clearly, by other countries he meant mostly one country. 

PJM, which runs the grid across much of the US, pays power plants ahead of time to be available at peak. And it sets one price for all of them at an auction. Data centres added so much demand that the price jumped. PJM’s market monitor estimated https://ieefa.org/resources/projected-data-center-growth-spurs-pjm-capacity-prices-factor-10 the extra cost at $9.3 billion in the 2025–26 auction. Every customer on the grid pays that price, including those with no data centre nearby.

The problem in India comes in a different form. Here, industrial and commercial customers pay more than it costs to supply them, and the excess subsidises households and farmers. The draft Electricity (Amendment) Bill that was circulated last October puts the premium at up to 30% above the cost of supply. Data centres fall in the commercial and industrial class, so they are natural payers of it.

The same draft bill would phase the premium out within five years. A 2006 plan to bring cross-subsidies below 20% by 2022 was never carried out, so five years may prove optimistic.

The draft National Electricity Policy from January also reduces cross-subsidies progressively and says states should pay subsidies in advance. If that happens, household power stays cheap only if state budgets pay for it directly. Data centres are arriving in the middle of that transition, and some are arranging to stay out of the old system.

They are arriving in volume. Wood Mackenzie expects capacity to rise from 2.2 GW in 2025 to 12 GW by 2030 https://www.woodmac.com/press-releases/india-data-centre-capacity-to-reach-12-gw-by-2030/. The state budgets that would have to carry the subsidy are the ones competing to host them. 

Mysuru’s untied state grant fell from Rs 25 crore to Rs 3 crore in three years, and Karnataka is building an AI city anyway, as Mrunmayee wrote in a newsletter this month. Telangana and Andhra Pradesh are building their own. A week earlier, Mrunmayee found that nearly 90% of the investment and debt in India’s GPU-and-data-centre ecosystem is foreign, and Yotta’s $12 billion GPU order is more than 100X its FY25 revenue. The foreign money pays for the chips, and the states provide land and power.

Sakshi’s story in September was about Adani’s 80 MW data centre in Noida, which would like to buy its electricity from another Adani company. Andhra Pradesh has already granted one. Google’s Visakhapatnam data centre holds a deemed distribution licence, the first a state has given a private firm outside the power business. A developer needs 300 MW of connected load to qualify, and the licensee can buy power from any lawful source, including renewables through open access. 

The licence covers just the project’s own loads. And nothing else. Google’s campus is still being built, so no existing customer is leaving. The customer that would have joined the system is setting up its own supply instead. Open-access buyers normally pay surcharges to compensate the electricity company for lost revenue, and the licence order will say what applies here. ICRA’s Girishkumar Kadam said electricity companies are already losing large commercial and industrial customers, a class that makes up about 45% of national demand, to open access, and are offering time-of-day discounts to keep them.

The states get little employment in return. Mrunmayee and Inderpal calculated on 31 July that one data-centre job takes about $4.5 million of capital, against about $100,000 in a mobile-phone factory, or 45X. Andhra’s incentive offer to Reliance and Google came to Rs 41,000 crore. So a state budget pays to attract a customer who then buys power elsewhere, just when state budgets are being asked to take over the subsidy that customer might have funded.

The incentives keep coming. Inderpal wrote last October that the government had offered a 20-year tax break to facilities of 100 MW or more. The large operators also prefer to own what they use. Earlier in 2022, Pratap reported that AWS and Microsoft each planned about 1 GW in India and expected to meet more than half their needs in-house, which one executive said would cut costs by over 50%. A company that builds its own data centres to save on rent has the same reason to want its own power supply.
