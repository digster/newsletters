How does a monopoly hand over to its future competitors the keys to technology it alone owns? It starts charging consulting fees.
Late last month, the Department of Atomic Energy (DAE) announced that it was pushing a cabinet note to create a consulting subsidiary under the Nuclear Power Corporation of India Limited (NPCIL), the state-run company that has so far commercially operated every nuclear power plant in the country. This announcement couldn’t have been more timely: it landed within days of the release of the draft rules of the Shanti Act, which breaks the state monopoly and makes way for the private sector to enter India’s nuclear energy space.
With this announcement, it is clearer what the umbilical cord linking the private sector to NPCIL will look like. But for private players looking to enter this space, this connection is also causing some friction.
NPCIL currently holds proprietary ownership of the pressurised heavy-water reactor (PHWR) technology in India. A player like Reliance or Tata looking to build a PHWR plant, but lacking the know-how and trained personnel, will inevitably need help from NPCIL. Especially because NPCIL has its own ambitions.
It plans to build roughly 54 gigawatts (GW) of India’s overall 100-GW nuclear energy target by 2047.
Setting up a commercial subsidiary and charging a fee for this service is how NPCIL is resolving that tension, making it easier for new entrants to access and use PHWR technology.
That has created a “conflict of interest”, per the potential new entrants.
The Ken has learnt that the private industry has formally submitted concerns about this conflict to the DAE. The department’s response is not yet known.
This is only one of the gaps India will have to bridge to build the missing middle between the government and private sector.
