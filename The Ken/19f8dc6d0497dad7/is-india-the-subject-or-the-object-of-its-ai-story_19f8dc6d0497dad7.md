Good Morning Ishan,
If we were to write a book about modern India’s economic and technological history, at first glance the chapters would be about missed opportunities.
We missed transitioning from an agricultural economy to a manufacturing one.
We missed providing enough of our citizens with telephones.
We missed laying broadband infrastructure to bring the internet to everyone.
We missed investing early and often enough in tech R&D to shape global telecom standards.
But, a second reading of some of our chapters after a decade or two reveals a counterintuitive pattern: our misses would often turn out to be strategic advantages.
We became a services-led economy without having first become a manufacturing-led one.
We jumped from not enough telephones to more than one mobile telephony connection per person.
We spent billions on spectrum instead of digging roads, to offer high-speed mobile broadband to everyone.
Our inability to move fast has often given us the ability to strategically use our lateness and scale to our advantage later on. Sometimes even tilting global scales in the process, as it happened when our decision to adopt LTE as the technology for 5G telephony put the conclusive nail in the coffin for its rival standard, WiMax.
Then there are Aadhaar and UPI. Neither was early or cutting-edge, but they ended up winning the undeclared war for being the rails for identity and digital payments, respectively. In the process, they prevented Indian businesses and consumers from being locked into proprietary technologies from foreign companies.
There is a new war brewing. It is a war for control of the ultimate rail: intelligence. “AI is not just another application layer. It is emerging as the underlying rail for everything from enterprise workflows to consumer interactions,” say Venugopal Garre and Nikhil Arela, analysts at Bernstein in a research note dated 22nd June and titled, “Why India needs its own DeepSeek”.
The scale of this invisible war and of its stakes are so high that even the CEO of Microsoft, the world’s fourth most valued company, is beseeching everyone to pay attention. “In consuming intelligence, you are creating intelligence. And what you create should belong to you,” [he says](https://snscratchpad.com/posts/reverse-information-paradox/).
The last few months have seen a dramatic escalation in the global fight to lock in entire companies, industries and even countries to a single, proprietary AI rail. The trillion-dollar valuations of frontier labs like OpenAI and Anthropic rest on the unsaid assumption (or dream) that AI will become a winner-takes-all market. So, there is competition, but there is also a rush to erect moats and create lock-ins while you still can.
From India’s vantage point, we find ourselves on familiar ground again. We missed the AI development bus. But could India’s inability to move fast on the cutting-edge of AI model development now help it make a more strategic and advantageous choice?
Perhaps, but it won’t be easy. “Waiting 20–30 years, as India effectively did in earlier tech cycles, could entrench dependence in ways that are far harder to reverse, locking in value capture outside the country for decades,” says Garre and Arela.
Luckily for India, a fork has opened up on the AI road.
The AI road not taken
“Two roads diverged in a wood,” wrote Robert Frost in 1916. We know he took the one less travelled by.
India – and now I do mean startups, businesses, institutions, developers and professionals – finds itself in Frost’s exact predicament.
The AI road we’ve been on is the proprietary one. India is one of the largest markets and user bases for both [OpenAI](https://cdn.openai.com/signals/how-india-uses-chatgpt.pdf) and [Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/research/india-brief-economic-index).
In a survey The Ken is [currently running](https://theken.typeform.com/to/R9HRtGwM?typeform-source=the-ken.com) (426 responses as I write this), close to 80% of respondents said they have used AI models from OpenAI or Anthropic during the week. Google’s Gemini is close to 70%, followed by multi-model tools like Microsoft’s Copilot at nearly 30% and Perplexity at 18%. All of them are US companies.
Meanwhile, just 6% have used Sarvam, the only Indian model in the list.
The strategic road that is opening up is bright, open and commoditised. That’s right.
The market share of closed, proprietary and “frontier” models is collapsing as users switch to open-weight ones that were always cheaper, but are now increasingly as good in quality too.
A slew of recent models from Chinese labs – first GLM 5.2 from Z.AI, then Kimi K3 from Moonshot AI, and finally (for now) Qwen 3.8 from Alibaba – have brought the performance fight to the doorsteps of US AI labs. Nvidia, a US company and the ultimate shovel-seller of this Gold Rush, has its own powerful open-weight model, Nemotron.
Open-weight models are now [undeniably](https://stateofopensource.ai/) the future and where countries, companies and entire ecosystems are headed.
The combined usage market share of open-weight models measured by OpenRouter, a popular AI model gateway, has crossed 50%. Token prices are collapsing. What seemed like a gradually coalescing AI market where one or a handful of winners would win it all is now splintering into a fragmented and intensely competitive one where models are becoming commodities.
Naturally, this has unnerved US AI companies and investors. If AI models become commodities, the entire foundation of future revenues and profits atop which rest their fantastic valuations and impending IPOs comes back to earth.
The more market share that is clawed by open-weight models, the less the opportunity to lock in customers. It is this narrowing lock-in window that is causing Anthropic, especially, to launch product after product every few months, like Claude Code, Claude CoWork, Claude Management Agents, and Claude Tag.
OpenAI has been slower to catch up, but its Codex and Company Knowledge are very similar in nature.
All of these are attempts to move up the AI value stack to [capture the intelligence layer](https://www.normaltech.ai/p/up-the-stack-how-ais-escape-from), as explained by Arvind Narayanan, a Princeton University Professor, and Akash Kapur, a visiting fellow.
The layer below intelligence is software products. The global collapse in SaaS valuations tells us that it might be too late for most products to own intelligence.
But it’s the layer above that should give us all pause.
“We see a future where intelligence is a utility like electricity or water and people buy it from us on a meter and use it for whatever they want to use it for,” said OpenAI CEO Sam Altman a few months ago.
Is India the object or the subject?
In grammar, the subject is the person or thing doing something. Whereas the object is the one having something done to it.
In the current AI story, India is mostly the object. It isn’t making any strategic choices around AI. Instead, it is the largest market for proprietary AI models. Its companies, startups, ecosystems, industries and knowledge professionals are prime targets for lock-in.
If intelligence is a service you buy on a metered basis, India is the ultimate prize given its scale, software and services pedigree, and tech ecosystem.
The same Sam Altman had also (rightly) told India in 2023 that “it’s completely hopeless to challenge us in training foundational models, and you shouldn’t even attempt it.”
If we bookend Altman’s words three years apart, it’s clear what the battle really is.
It’s about commoditization. Not to become it, but to avoid it.
Can frontier AI labs commoditise their customers before their customers commoditise them? It depends on who controls the intelligence layer. This is what Microsoft’s Satya Nadella was alluding to. It is what Palantir’s (an IT services and consulting company wearing combat camouflage) Alex Karp calls [“alpha”](https://www.palantir.com/ai-sovereignty-is-your-alpha/).
For India, the threat doesn’t end with being locked in. On the other end of the spectrum lies the risk of being locked out.
Bizarre as that may sound, that’s true too. India, along with a host of other countries, was cut off from Anthropic’s and OpenAI’s latest models based on US government orders.
“Enter a geopolitical disruption, and that access could be curtailed overnight, bringing critical applications to a halt. A more likely outcome is subtler but equally concerning: India operates one or two generations behind. Applications built on older models struggle to compete with global offerings. An Indian IT giant with talent at its disposal could lose out to a young US SaaS firm bootstrapped by amateur coders having access to cutting-edge models,” says Bernstein.
This is the India-as-object story.
Flip the lens and make India the subject and things change. AI models are dependent on India. It needs the users, the businesses, the knowledge, the scale and the expertise acquired over many decades.
An AI model that is very capable but doesn’t get used (and fed real data and interactions) much is going to lose to an AI model that is less capable but used daily. Because the data and usage is the point. It’s the intelligence.
As a subject, India has to play to its strengths. India’s IT Services giants, known and respected globally for clean business models, high ethics and long-term alignment with their customers’ success, could unlock a new era of evolution. Businesses the world over will want trusted partners with no conflicts of interest to keep their “alpha” (read: data, processes, operations) secure and orchestrate the numerous AI models that run underneath. Who can argue against the reality that every single company in the world is trying to figure out how to refresh their company’s technology architecture for the future?
By actively combining its advantages and scale around data, infrastructure, talent depth, and ecosystems, India has a chance to do what it has done before: build a system rather than chase the best technology. Aadhaar and UPI succeeded because of their systemic approaches, not technological superiority.
Whether that works for AI is far less certain. The harder questions are only beginning.
Does India need its own frontier models? Can open-weight models really prevent lock-in? Will value ultimately sit with models, platforms, or orchestration? And who should be making these bets? Startups, enterprises, investors, policymakers, or professionals?
