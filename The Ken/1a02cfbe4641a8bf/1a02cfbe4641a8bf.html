Good morning, dear Ishan. After a terrible monsoon in June and July this year, Bengaluru has been seeing virtually daily downpours each evening this past week! 

While I do not think it is anywhere near enough to make up for the deep shortfall this year, it’s still welcome. 

Not for the street dogs though. The waterproof cover for the cage we got for Bribo doesn’t seem up to the task, so he still ends up getting a bit soaked. This usually means he expects extra post-dinner pets most days, as if telling me, you did a shoddy job with my shelter, so now make up for it! Making his shelter weatherproof is now a new project for me, especially given Bengaluru’s proclivity for random rain showers.

Gabru too, hates going for a walk after the rains, preferring the sterile but dry comfort of the basement. Right now he’s sitting beside me, all floofed-up after his weekly bath. It used to be Sundays, but I shifted it to Saturdays earlier this year so that I didn’t have it hanging over me on a Sunday. 

What is it about Sundays that makes you yearn for nothing to do, but also strive to protect your right to do nothing? Such a funny and weird day, I tell you.

2. The space between ‘opportunity-resentment’ and ‘commitment-resentment’

“Let’s start a new series!”

In the nearly 10 years that we’ve been running The Ken, this line has come up multiple times. Journalists and newsrooms are naturally drawn to reporting and writing about specific topics on an ongoing basis. Reporters are usually assigned to “beats”—industries, companies, or trends—to track and cover them consistently and deeply over time. It is also expected of us, from our readers and subscribers. 

But there is an invisible line that lies beyond the beat. It’s the “series”. 

It’s when we commit ourselves to publishing something on a topic at a regular frequency. Sometimes it’s a weekly column or newsletter, sometimes it’s a podcast episode, sometimes it’s an investigative story or feature. 

The allure of the series is strong. It allows journalists to stay focused on a theme and become progressively better at understanding the various issues, developing trusted sources, and building their public profile. 

But the “series” has also been the one that has trapped a lot of talented people, including current and former colleagues. And me!

It’s because the series, in my opinion, lies in what I call the valley of resentment. 

Let’s zoom out a bit now and ignore the series. Instead, let’s look at it as a professional opportunity.

A well-executed series elevates journalists into well-recognised and respected brands. It makes them experts in interesting, often high-potential sectors. And sometimes, it even gets them recognition beyond their series or employers.

But not everyone gets to helm a series. Because newsrooms understand that making a promise of a series to their readers or subscribers is a serious matter. They would rather not make a promise, than make one and do a poor job delivering on it. That latter burns subscriber trust in their word and brand.

Which means there’s a certain resentment at not getting such an opportunity, especially when you see a colleague or peer get one.

I’ve felt that too, many times in my own career.

But then sometimes you get the opportunity. And you’re excited! 

You realise there’s a significant amount of learning and effort involved. Of course you underestimated it. 

And then, there are responsibilities. And deadlines. And ownership. And accountability. 

The buck too has nowhere else to go. It stops at you. Everyone expects you to have the answers, since the opportunity is yours to run now.

And this is where we encounter the second resentment—that of responsibility. 

What started as “I wish I’d get the opportunity” often ends up at “I wish I didn’t have the responsibility.”

This is the chasm of resentment. It’s resentment at having to do something because it is expected, versus the resentment at not being given opportunities that are deserved.

Left unaddressed, it first manifests as “Quiet Quitting”.

And then, “Resenteeism”.

But the chasm of resentment is often also the chasm of growth. We often rise to greater heights that we ourselves didn’t know we could, when someone trusts us with the responsibility. 

Coming back to journalistic “series” as a resentment chasm, at The Ken we’ve tried to address it in two ways. First, by setting a very high bar for someone—anyone—to begin a new series. There is a thin line between being excited at having to publish something new every week or fortnight, and dreading deadline-driven versions of the same. 

But sometimes an equally important solution is to allow people to step back from their commitment, by killing a series consciously.

I had to do that last week when we ended Zero Shot, the shared column-podcast series on AI that Praveen, Brady, and I ran for nearly a year.

4. Blindspot

Good day, Ishan! This is Tanim. On an unremarkable Friday afternoon, I visited Book Worm with a friend, hunting for a birthday present for another friend. (For non-Bengaluru folks, Book Worm is Blossom’s quieter rival, and my favourite book shop in the city, worth a drop by if you are ever nearby.) 

Walking through the tunnel of book spines, my friend asked me for something fun and light to read. And immediately, unhelpfully, my mind was filled with colourful children’s books. Is ‘fun and light’ simply a category that adult fiction never gets tagged with? Or has my reading simply narrowed over the years, so I am not familiar with the world of whimsy? After all, deep, thoughtful stories pull me in a way I can’t resist, but I am no longer sure if that’s taste or habit. 

Does a book come to your mind for ‘fun and light’, or are you too a reader who’s forgetting how to be anything but serious? Share a book here that fits either category. 

Also, if you ever feel like you are not reading enough, here’s one way to think about it. 

We are 8.3 billion people, and according to Google Books and Unesco’s estimates, roughly  155 million unique books have been published. 

Even in a world of perfect literacy, one book each wouldn’t go around; we’d run out before everyone had a story to hold (including non-fiction).

Though, it would be patronising to assume the illiterate any less richly for it. Long before we had books and scrolling, our earliest ancestors were painting stories onto cave walls. From etchings in stone to books burned for machines to learn writing, we have come a long way in storytelling. 

So there are not enough books in the world, and if you are reading more than one book, you are already reading someone else’s share.
