Good Morning Ishan,
NK has done this hundreds of times, but the hesitation at the doorstep is always the same.
People often mistake him for an income-tax officer and hold their breath as he looks at the tablet in his hand, his glasses inching towards the bridge of his nose.
Like the previous house, the lady in this one too does not give him her name. She is “Ram ji ki biwi”. The one before was “Kunal’s father’s wife”.
The date of birth is even more difficult to ascertain. “There was a flood the year I was born,” is the best one lady can recall. Another remembered it as “the month there were 15 weddings in the village”.
NK hasn’t even gotten to the difficult questions yet—how much the household spent last month; how much the husband made; what they have in savings, investments, land, and cattle.
This is Shahpur Matiyo village in Bihar, and NK is one of the enumerators for the National Household Income Survey (NHIS), the first of its kind in India. The country is decidedly late to the party. After all, the US, UK, and less developed countries, such as Sri Lanka and Bangladesh, have been doing income surveys for years.
But economists have long been wary of these surveys.
“Income is one of the most difficult things to survey, since it is also something most lie about,” said Varna Sri Raman, a development economist and a former lead at the non-profit Oxfam India. “You always claim to be ‘poorer’ than you are, so the results of these surveys are often unreliable.”
