Companies rarely tell you when their numbers are fake. Not fake in the accounting sense. Fake in the sense of: this number is real today and will not be real once we stop paying for it to be real. Usually you have to infer this yourself, from unit economics or from what competitors are doing. 

But Urban Company wrote it down.

Buried in the company’s Q1 FY27 shareholders’ letter, in the FAQ section, an analyst asks what management expects Instahelp’s long-term margins to look like. The answer includes this: “current pricing is unsustainable for anyone. If and when pricing normalises, there may be a one-time adjustment in volumes across the category.” 

What this means is that some real portion of this quarter’s growth exists only because prices are set below where they will eventually have to sit, and the company knows it, and said so, in the same document reporting Instahelp’s biggest quarter yet. 

Almost none of the coverage of the stock’s 17.7% jump on Monday got that far into the letter.

A few lines earlier, there is a second admission, and it’s the more interesting one. Asked why the company keeps funding a business losing this much money, management doesn’t reach for a profitability argument. It writes: “Instahelp’s value to Urban Company is larger than its own P&L. It is our highest-frequency offering, keeping users engaged with the app week after week… We would want to lead this category even if it were smaller than our estimate.” That’s the company saying, on the record, that Instahelp was never supposed to be judged as a business in its own right. It’s a retention mechanic wearing a growth story’s clothes. 

Everyone read the 100,000-orders-in-a-day headline as evidence of a business working. Management is telling you, in the filing itself, that whether the business works isn’t really the point.

Morgan Stanley published a note on Monday upgrading Urban Company to Overweight, and the stock went up as much as 17.7% on it. The brokerage’s actual argument, though, was that Instahelp is losing money fast enough to bankrupt smaller competitors first, at which point Urban Company inherits whatever is left of the category. 

Essentially, what I am trying to get to is that the stock market rewarded a company for winning a race that, by the company’s own account, is currently being run at a pace nobody expects to hold.

What do the numbers say, then? Instahelp earned Rs 29 in revenue per order this quarter and lost Rs 346 in adjusted Ebitda doing it. That’s a loss almost 12X the revenue each order brought in. That loss per order has been improving, if you can call it that: Rs 760 two quarters back, then Rs 381, then Rs 447, and now Rs 346, which the company attributes to rising density as more orders and workers pack into the same neighbourhoods. 

Now, multiply Rs 346 by 3.82 million orders, up 43% on the previous quarter, and you get a business generating headlines about six-figure daily order counts while burning Rs 132 crore to produce them. 

Group-wide revenue grew a strong 44%, to Rs 528 crore. Net profit of Rs 6.9 crore a year ago turned into a net loss of Rs 92 crore this year. Both facts are true

What’s interesting is that Urban Company didn’t need any of Instahelp’s numbers to have a good quarter. 

Strip Instahelp out entirely and the original beauty-and-repairs business grew net transaction value 29%, pushing adjusted Ebitda margin up to 6.9% from 5.2%. That’s the fourth straight quarter of accelerating growth, at prices nobody has called unsustainable. International, in the UAE and Singapore, grew revenue 82%. Native grew revenue 60%. 

Three businesses, three quarters of improvement, none of them needing a caveat about future demand falling off a cliff. Management picked the fourth business to headline anyway. 

Any which way, the quarter should have raised some questions. Nobody actually knows how big Instahelp is. Not the subsidised version doing 100,000 orders a day. The version that exists once prices move to wherever they are supposed to land. (That’s mainly because Instahelp wasn’t Urban Company’s idea. Snabbit built the category first, in September 2024. Urban Company followed four months later, at Snabbit’s prices, chasing a demand curve someone else drew.)

I went back through five years of our own reporting on this company, and the honest read is that almost everything above was foreseeable in pieces.

Most recently, we asked, on Daybreak in April, whether India’s instant home-help category has a product people love and a business model that’s breaking underneath it. Snabbit, Instahelp, and Pronto were all, by then, sending a trained worker to doorsteps in under 10 minutes for less than what a coffee costs, and losing money doing it. The line we used then still holds: quick commerce eventually figured out how to make money. Home services hasn’t. This week’s numbers don’t argue otherwise.

A few weeks before that, we mapped how Instahelp overtook the company that started this fight. Pronto joined the category three months after Instahelp did, and by February all three were roughly tied. Instahelp at 840,000 monthly orders, Snabbit at 830,000, Pronto trailing at 340,000. My colleague Suprita was doorstepped by a woman in Urban Company’s indigo uniform, offering Rs 99-an-hour domestic help with a Rs 100 referral bonus attached. That is what customer acquisition looks like in this category.

The same story flagged the number that should have been this week’s headline, rather than a footnote: a loss of Rs 381 per order at 50,000 orders a day. The per-order loss has since improved. The daily order count has since doubled.

A month before that, we asked the question this week’s rally is really an answer to, just not the answer anyone expected: could Urban Company avoid Bigbasket’s fate? Worth finishing that story, because Bigbasket’s fate stopped being hypothetical a while ago. Bigbasket built and owned online grocery for a decade, got folded into Tata, and watched Blinkit, Zepto, and Instamart carve up the category anyway. Its consumer arm’s revenue fell 3% in FY25, to Rs 7,673 crore. Losses widened 46%, to Rs 1,851 crore. Market share slid to a niche 5–7%, and it is now repurposing Croma and 1mg storefronts as makeshift dark stores just to stay relevant inside its own parent’s ecosystem. That’s the fate our piece was asking whether Urban Company could dodge, back when Snabbit had raised $56 million in 18 months and a beauty-focused insurgent called Dazzl had raised $3.2 million specifically to chip at Urban Company’s most profitable categories. 

Urban Company’s actual answer was to bet that it can out-lose the insurgents rather than out-earn them.

Let’s go back to last September. Around the IPO. It was a small case study in a company telling two audiences two different stories in the same week. One piece we ran covered how Urban Company had turned its own gig workforce into a sales force, technicians upselling add-ons mid-job. Monetising the same worker was a clever growth lever.

Another, the same week, pointed out that the IPO prospectus listed those same workers as a “potential risk” factor while the roadshow upstairs called them an asset. That was probably two documents written for two audiences, each of whom needed to hear the opposite.

This tension is old for Urban Company, older than Instahelp. In 2023, we covered partners protesting after the company started permanently blocking partner IDs over rating disputes, with the All India Gig Workers Union backing marches in Gurugram and Ghaziabad. Notable, because Urban Company had been rated one of the country’s best platforms for gig workers by Fairwork India just a year before. Net loss had already doubled to $63 million by then.

View in Browser

Two years before that, in 2021, it was the same fight in a smaller key: commissions cut, wages disputed, a balance sheet already in the red, years before Instahelp existed to give the same pattern of grow the number first, work out later whether it’s real a second, faster front.

Even the idea that quick commerce would eventually reach into home services isn’t new. We flagged Blinkit’s own designs on Urban Company’s turf back in March 2023, three years before Snabbit or Instahelp existed, noting that Urban Company’s own tech team had drawn inspiration from a former Zomato CTO. 

So, Monday’s rally was investors betting on Urban Company’s ability to lose money for longer than Snabbit and Pronto can afford to, inside a category it didn’t invent, running at prices its own letter admits are temporary, stacked on top of three businesses that were already improving without any of these caveats attached. 

Bigbasket lost this exact kind of fight once already, against well-funded insurgents in a category it didn’t design fast enough for. This time, Urban Company is the one doing the attacking, on a demand curve that, by its own admission, hasn’t been tested at a real price yet.
