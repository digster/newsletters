Multiple stories, multiple perspectives, one theme worth your time—every week.
Good Morning Ishan,
At one point in Netflix’s recent documentary Rafa, Rafael Nadal is sitting with doctors, staring at scans of a body that has spent two decades absorbing punishment.
The doctors explain the damage while the cameras linger on the injuries. Of course, retirement hangs over the conversation. Everyone in the room seems to understand that Nadal’s career is nearing its end. And yet nobody is entirely convinced.
That’s what I found strange about the series. It spends hours showing you Nadal’s world—his family, rivals, doctors, routines, fears, and failing body. Yet the closer the cameras get to him, the more elusive he becomes. It explains almost everything except the one thing you are most curious about.
What exactly went on inside Nadal’s head?
I had a similar feeling while reading the interview today around Sarvam AI.
The company has spent the past two years in plain sight. It has raised money from top investors, become the face of India’s AI aspirations, won government backing, reinvented itself several times, and now attracted a $150-million investment from HCLTech. Yet the closer you look, the harder it becomes to answer a surprisingly basic question:
What exactly is Sarvam? And the answer is more important today than it was a year ago.
HCL isn’t some venture capitalist placing a speculative bet. It is one of India’s largest IT-services companies investing in a startup whose revenue remains tiny relative to the cheque being written. Viewed that way, HCL’s investment seems like a bet against standing still.
At one point in Rohin’s conversation with Abhishek Pathak, who is the lead analyst for IT services and internet at Motilal Oswal Financial Services, the discussion turns to AI’s impact on Indian IT services.

But the line that stayed with me was this (showing why one of India’s largest IT-services companies is suddenly writing nine-figure cheques to AI startups):
That’s a much bigger story than Sarvam itself.
$150 million buys influence over Sarvam's roadmap and a stake in India's sovereign-AI story. Whether it buys revenue is the question nobody can yet answer
Over the past year, The Ken has been approaching the same question from multiple directions. Through Cognizant, Amazon, TCS, and more. They tell the story of an industry trying to figure out where the money goes when software starts writing software.
If you spend enough time talking to software engineers and executives about AI, you would realise they are often describing two different realities. Repeatedly in our reporting, it came up that while executives speak of productivity, engineers point out mistakes and the need for verification.
In one story, management’s enthusiasm was easy to understand. AI helps improve margins and revenue per employee. Naturally, investors love hearing about adoption rates and productivity gains.
AI may be to thank for rescuing IT companies’ margins, but it’s also holding them back
The view from the engineering floor looked somewhat different.
In another story, developers described AI tools that often generated usable code, but also generated plenty of additional work. The result was a growing disconnect between AI as measured by leadership presentations and AI as experienced by the people expected to use it every day.
Enterprise-wide contracts and vendor bundling are hardwiring AI coding tools into India’s largest tech firms—whether engineers want them or not
Are the engineers right and management wrong? For now, it’s still under negotiation.
The important point is that AI is surely not an experiment inside the IT industry anymore. Whether everyone is enthusiastic about it is increasingly beside the point.
The deeper we reported on Sarvam, the more it looked like a proxy for India’s AI dreams.
Every few months, the narrative changed. In one story, we saw the technical reality of building AI products for a country where language itself remains one of the hardest problems.
The one-year-old company released a voice model in August, with a text model in development. But there’s a difference between its services—which customers love—and its models—which developers don’t
But the bigger shift came later. In another newsletter, we traced how the company gradually moved away from being merely another AI startup and towards becoming something larger.
That happened at a time when the country’s AI conversation itself changed.
In a fascinating story, we explored how the nation’s AI strategy became driven by the fear of being left behind. Suddenly, governments, startups, cloud providers, and IT companies were all racing to secure GPUs, build models, and stake claims across the AI stack.
Taxpayers' money is spent on looking for 'one' breakthrough moment, aka Deepseek. Building competitive advantage and solving large problems could be surprise by-products
Sarvam happened to be standing at the centre of that rush. Which is also why comparisons with UPI increasingly started appearing.
We caught ‘lightning in a bottle’ with payments. But it is unlikely to happen again
Suddenly, post-Deepseek, the discussion shifted from frugality to sovereignty and applications to foundational models. Sarvam then adapted.
Whether by design or necessity, it increasingly became whatever India’s AI goals need at a given moment.
India’s big bet on its sovereign AI model is off to a familiar start: one winner, many confused contestants
That’s one reason the company continues attracting attention despite repeated pivots. Investors are betting on the possibility that India eventually figures out what role it wants to play in the AI stack.

Governments want it. Startups promise it. Cloud providers offer its infra. And tech companies talk about data, models, and compute. But the further they go, the harder “sovereign AI” becomes to define.
We explored a simple thing: many of the technologies underpinning India’s AI mission remain controlled elsewhere. The chips, the cloud infrastructure, the software frameworks, and often even the legal jurisdictions sit outside India’s direct control.
The data lives in Mumbai, the legal architecture is American, and the chips are Nvidia’s
Neysa took a $600 million debt to build sovereign AI even as 22% of India’s chips are lying unused, and the market is running into Big Tech’s arms
At another point in Rafa, Nadal faces the retirement question. He doesn’t really answer the question. Instead, he talks about competition and about finding reasons to keep improving.
That’s essentially where IT services is today. The old game still works (probably), but perhaps not for as long as it once did. AI is forcing companies to rethink where value comes from and eventually where it fits.
HCL’s bet on Sarvam is one attempt at an answer. It won’t be the last.
