1. Is “Human Skill” destined to become an oxymoron?

Last week, we asked ourselves what sort of skills—human skills—would still be valuable in a world where we humans ourselves have fed all of our history, traits, behaviours, and skills into giant AI models, so that they may copy us better.

Remember, LLMs (the current underpinning of AI) are trained on human knowledge. Which is why they can write code, find patterns, search for information, generate essays, reproduce art and even mimic conversations so well.

What’s the most important human skill you think will still be valuable and hard to mimic by an LLM or AI?

It isn’t First Principles style to do surveys that are easy to take, because those usually lead to responses that are easy to guess.

Instead, we prefer subjective, deliberate, and thoughtful questions that force us to stop and think before replying.

Ninety-two of you did that.

Perhaps because of the self-selecting nature of both the First Principles community (thoughtful, contrarian, reflective, and hopeful?) and the way I’d written the survey introduction and questions, the responses are biased quite significantly in favour of, well, humans.

The 127 skills that people submitted were split in nearly an 8:1 ratio in favour of emotional or relational ones (innately human) versus cognitive ones (easier for LLMs to learn and mimic).

Over 25 years ago I took one of the first personality assessments—the Myers-Briggs Type Indicator, or MBTI. It is widely considered inaccurate and pointless these days.

MBTI divides people into 16 personality types on the basis of four key dichotomous variables.

– Introversion versus extroversion

– Sensing versus intuition

– Thinking versus feeling

– Judging or perceiving

I bring that up because most of the survey responses from you seem to tilt towards feeling, instead of thinking. Perhaps because the former is harder for an LLM or AI to mimic.

Ironically, I had to use an AI to process the 92 responses, given the unstructured nature of the survey. I used Claude.

Here’s the first visual depiction Claude generated when I gave it the responses.

This was generated as a working HTML, with the “stars” twinkling. I could not bring myself to embed that in this email because I am still uncomfortable with the idea of acting merely as a conduit for AI-generated code.

Perhaps one reason for my discomfort was also the fact that the analogy Claude used (constellations in the sky) was actually quite good. It fell into the “uncanny valley” of AI-generated material for me.

But I was more comfortable with another of Claude’s designs.

The design above reminded me of something I could have created using Excel or Sheets.

This is one reason I (personally) disagreed with many of the responses that argued that creativity, taste, innovation, or even imagination would remain human. We underestimate the ability for these incredibly sophisticated programs to not just mimic, but also evolve once they’ve been fed “the sum total of human knowledge” (yes, it is an exaggeration, but you get my point).

I picked a few of my personal favourites, the ones I couldn’t easily write away.

The ability to take a risk

“Taking the right risk requires knowing what it is, what it could do, and the choice to actually commit to the risky act or not. I do not think a human-taught machine can mimic this process.” – Liby T Johnson, Bhubaneswar

Curiosity

“The urge to know, understand or tinker with something is innate to humans and I don’t see that LLMs by design can be trained on this. The recent models are in fact a force multiplier for the curious because it is easier than ever to cater to your curiosity. Curiosity is what I consider to be the trait that makes us different from any species (natural or artificial) – as babies, curiosity dominates survival instinct but makes us learn at astonishing pace, as adults curiosity is the random trigger that pushes us in directions not practical (or not something where any material ROI is expected) but almost anything significant that has pushed humanity forward has originated out of curiosity.  To go on a tangent, the worst crime of the Indian education system isn’t the curriculum from stone age or “marks-maxxing,” but the system designed to abolish the curiosity of a young mind at every stage – this bleeds into not enough research or innovation coming out of India.” – Saksham, Bengaluru

Curiosity without reason

“AI will always work towards a purpose, whether ours or its own. Curiosity without purpose or reason is something that perhaps only a human mind is capable of, on planet earth.” – Megha Mookim, Bengaluru

To feel stress

“Stress makes us do things we can’t normally do or won’t do.” – Anmol, Delhi

Fear

“Of course all the emotions are hard to mimic, but fear is such a thing that AI will never be able to mimic by AI. Its basic foundation lies in acting with no consequences of the sort human fear and refrain from taking actions or reactions.” – Mahesh Patrimath, Bengaluru

Ethical dealings

“AI can convince you to accept wrong information, just to prove itself right.” – Sudhir Y R, Bengaluru

The ability to appreciate imperfections

“All LLMs are designed to solve all problems and take any discussion to a logical end, at least in perception of its users. Thus a human being who can still appreciate imperfections is one who will still remain curious.” – Sameer Poddar, Delhi

Skills performed by hands at regular intervals by a human

“It is insanely expensive to build and train a robot to do human things in the human world. As the world is made for human convenience and not robot efficiency.” – Anonymous

Pursuit of art

“I’m not implying that AI can’t do art. But I’m saying even if it did, it doesn’t matter. Artwork is not just what’s in the frame or what you hear – it includes the context and the story behind its creation. Which is why a perfect Mona Lisa made by AI in 0.2s won’t fetch millions. Secondly, the Flow state of creating can happen only if _you_ create, not if someone else does it for you. Thirdly, no two artworks can ever be the same – AI may be a brilliant artist, but it can never make the exact same art as me. There’s no objective scale to compare art, so we may coexist as artists along with AI, forever being different, and in flow.” – B G Gautham, Bengaluru

I was particularly taken by a set of responses that argued a very contrarian POV–namely that managing or taking care of other humans would be a very valuable skill. This, at a time when not only are companies globally reducing the number of humans who work for them, but also doing away with the need for other humans to manage them (“managers”).

But I can see why it would be non-trivial to have LLMs truly manage humans.

Managing people

“Human beings are imperfect, by definition.  Managing people is like herding cats. It’s not going to go out of fashion. There will always be some jobs requiring people management – politics, for eg!” – Kaushal Sampat, Mumbai

The ability to help someone

“If AI answers everything it doesn’t give humans the joy and sense of purpose that comes from being helpful to another human.” – Anonymous

Caretaking

“AI or LLMs cannot mimic the level of empathy and attention needed to be someone’s caregiver. This is a trait that is partially built in your personality and partially acquired by years of experience and training.” – Prateek Burad, Bengaluru

I’ll end with some text generated by Claude as a response to this survey. There is just the right amount of shade in it.

“When a tech-savvy, largely Indian-metro audience is asked what AI can’t do, they don’t protect their professional turf. They protect their humanity. That’s either deeply reassuring or a sign that people have already mentally conceded the professional battle.” – Claude response

P.S. In case you’re wondering, I evaluated as an “ENFJ”. No, I don’t believe in it any more than I believe in sun and star signs.

I was on a production set last week, for The Ken’s most ambitious offering yet (more on that later), and was thinking about how “it takes a village” is the closest description I could think of for a movie production.

(And no, The Ken is not making a movie. Those were separate points.)

Sure, you might not notice the amount of work that goes into making a single 20-second scene, let alone an entire movie. But it manifests itself in ways you might not realise until you consciously think about it:

“I want to stop playing – that looks so claustrophobic.”

“That sounded so eerie.”

“The music in the background is making me feel anxious.”

“I feel like dancing!”

All this, because of people who you don’t see covered or spoken about in the mainstream, but who absolutely deserve to be:

The composers. The sound teams who work 18-hour days, making sure a door creak sounds exactly right. The cinematographer who figures out how to get that niche, specific angle she is looking for.

Let’s talk about this year’s Oscar Nominees for these categories:

3. Of other tongues

Hello there, this is Tanim.

A lot of our readers have recommended Heart Lamp here before and that’s what I am reading this week. It’s a translated book and we have talked about translations in this section, twice! I mean, with a country of 123 major languages, we are bound to read one translated text or another at least once in our life, even if it’s just the abbreviated version of Ramayana. 

Is there any specific translated work that you would like to recommend to the community? You can share here https://theken.typeform.com/FPBooks?typeform-source=the-ken.com.

Translations and abbreviations are derivatives in different forms. Taking something and shaping it to fit another language or a shortened version seems a pretty huge task. Some complications that come to my mind (which I assume might be part of this derivation process) are what part of the language/story do you keep the exact same? Which part do you change and how and why do you change it? Will the output be as dear as the original to the reader? Are we doing justice to it? 

To go off on a tangent, that sounds a lot like going through life. Which part of ours do we need to change and evolve and what parts do we persevere through to keep the same? Is this worth it? 

Personally, I think so, yes. Presently 🙂 

But you may challenge me! Is there a book that you think needs to be read in the original and the translated or abbreviated version isn’t worth it?

Abbreviated versions are the easy scapegoats for this answer, right? To argue in their favour, I would like to say that, as long as it gets someone reading, I accept this necessary evil. Language, which is the medium itself, should not be the barrier to sharing ideas. Yes, it is ideal we put in the effort to acknowledge the original but there’s no harm in enjoying a translated or abbreviated version. 

Before I close this, are we also thinking about AI-generated translations and abbreviations? That’s a topic for a later date.
