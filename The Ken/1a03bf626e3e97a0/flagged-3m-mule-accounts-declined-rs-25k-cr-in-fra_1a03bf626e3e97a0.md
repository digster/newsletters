Good Morning Ishan,
It took about the price of what could have been a routine shopping bill to freeze the bank account of one of India’s biggest retailers.
The money began its journey more dramatically. Last May, a fraudster in Tamil Nadu had conned a victim out of Rs 1.5 crore through a bogus investment scheme, then set about hiding the proceeds. In one thread, Rs 22 lakh went to one account, Rs 9 lakh to a second, Rs 48,800 to a third. By the time a mere Rs 4,194 trickled down to a fourth account, belonging to V-Mart Retail, the trail had grown so tangled that HDFC Bank simply had to freeze the lot.
The fact that the retailer’s account had cropped up in 172 separate cybercrime complaints did not help its case. Yet when police finally added up what was actually in dispute across all of them, the figure came to just about Rs 3 lakh. The Madras High Court took the hint: it lifted the blanket freeze and confined the restraint to that smaller sum.
V-Mart’s brush with India’s fraud-detection machinery is a study in a problem now confronting India as it gets better at spotting dirty money. Knowing an account is suspicious is the easy part.
The reason is that a mule account works rather like the baton in a relay race staged by criminals. The first bank to receive stolen money is “Layer 1”; from there, the cash is passed on, and on again, through more accounts and more banks and more states, each hand-off designed to make the trail harder to follow and the money harder to claw back. The Ken has covered this trade before, when we wrote about the “mule-as-a-service” industry that now supplies such accounts to fraudsters much as a staffing agency supplies temps.
India’s answer has been to cast an ever-wider net…
