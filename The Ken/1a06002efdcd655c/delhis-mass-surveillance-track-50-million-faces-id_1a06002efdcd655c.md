Good Morning Ishan,
If you want to go around Delhi and expect not to be pursued by prying eyes, good luck.
If any of the mushrooming crop of cameras installed around the capital catches a glimpse of your face or vehicle number plate, law-enforcement officials can identify you and track you down without breaking a sweat.
Especially if you have a voter ID. Or a driver’s licence. Or a vehicle registration certificate.
Or if you’re a tenant. Or a domestic worker.
Or if you or your employer has ever applied for or obtained a police-clearance certificate. Or a character certificate.
Or if you’ve been named in a court case, anywhere in India.
You’ve landed in the grid, and there’s no escaping it.
Consider this. In April, a 37-year-old man driving a white Tata Sierra ploughed through a security barricade at the entrance to Delhi’s legislative assembly, sparking a security scare. He stepped out, placed what appeared to be a bouquet in the speaker’s parked car, and fled.
The man was caught in less than two hours.
The Sierra was stopped at a police picket barely 4 km from the assembly and the driver detained.
The police didn’t get lucky. They had help: the Safe City Project.
That’s a benign-sounding name for a Rs 857-crore AI-based mass-surveillance system the Delhi police have been quietly putting in place over the last four years. It searches faces against a web of databases—Police Clearance Certificate, Interoperable Criminal Justice System, Electoral Photo Identity Card, Vahan, Sarathi—to identify a person of interest.
It’s a data lake of pictures, videos, and personal and biometric information of nearly four million individuals, and counting. To access which, technology service providers like NEC and Awiros have built applications and search engines for the Delhi police.
While the Japan-headquartered NEC is the system integrator, Gurugram-based Awiros provides the core applications like AI video analytics, facial recognition, and a reverse image search engine called Anveshan.
The Ken conducted a month-long investigation, interviewing at least 20 police officers, service providers, and consultants, and reviewing official documents related to the Safe City Project, to unravel the workings of one of the most pervasive surveillance systems deployed anywhere in the country…
