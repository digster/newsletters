Almost every edition of this newsletter is about one listed company that happens to be in the news. But I look forward to the exceptions that prove the rule. I have written about the welcome plainspeak of shareholder letters and why Oyo* founder Ritesh Agarwal has no more tricks up his sleeve. 

As I read about the changes at e-commerce major Flipkart in the run-up to its long-overdue listing, I began to think not just about the number of internet companies that are now publicly traded, but also about how enmeshed they are in the broader narrative about India’s capital markets.

You can’t talk about the role of retail investors, how overpriced Indian stocks are relative to global peers, or imminent big IPOs without putting internet firms front and centre. 

It came as a surprise to no one when the BSE launched its Internet Economy Index in October 2024, and the NSE played catch-up four months later with its own India Internet Index. 

Index providers create new indices in response to, or in anticipation of, investor interest, as my colleague Anand and I wrote in 2021.

Indices are not merely a reflection of the performance of a country’s stock market. Besides being a decisive factor in institutional and retail investment decisions, they are licensed by fund houses to benchmark active mutual funds, which have a fund manager trying to outperform the index. Indices are even more crucial to passive funds, whose portfolios just mirror the index they are tracking. 

So far, there are only two exchange-traded funds tracking the Nifty India Internet Index and one index fund tracking the BSE Internet Economy Index. And the largest of those has assets under management of a mere Rs 30 crore. But as more internet companies list and their share of India’s overall market capitalisation rises, such funds will certainly find more takers.

So this question is not merely theoretical: what are these indices actually made up of? 

The Nifty one has the usual suspects among its 27 stocks, which are also the largest in terms of index weight: Eternal, Policybazaar, Paytm**, Info Edge, Swiggy, Nykaa. It also has discount brokers Groww and Angel One***, whose addition makes sense given that online platforms are central to their business. But the index also includes Motilal Oswal Financial Services. Motilal is a broker, too, sure, but it’s more of a diversified financial-services company. 

What’s even more bizarre is the inclusion of travel-services firm Thomas Cook. One possible reason is that Ixigo is also part of the index. Online revenue for businesses across sectors has shot up in recent years, but does that make them “internet” firms? 

In the nine months ended December 2025, one-fourth of Thomas Cook’s operating profits came from hotels. But you can look past its entry into the index because the top six firms account for three-quarters of the index’s total weight. 

The BSE Internet Economy Index offers no such relief, primarily because telecom companies are among its constituents. It doesn’t just include Bharti Airtel and Vodafone Idea but also telecom-gear maker Tejas Networks and network-services provider Tata Communications. 

Unlike the long tail of “non-internet” firms on the Nifty index, Bharti has the second-highest weight on the BSE index, just behind Eternal. Telecom contributes almost a quarter of the index weight. With Jio Platforms, which owns Bharti rival Jio Infocomm, set to go public soon, the index will lean even further in that direction.

No one would expect to find the Multi Commodity Exchange of India and the Indian Energy Exchange on an internet index. In fact, the former is the third-largest stock on the BSE index by weight. 

A retailer investor is, of course, free to invest directly in any of these tech companies, but anyone who wants to hedge themselves against the volatility of a few stocks, while remaining upbeat on the internet economy will naturally be drawn to these indices. 

But they won’t exactly get what they signed up for.
