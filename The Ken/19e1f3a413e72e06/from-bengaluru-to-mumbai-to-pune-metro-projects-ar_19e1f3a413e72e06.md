Good Morning Ishan,
Every year, in the middle of summer, Bengaluru’s civic machinery shifts gears from “water scarcity” to “rains incoming”. This year was no different.
At a monsoon preparedness meeting in early April, the mood was tense. The meteorological department was predicting less rainfall than usual this year, uneven across the season, and El Niño’s impact was still unclear. No one in the Greater Bengaluru Authority (GBA) conference room wanted a repeat of the 2022 floods.
At the head sat the officials from the GBA, assuring industry representatives and policy makers that their concerns were noted. Butter cookies and coffee sat untouched on the table as they listed a litany of tasks they had completed: stormwater drains cleaned, lakes desilted, pumps deployed, roads hastily fixed up.
Within weeks, on 29 April, a hailstorm hit the city, bringing rain, thunder, and lightning, uprooting trees, and turning the newly repaired roads into mini-rivers. Eight people died when the compound wall of a hospital collapsed near them.
The flooded areas—Shivajinagar, Richmond Circle, and MG Road—sit directly above the city metro network’s upcoming underground Pink Line.
Rewind to the monsoon preparedness meeting in early April. The metro corporation, BMRCL, was conspicuously absent. In fact, it has been largely absent in all the years before this, too. That’s because it was just not included.
