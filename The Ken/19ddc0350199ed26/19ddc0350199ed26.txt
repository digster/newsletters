Municipal bonds can be a cool bet in a hot Indian summer... if you can lay your hands on these limited-edition offerings

India’s richest civic body, the Brihanmumbai Municipal Corporation (BMC), has set the ball rolling to raise https://indianexpress.com/article/cities/mumbai/bmc-municipal-bonds-raise-funds-mumbai-infrastructure-projects-debt-10659727/lite/ money from the capital markets. It’s the first time in its 133-year history that the BMC is going down this route. Its instrument of choice? Municipal bonds (popularly known as muni bonds).

These fixed-income instruments—essentially debt—are issued by municipal or civic bodies to raise money for a swathe of public infrastructure activities, including building and repairing roads, providing water supply, and constructing transport facilities. The structure is generally simple: issue debt for a longish tenure (say five or 10 years), pay interest to investors at agreed rates at regular intervals (say every three, six, or twelve months), and finally repay the amount borrowed at maturity. The idea is that municipal revenues, such as taxes and tolls or specific project revenues, help pay the bond dues. 

Muni bonds are commonplace in many countries, and are a multi-billion-dollar, even multi-trillion-dollar market in the US, where they originate from. 

Though they were first introduced in 1997, the bonds haven’t really taken off in India. At least, not yet. 

This data https://www.sebi.gov.in/statistics/municipalbonds.html from market regulator Sebi is telling. Just about Rs 4,500 crore is now outstanding from municipal bonds issued by less than 25 municipal bodies across the country—from Pune to Indore to Coimbatore. That’s a fraction of what hundreds of civic bodies in India need each year. This, despite the government incentivising civic bodies to lean much more on muni bonds. After all, government grants and financing for municipal projects are limited.

Reports say that since their introduction in India almost three decades ago, there have been just around 55 issuances totalling around Rs 5,000 crore.

But, the carrot got bigger this year—Union Budget 2026 announced an incentive https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleaseDetail.aspx?PRID=2221401&reg=3&lang=1 of Rs 100 crore for single bond issuances of more than Rs 1,000 crore. That’s probably a reason why the BMC is finally looking to tap muni bonds. Though the details of BMC’s expected fund raise, including when, how much, and at what cost, are not known yet, it may serve well for other municipal bodies to take the cue.

Going by past data from the Sebi report https://www.sebi.gov.in/statistics/municipalbonds.html, muni bonds in India offer tidy rates to investors. Consider the most recent March issue of Rs 200 crore by Nashik Municipal Corporation—across tenures, it had a return of 8.05%. That’s much better than the fixed deposits of most banks today. The Rs 100 crore issue by the Tiruchirapalli civic body in February gave 8.5%, while the Rs 100–200 crore issues by municipal corporations in Chennai, Coimbatore, and Tiruppur in January offered rates between 7.9% and 8.5%. 

And in 2025, rates offered by civic bodies in Agra, Prayagraj, Varanasi, Chennai, Pimpri Chinchwad, Gandhinagar, Bhavnagar, Surat, and Nashik ranged between 7.65% and 8.15% on their Rs 25 crore to Rs 200 crore issues. Quite good in a low-rate environment. 

In fact, in pre-pandemic years, rates on muni bonds were as high as 9.5% to 10% in issues by the Bhopal, Visakhapatnam, and Hyderabad municipal bodies. In general, muni bonds seem to offer more than banks and corporate deposits. 

But do higher returns come with higher risk? Yes, to an extent. 

“Markets generally perceive civic body issuances in India to be riskier than high-rated corporate bonds,” said Subalakshmi K, founder of Munify, which analyses city-level public finances. It seems odd, but the problem, or rather the scepticism, stems from perceptions of suboptimal management, record-keeping, and transparency at the municipal level. But, most muni bonds in India are rated in the AA category, added Subalakshmi, indicating high safety levels, even if not the highest. 

“There have been no defaults on muni bonds in India so far.”

Subalakshmi K, founder of Munify

No surprise then that these bonds are now getting lapped up fast. The recent issue by the Chennai corporation, for instance, was subscribed https://www.newindianexpress.com/cities/chennai/2026/Jan/10/gcc-raises-rs-206-crore-via-green-municipal-bond to over five times.

The trouble is that many retail investors seem unaware of muni bonds. Most folks I know haven’t invested in them so far. And neither have I. While earlier the minimum investment amount of Rs 10 lakh kept away retail investors, the threshold is now far lower at Rs 10,000. Yet, awareness and access remain key challenges.

“It’s essentially a lack of imagination by the powers-that-be that many muni bond issuances have not had significantly more retail participation,” said Subalakshmi. She added that people would gladly invest in city schemes that have an impact on their lives. While, of course, retail investors always have an option to invest in muni bonds of other cities, too.

It also doesn’t help that while muni bonds are regulated by Sebi and listed on the stock exchanges, liquidity and traded volumes are almost non-existent for these bonds, making it difficult for retail investors to reliably buy or sell these bonds in the secondary market at fair prices. In any case, this needs some awareness of more complex concepts like yield-to-maturity. 

The better solution for now would be for many more civic bodies to issue muni bonds for much larger amounts, and to spread the word among retail investors to participate in primary issues. Access to these bonds through brokerages and platforms also needs a big leg-up. A deep primary market can be complemented by a robust secondary market with high liquidity and investor awareness. Both would eventually feed into each other.

Beyond government incentives, experts also call https://india.mongabay.com/2026/03/municipal-bonds-need-more-than-incentives-to-fund-indias-cities-commentary/ for structural reforms, including stronger municipal finances, better planning, financial transparency, and investor safeguards to scale up the municipal bond market in the country. 

A broader, deeper market would serve not just retail investors, but also hundreds of municipal bodies across India.
