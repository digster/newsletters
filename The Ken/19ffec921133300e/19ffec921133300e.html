The year is 2017, and Tata Sons has a problem.

Cyrus Mistry, who had been chairman since 2012, has been fired. The board vote apparently takes mere minutes, and he is escorted out before the media finds out. This leaves Tata, whose entire brand is built around stability, with one question. Who actually runs this company?

The Tata Trusts own roughly two-thirds of Tata Sons and appoint its directors. The chairman runs the businesses. So now, Tata goes looking for a new chairman.

Into that thicket walks N Chandrasekaran.

He is not the most exciting candidate the group could have picked. But he is the safe one. A Tata lifer since 1987, unrelated to the Tata family, outside the Parsi business establishment the Mistrys belonged to, an engineer by training who had spent his TCS years with a details-first way of running a company. Chandrasekaran’s mandate, unwritten but understood, was to make sure that particular kind of thing never happened at Tata again.

The year is 2026. It has happened again.

Chandrasekaran’s term as chairman was supposed to run until February 2027. Instead, he is stepping down days before an AGM at which his reappointment as a director was due to be voted on, after the board had already deferred it once.

Noel Tata, who now chairs Tata Trusts, had raised concerns about the performance of some of the group’s newer businesses. There were also disagreements over board seats and strategy, while the long-running exit of Shapoorji Pallonji from Tata Sons remained unresolved. A Tata Sons director was reportedly removed somewhere along the way too.

So the obvious ask is: what exactly did Tata fix in 2017? Because if the answer was “the problem of the chairman and the owners falling out”, the evidence doesn’t look great.

The stock market answered immediately. TCS fell nearly 4% on Wednesday after dropping 6% intraday, and other major Tata stocks also fell. When Mistry finally left Tata’s boards in December 2016, TCS rose over 2%. Investors then thought that the uncertainty was over. This time, the uncertainty is what investors are pricing in.

Tata’s argument is that some of the newer businesses aren’t performing. True enough. Some worked, and some didn’t, which is pretty standard for a conglomerate chairman’s tenure. The interesting part is how selectively that logic has been used.

Look at the portfolio itself.

We reported last week that Tata 1mg, in which Tata bought a 55% stake five years ago, has grown revenue nearly 10X to around Rs 3,000 crore and turned net profitable. It got there without the usual consumer-tech routine of raising money and spending heavily on discounts. That is quite the win in the same annual report Tata Sons released in late July, weeks before this fight went public.

The weird issue is that “newer businesses” is where the scrutiny landed at all. Especially when the business carrying Tata Sons’ fortunes has had its own rough stretch, and it isn’t a newer one.

TCS is where Chandrasekaran made his name, and it still supplies the overwhelming majority of Tata Sons’ dividend income.

We reported last August that TCS cut around 12,000 jobs, its first mass layoff in company history, a decision its own CEO called one of the toughest of his career. We suspected at the time that the moment would be remembered as the first stone in a much larger avalanche across Indian IT.

All of this is happening at the old, dependable business that is going through the roughest stretch of Chandrasekaran’s tenure, under a CEO he appointed. Even his own succession at TCS has been less than smooth: Rajesh Gopinathan, the man who took over from Chandrasekaran as CEO in 2017, abruptly resigned in 2023, nearly four years before the end of his tenure. 

Yet the public debate over his exit remains focused on Bigbasket-sized bets.

We also know performance alone doesn’t usually end a Tata Sons chairman’s tenure, because it already didn’t once, on a far bigger scale.

We wrote in 2021 about Tata Sons writing off roughly $4 billion on Tata Teleservices, a bet that had looked functionally dead for years, with people inside the company unsure it would survive, before it was eventually reshuffled into something workable.

Chandrasekaran was chairman the entire time but nobody pushed him out over it, even though the losses dwarfed anything else. So performance, on its own, has already survived a much bigger test under this exact person.

Something else had to change for it to become disqualifying now.

That something else looks more like a fight over final say, and Tata’s own structure makes a fight like this almost impossible to avoid indefinitely.

We wrote last year about the accountability gap underneath India’s biggest family business groups like Reliance, Tata, Bharti, Adani, and Birla. All of them are growing larger with strikingly little outside scrutiny of what that growth actually costs. Tata’s version of the gap is specific.

We have also covered Shapoorji Pallonji’s finances, including its growing use of large private-credit deals as it looks for liquidity. That gives the family a clear reason to want a say in the Tata Sons board while it tries to extract value from a decades-old, hard-to-sell stake. The timing is important as well. The chairman-succession fight, the family’s exit talks, the removal of its board seat, and the disputed reappointment vote have all landed within a few months of each other.

None of this means Chandrasekaran was the right person to keep, or that Noel Tata’s concerns are wrong. It means “performance” is the neatest explanation, which is also why it is so difficult to prove. The Financial Times reported a dispute, with Noel Tata pushing for a bigger role for his son. 

If that is all true, the losses and layoffs are just supporting evidence for a fight that may be about succession alone.
