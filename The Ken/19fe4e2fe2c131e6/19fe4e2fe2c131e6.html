Dear Ishan, do you remember from two weeks ago when I discovered that Gabru was being two different dogs when walking with me and a dog walker?

Was I the problem?

Partly.

Because I recognise all the dogs Gabru barks at on his rounds, I instinctively tighten my hold on his leash as we approach one, careful not to lose hold of him.

But, that tightening sends an entirely different signal to Gabru. He senses the tension in the leash and (rightly) concludes that I am tense too. And if I am tense every time certain dogs appear, then those dogs must be the cause of my tension.

So he barks at the dogs to scare them away, simultaneously proving himself and me right. It’s a feedback loop of apprehension, tension, and aggression, all connected by a leash. 

The dog walker, in contrast, walks confidently and nonchalantly with a loose leash. No tension, no danger, no barking.

That is only part of the answer though. There’s also “resource guarding”. I am his person. The one who loves him, plays with him, talks to him and gives him treats and belly rubs. The dog walker is just another person. So, when another dog approaches, there is an element of “stay back, this is my human, he’s taken” at play.

It’s the same reason Gabru will bark at and even bite our trusty old robot vacuum cleaner as it whirrs around near me (or my wife and son), but doesn’t bother at all if it’s cleaning and no one else is around. He thinks he’s guarding us from the cleaner!

This isn’t all. Apparently, when he gets scolded for biting the cleaner, he clocks it as a “reaction” from us. Like how kids do things to get a reaction out of their parents.

Finally, having their human around when confronted with another dog or a robot means they have “backup”. Instead of merely avoiding conflict and de-escalating, they now have the confidence of support, should things get ugly.

All of these explanations make sense, but I also realise they might be convenient theories to help us make sense of our unpredictable furry friends.

1. Giving yourself permission

One late Friday afternoon in early July, I sat with a bunch of my colleagues. We were discussing the first two months of our weekly group sessions around mental models and growth, which draw heavily from these First Principles editions.

Somehow, I still can’t recollect how we ended up on the question of inner monologues. How many of us had an “inner voice”?

I don’t. I do have thoughts—visual, conceptual, even emotional—but not as voices. Suffice it to say both sides were adequately (not equally) represented in the room—the voice people, and the thoughts people.

But today’s edition isn’t really about either. Instead, it’s about something both camps can do: giving yourself permission.

I’ve always been fascinated by “in-between” spaces where the normal rules don’t apply. The one I’ve often seen leading to unpredictable and new journeys is the “post-job” or “post-exit” one. When someone quits a job or sells their company and doesn’t know what comes next. 

The ones who emerge renewed and richer (not in wealth, but in knowledge and perspectives) are the ones who “allowed themselves” to pace it out.

“I said I would just talk to people for the first two months,” said a recent founder.

This newsletter is about mental models, which are nothing but invented constructs to convince our own selves to look at the world differently. Each model is like a filter you wear on your eyes to see the world from another angle. The best ones help us see things better, so we can decide better.

The tough ones to wear are the ones where you see yourself. Because, often, we come up against unexamined and uncontested parts of ourselves that make no sense. Or the invisible rules that govern our behaviour.

“I must complete all the pending tasks.”

“I don’t miss deadlines.”

“I should work the weekend so I can be ready for Monday.”

“I must always peel oranges in an unbroken stretch.”

I am guilty of all of these. To the extent that I am virtually incapable of consciously taking an hour-long nap on a weekend, because, “there’s so much I could do”.

No, I don’t hear that as an inner voice. But it is like a constant presence. Like air or gravity. You don’t need to observe it, it’s just undeniably there and true.

For the last few years though, I’ve realised I can push back by “giving myself permission”. And not just simple permission, but an almost rebellious sort of permission, filled with energy and abandonment.

“I am not going to do this because I am no longer convinced it is important.”

“I am going to defer this by a month. I’ll see if it still makes sense then.”

“I am going to do this in a passable manner.”

“I am going to watch a few episodes of Dark Waters instead of catching up on pending reading.”

In each case, my decisions take me to places that are subconsciously classified as “bad, stay away”.

Subtraction. Procrastination. Unproductiveness. Mediocrity. Stagnation.

The true “unlock”, as they say, came about when I saw those places as valuable in their own right.

As the American author and teacher Gregg Krech puts it, we need to learn to get better at procrastinating. Procrastination of some kind is inevitable: indeed, at any given moment, you’ll be procrastinating on almost everything, and by the end of your life, you’ll have gotten around to doing virtually none of the things you theoretically could have done. So the point isn’t to eradicate procrastination, but to choose more wisely what you’re going to procrastinate on, in order to focus on what matters most. The real measure of any time management technique is whether or not it helps you neglect the right things.

Excerpt From Four Thousand Weeks: Time Management for Mortals – Burkeman, Oliver

And what if trying to do every “pending” task is actually what’s mediocre?

In the past, time management experts would recommend that you divide up your work into A tasks, B tasks, and C tasks. The concept was to do the A tasks first, then the B tasks, then the C tasks, when you can get to them. If priorities changed, you just changed the order of your As, Bs, and Cs. Doing all aspects of a job seemed possible then, if you just followed some basic time management rules.

[…]

And although there have been real gains in productivity since then, the days of A, B, and C tasks are over. Overwhelmed is the new normal.

Therefore, it’s actually a matter of professional life or death to get rid of your low-value work – tasks that mean little or nothing to customers or colleagues. Take an active approach. Design a new, do-able job for yourself.

Excerpt from Stop Doing Low-Value Work—Priscilla Claman (HBR)

And what if success can sometimes end up sowing the seeds of failure?

Why don’t successful people and organizations automatically become very successful? One important explanation is due to what I call “the clarity paradox,” which can be summed up in four predictable phases:

Phase 1: When we really have clarity of purpose, it leads to success.
Phase 2: When we have success, it leads to more options and opportunities.
Phase 3: When we have increased options and opportunities, it leads to diffused efforts.
Phase 4: Diffused efforts undermine the very clarity that led to our success in the first place.

Curiously, and overstating the point in order to make it, success is a catalyst for failure.

[…]

If success is a catalyst for failure because it leads to the “undisciplined pursuit of more,” then one simple antidote is the disciplined pursuit of less. Not just haphazardly saying no, but purposefully, deliberately, and strategically eliminating the nonessentials. Not just once a year as part of a planning meeting, but constantly reducing, focusing and simplifying. Not just getting rid of the obvious time wasters, but being willing to cut out really terrific opportunities as well. Few appear to have the courage to live this principle, which may be why it differentiates successful people and organizations from the very successful ones.

Excerpt from The Disciplined Pursuit of Less—Greg McKeown (HBR)

None of these concepts around inversions will be new to you. In fact, we’ve discussed versions of them over the years.

Subtraction. Procrastination. Unproductiveness. Mediocrity. Stagnation.

It’s easier for us to accept the intellectual arguments behind each of those. It’s much harder to internalise them and shift our own behaviour. Because the gatekeeper standing in the way is us. 

The idea of “giving yourself permission” is helpful for sidestepping that gatekeeper. 

We’ve also discussed the concept of “self-authoring” in an earlier edition. Giving ourselves permission is part of our shift to self-authoring. We realise we hold the rules, and can negotiate and rewrite them.

3. FP Recommendations ¯_(ツ)_/¯

You’ll agree there’s always been a sense of YOLO to First Principles’ scattershot “evolution”. We start things, we run things, we shut down things. Everything is always an ongoing experiment. The only constant is a high bar on authenticity, craft, and value.

So, in that spirit, I’m kicking off an intermittent section called “FP Recommendations” featuring sharp and short recommendations about interesting products that clear our high bar. Zero paid recommendations or sponsorships. Only personal usage counts. If you don’t like it, skip it. What can I say, ¯_(ツ)_/¯

This is the first edition of First Principles I’m not writing in Word. I’ve been a hardcore user of Microsoft Office since 2001. That’s 25 years. 

Even now, my first instinct is to open Word every time I want to write anything, or Excel if there are any numbers involved. But Microsoft’s relentless march towards slopifying (or is it enshittifying?) Office has given me pause. Between all the attempts to sneak AI settings into Office products and the terrible decision to rename Office into “365”, I’ve lost my faith. 

While .doc (or .docx) used to be the format de rigueur for knowledge work, that is no longer true. The new format is .md, a Markdown file. The open, minimal format is the default for the AI-pilled world we’re living in.

Unfortunately, most Markdown editors are optimised for coders, not writers. I tried a bunch. Obsidian. Zed. iA Writer.

But the one that finally clicked for me was Typora. It’s sleek, minimal, fast, and optimised for both writing and reading prose. It even allows me to export files as Word documents!

Do check it out if you’re looking for a solid Markdown-native editor. It’s not free, of course.

4. A corner of the internet

Hello, this is Tanim for the books section. 

Do you read website blogs or Substack articles? Tell me your favourites and why they have stuck with you here. I will share them in the next edition. 

Before I could afford to buy many books, blogs were my my main diet of reading. Some amazing writers wrote entire series there, spanning everything from fantasy  to political satire. 

I have always been in awe of writing where style pulls as much weight as plot or character, where how something is said matters as much as what’s being said.

A few categories come to mind. 

Helpful accounts of people’s lived experiences—how they have navigated the big decisions in  their lives, whether career, relationships, or money. Research cna back up a hypothesis, but nothing shapes the mental models we choose to live by quite like anyone else’s lived experience, whether it arrives as a philosophical ramble or plain old storytelling.  

Then, of course,  fiction, where the writer has full creative license over how the story gets told. This corner of the internet has also produced entire fandoms, writers crafting the story they wanted to read because no one had written it yet.

5. Movie pick(s) of the week:

Hey there!

This is Vishnu, with the movie picks for the week.

This time, I have three movies that I think aren’t really mainstream (well, at least according to me) but are titles that are really worth watching. I hope you enjoy them as much as I did 🙂

Tuner (English, Thriler/Drama, 2025)

A piano tuner with a heightened sense of hearing (and perfect pitch) gets embroiled in a criminal group as a safe-breaker. The movie is much more than that, though—it touches on themes of talent, love, and music. 

With some great sound design and solid acting, this is a movie you should check out.

Streaming on Prime Video/Apple TV, depending on your location.

Elephant (English, Psychological drama, 2003)

Based on the horrific 1999 Columbine High School massacre, this movie has a multi-perspective structure, showing the unfortunate day through the eyes of multiple students, with a clever editing style that brings it all together to the same timeline. I don’t think I’ve seen anything like it.

I’ll be honest, this was a tense watch for me, especially the last act, where I knew what was coming. That’s part of what makes it a brilliant movie, too.

Streaming on Hotstar. (Please go in blind. It will make for an even better experience)

The Outfit (English, Crime/Drama, 2022)

If you love the aesthetics of a Guy Ritchie movie, the tension of Knives Out, and extremely well-written dialogue, you will love this one.

The movie is shot entirely inside a small tailor shop, where the protagonist (played by Mark Rylance) makes beautiful clothes for the only people around who can afford them: a family of vicious gangsters. One night, everything goes south, and that’s the movie.

Stylishly shot, written and acted, this should definitely be on your watchlist. 

Streaming on Prime Video/Netflix, depending on where you are.
