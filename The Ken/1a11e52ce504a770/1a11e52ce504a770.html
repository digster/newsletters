“A cap is simply a number on a sheet.” 

The latest episode of Two by Two is about how we can fix insurance. 

But why are we discussing insurance, and what needs to be fixed?

Well, it’s because of what the Irdai, the insurance regulator, did last week. It put out a consultation paper arguing that selling insurance in India is far too expensive. So one of its proposed solutions is to institute a couple of dozen commission caps, essentially fundamentally rewiring the economics of how insurance will be sold and distributed in India.

The regulator has good reasons to be concerned. Over the last two years, insurance premiums in India have gone up by 28%. However, the amount insurers paid their distributors who sold those policies grew 125%. In motor insurance, it was worse: premiums rose by 34%, and commissions by 259%. 

This isn’t the first time the regulator has tried to solve this problem. 

Three years ago, it got rid of caps and decided to control spending on the insurer side. Now, it has decided that caps are back… with a vengeance. 

In today’s episode, I describe this as the “caramel popcorn” moment for insurance regulation in India. Or at least, that is how the proposal treats it. Specific caps for agents. Another set of caps for app-based products. Another cap for motor insurance. Another one for life. I spent a few hours reading through the table, and my head was swimming. 

How is one supposed to keep track of all of this? 

Also, how did we get here? 

And where do we go next?

To answer the question of how we can fix insurance selling in India, I decided to call two guests, each with their own perspective. 

Shrehith Karkera is a co-founder of Ditto Insurance, an insurance advisory company started in 2021 by the team behind Finshots. Ditto sells retail health and term insurance through hundreds of trained human advisors and stays out of motor, savings plans, and ULIPs. 

Last year it placed close to Rs 500 crore in premiums, and 95% of its customers renew their health policies at the end of the first year. In today’s podcast, Karkera argues that the way to fix insurance distribution is to pay for good conduct and enforce it, rather than cap commissions.

Ishaan Wadhwa is the founder of Kubrick Cube, a Bengaluru-based firm that advises insurers, governments, and development organisations across Asia and Africa on insurance strategy, risk, and social protection. He’s a qualified actuary with nearly two decades of experience in insurance. He has priced products from ULIPs to low-cost online term plans in India, worked on insurers’ balance sheets and solvency, and served as head of actuarial reporting at Prudential Cambodia. 

In the episode, he argues from the position of what a household needs protection against, rather than from the product being sold.

It’s a great episode.

What happened in 2023

Shrehith Karkera

Three years back was an interesting time in insurance. At that point in time, there was this understanding within the industry that there was an open secret. And this open secret is that what was being touted as the official commission rate based on the caps that Irdai had implemented was only commission caps on paper. But the commissions were paid nonetheless. However, they weren’t paid directly. Instead, they were paid through other channels. They were paid through third-party marketing entities. They were masked as payments of a different kind, but in effect, they were commissions. And everybody knew it. 

And the understanding within the industry was that this is something that is endemic to the industry and this is how we do business. Unfortunately, at that point in time, there was one chink in the whole process, which is that I think the GST department and the income tax department got wind of this. They began this massive investigation into these payments. Now, these payments technically were fictitious in nature because they were being routed as other forms of payment, when in fact they were commissions, and they’d been routed through other entities. And so the taxman wanted its dues. […] And Irdai at the time, I think, rightfully recognised that the only way to stop this is to actually reclassify these other incomes, which were actually commission income, as official income. So they said, look, we’re doing away with the caps, and what we’ll do instead is we’ll have some general guidelines on how insurance companies should be spending their money. And so maybe we’ll cap that, and in effect, we will try and control costs within distribution by imposing that cap.

Shrehith Karkera

You have to understand that the commission line, which is this 180% growth, 170% growth, is largely a function of reclassification. What was in 2023 as ORC off-the-book payments is now being officially classified as commissions. And the paper dedicates one line to that. If you read the entire paper, it’s the basis of the paper, by the way. But there’s this one line that says, hey, this is reclassified income, and that’s about it. 

And so technically speaking, if you actually want to look at how much commissions grew, you’ll have to separate the reclassification component and then look at costs. In fact, if you look at total expenses, they haven’t grown disproportionately at all. And so I think that’s something that you’ll have to recognise while reading the paper, because everybody online right now who’s reporting on the paper is taking this number at face value, and they’ve talked about how commissions have grown disproportionately, and consumers have borne the brunt, when in fact large components of this were merely reclassification.

Ishaan Wadhwa

They have curtailed the commissions being paid out hugely. Primarily, I think the most affected ones have been the corporate agents, and from what I see, there has been a cut across all the intermediaries based on the products and also based on the intermediary who is selling the specific product. From what I read of it, they want to link it to the amount of effort being put in to sell a certain kind of product. […] 

And so the Irdai wants to compensate people based on the effort people are putting in, hence massive commission cuts for corporate agents, and banks that are corporate agents largely. But that applies to brokers also; that applies to individual agents also. At the same time, they have increased commissions for the renewals of the premium because they want to promote a certain behaviour in terms of higher persistency. And they’re trying to address issues around the poor quality of the sales that have been made for a long period of time.

Where the regulator is right

Shrehith Karkera

But there are very specific situations where prices actually go up, which is an idea called reverse competition. Let me explain that idea. For instance, let’s assume a bank. So you have a corporate agent, a bank, who’s selling, let’s say, insurance with home loans, credit-linked home loans. Now, these are products where the customer doesn’t make a decision. There’s no choice for the customer. So when you’re taking a home loan, the insurance goes along with it, and the bank makes that choice. […] So now you have a bidding war going on. 

So every insurance company will say, look, I’m going to bid with my commissions. So insurance company A says, look, I’m going to give you 30%. B says 35%, 40%, 45%, 50%, and it goes on. So you see how even though there’s competition here, it actually bids up the prices, and ultimately the price is borne by the consumer, who, by the way, has no choice in any of this. The bank makes that choice. So the case for caps, at least at some level, is a reasonable one when you look at the dynamics at play here.

Shrehith Karkera

The only issue perhaps is that once this diagnosis becomes an all-encompassing solution, which is where you say, hey, you cut down distribution costs, and you’ll cut down premiums, and reduced premiums will improve penetration. Now, that’s a bit of a leap. And the same logic doesn’t apply to all insurance channels and all insurance products, which is perhaps the nuance you’ll have to identify here. Let’s say, for example, Ditto. Ditto has some influence, by the way. We offer some degree of access to our customer base. 

However, when a customer comes to us, I’m sure when we sold a policy to you, we did say, hey, this is what I’d recommend. But then again, outside those recommendations, you have a choice to pick any provider you want, or you could simply walk away from the distributor altogether. Whereas with certain channels, with motor insurance, or let’s say even with banks and these home loan products, you don’t have that choice.

Shrehith Karkera

My wife went to a bank recently, and she wanted to get some transactions printed on paper because we were filing for a visa application. The lady at the counter said, I can waive the Rs 1,000 fee if you buy a ULIP plan. Now, this kind of behaviour, if it is actually on a portal, you have to have massive penalties for this kind of behaviour.

What a distributor is paid for

Shrehith Karkera

Ditto’s approach to insurance selling has been one that mimics what a broker should do, which is that we are not just selling insurance policies; we have to make sure that you service it well enough. You ensure that, at the point of claim, you have a genuine case to make with the insurance company so that the claim goes through without challenges. And you do it in a way that offers value to both the customer and the insurer. 

So you’re doing a lot of work that the insurer would have done otherwise. They would have to try to build a good book. They would have to service these customers as well as we do. They would have to take on a lot of these costs. We’re saying, hey, let us do this for the insurer. And whatever value we accrue to the insurer, and whatever value we can extract from the insurer, let us give it back to customers. So if there’s a problem with claims, a problem with servicing, a problem with the initial application, we can push through those issues because we now have leverage with insurers. We offer this value, and the insurers are willing to work with us so that we can offer this value back to customers. So the customers are happy, the insurer is happy, and that’s how we’ve built this business.

Shrehith Karkera

Distributors do a lot of work there. Like Ditto. Right now, we have our advisory force, who have very little incentive to sell or mis-sell. And yet, we see that disclosures don’t happen appropriately all the time. So we have a separate verification team. Every time a sale is done, afterwards, to remove all the incentives, we have another person calling and doing a risk analysis. They go through the entire disclosure list; they talk to the customer, and they understand this. And you will not believe the kind of stuff that comes up in the verification call, even though the advisor has done all their due diligence and has very little incentive to mis-sell. 

And then, hopefully, once you do all of this, you have a claim experience that is, let’s say— I wouldn’t say it’s enjoyable, but at least one that isn’t as dastardly as most people assume the claim process to be. And somebody has to do all of this work. And now distributors have been doing it, distributors like Ditto. And if you set the cap below cost, effectively that means either you don’t do that, or you have a perverse incentive to do more in terms of sales and less in terms of servicing and all of these other things that keep costs down for the insurance company.

Shrehith Karkera

Right now we employ close to 1,600 people. And largely it’s a human endeavour. […] Last year, in retail health and term only, I think we placed close to ₹500 crores in premiums. And this year we were thinking of doubling that figure. And so, in terms of revenues, I think we are looking at more than half our revenues evaporating overnight. So we are thinking about a 50% plus cut. And again, we are a profitable company, so we have reserves; we can cushion the tide.

Shrehith Karkera

I don’t think you should look at app versus agent, because these are completely abstract entities. It doesn’t matter what your shape is. What matters is what you do. It could be an app; it could be an agent. If an app does all the work that an agent does, why should you differentiate the two? […] But there’s a second, more important argument, which is that insurance companies pay these commissions to distributors because they do a lot of the work that insurance companies would have to do otherwise. And it is more efficient for them to pay this to the distributor than do it themselves, including acquiring the customer and servicing the customer. 

Now, you place these caps, and you’ve said, look, now the obligation is on the insurance company to do all of this. Perhaps it was more efficient earlier; that is why they delegated it to distributors. Now it is no longer as efficient, because the distributor cannot do his business at the cost that the caps have prescribed. So the insurance company has to do it. […] In fact, in the entire paper, there is zero mention of distributor economics on retail health insurance, which is these pure protection products that the regulator really wants to push, that the regulator really wants to insure individuals on. The only distributor economics that comes up is, I would like to think, the large commercial brokers who operate at a 50-60% margin. I can say very confidently that we don’t operate on such margins.

Why insurance is hard to sell

Ishaan Wadhwa

However, I always look at insurance penetration with a pinch of salt, because it just measures your total premium as a percentage of GDP. […] That does not really reflect how resilient your households are, how much protection they have. You can still pay a Rs 1 lakh premium and earn Rs 20 lakh a year, and then have a Rs 10 lakh sum assured. S

o if something were to happen, you just get Rs 10 lakh, and the rest of your household is exposed. So there are better ways to measure the utility of insurance, and also the performance of insurance as a tool. Protection gap is one way, I think, to look at things in a more correct way, where they are actually measuring what percentage of your liabilities are exposed entirely. And in India, there was a study conducted by the National Insurance Academy, based in Pune. In 2023, if I’m not mistaken, the protection gap they estimated was 87%. That means if the leading income earner dies, then you only have 13% of what you would ideally need. Which is a huge gap, and that gap has increased from 83% in 2019.

Shrehith Karkera

Term insurance, however, is a pain to sell. It is almost impossible to sell a term insurance product because there is an implicit expectation from the consumer, even the urban tier 1 educated consumer, that if I were to spend this money over the lifetime of my policy, then I’m entitled to something. And entitlement is the key word there, because no matter how hard we try and educate a consumer on why it would not make sense for them to expect returns in this product, it always seems to backfire. They just don’t seem to get the logic. 

And it takes a whole lot of convincing for us to simply pitch term insurance as a product to a prospective buyer, who, by the way, is already considering term insurance when they come to us. […] And then they put up a fierce debate on why they’re entitled to some money, some return, some payback at some X period. And insurance companies, to their credit, or perhaps to their discredit, have created products to accommodate this request. So now you get some returns back, you get some premiums back, on what should be a plain vanilla term insurance product.

Ishaan Wadhwa

At the age of 30, you put something in front of that person and say, if you pay Rs 10,000 every month, if you die, your family will get Rs 1 crore. If you don’t die, no one will get any money. That sounds like a very abstract investment for that person, because that person will not see what is happening after he or she dies. In that person’s mindset, that money is going to waste. So, to break that barrier, it’s never easy. And I totally sympathise with [Shrehith] and all other distributors who try really hard to sell term insurance. 

Because even if the commission rates are very high for that, the absolute amount of money that you make versus a savings product, even with a lower commission, is still lower, because the ticket size is so small, since you’re not giving a guaranteed savings benefit.

Ishaan Wadhwa

Historically, we have been programmed to look at insurance as savings because of LIC’s intervention. And it’s not like LIC started it one fine day. It was very much driven by the policy objectives of the government at that time, to raise capital and invest in infrastructure. Because whatever money comes from the insurance industry, at least the savings products, goes to the government, into government bonds, and the government utilises that money to invest in highways. And in the ’50s and ’60s, that was a major source of capital for the government.

Cut one cost, and the others move

Shrehith Karkera

Now, if you map this arithmetic this way, you would see that if you bring down B, which is distribution cost, premiums should automatically come down. But that only works if all these variables are independent, that B doesn’t interact with A and B doesn’t interact with C. […] So let me give you the insurance example. Let’s say with retail health. You say that I’m going to cut down distribution costs. Sure, you do that. And let’s say one of the things that this will do is completely eliminate advice-based distributors from the market. I’m not saying it’ll happen, but let’s suppose it happens.

The quality of the book that the insurer is creating right now, the quality of their business, would automatically reduce. So you would see a jump in claim costs, which is also one of the variables defining your premiums. […] Now, if that book goes away tomorrow and the quality of the big book deteriorates, there’ll be an opposite impact. There’ll be an opposite force on the premium that pushes it upwards. And we did some modelling over the weekend, while replying to the consultation paper, to see what it will do to the 3 stated objectives, which are premiums, mis-selling and lives covered. And in our model, we didn’t see any reduction in mis-selling. We actually saw a dip in lives covered, and we saw only a modest increase or a decrease in premiums, which, by the way, we think will rationalise over the next year or 2, because most insurance companies make an underwriting loss. So it’s likely that these savings will move to their books, and you won’t see a massive drop in premiums.

Shrehith Karkera

Now, I think that argument is not very strong, and I’ll give you an example of why. Ishaan talked about how the total premium that you source from a savings policy is considerably higher. Now, let’s suppose that commission drops. It drops to whatever level, and yet I don’t think the banks will do away with their targets. [Irdai] has explicitly prohibited volume-linked incentives in its paper, or it’s asking to prohibit them. But I don’t think this behaviour will go away. Banks will still have targets. Now they’ll have to source those numbers through other channels. So if they’re making less on some of these products, they’ll try to see— if you squeeze a balloon on one side, that pressure has to go somewhere else. 

So effectively now you’re saying, hey, I’m going to make less from selling a term insurance product. How can I offset the impact? And I think what’ll likely happen is they’ll start pushing more savings products. Because the absolute cost there is minimal, but when you talk about the quantum of premiums, there’s an opportunity to earn more through these channels. […] And for a bank, by the way, the running cost, the incremental marginal cost, is negligible. So they can still survive in the market. It’s all the brokers that are probably pushed away.

Ishaan Wadhwa

The initial amount of effort to acquire a customer, if it is more, then you need to compensate the intermediary for that. If I just go to the basics, a first-principles approach, I would like to chart the exact chain of where the intermediary gets involved, right from inception, from talking to multiple people and then converting one policy. So that entire effort that goes into speaking to 10 people and converting one policy, plus the advice being given at that time and on an ongoing basis. […] There has to be some basis, in my view, somewhere to quantify that number. Even to quantify a 35% cap or 40% cap, there has to be some rationale. […] 

So first-year commissions will inevitably be higher where there is more interaction acquiring that customer. Renewals naturally go down because now things are automated; you get the reminders to renew the policies, and/or the agent reminds you. The time involved from the agent side to pursue renewals is less; hence you pay a lower renewal commission. But that also may disincentivise the agent to pursue the renewals and maybe ask you to buy a new policy from somewhere else.

How to fix it

Ishaan Wadhwa

What I also see is that these regulations reform what an advisor should look like, and what kind of people want to be in this profession. Because now it’s not as lucrative as it used to be. You can still make money, but I also feel that when the cap is 30% or 35%, not everyone should get 30% or 35%. You’d reach 30-35% through certain incentives, by having some quality parameters in place. Your base starting commission should be lower than that, way lower.

Ishaan Wadhwa

At the same time, from the supply side, need-based selling and advice— the form of this profession may evolve eventually. From what I feel, you need specialists who really understand the household-level balance sheet rather than an individual’s. I don’t know what the recommendation is on the training aspects of agents and what kind of advice is being given. To me, it’s like a financial planner. 

If you’re advising somebody to invest Rs 1 lakh a month or Rs 1 lakh a year, it’s not a small amount. So you need a specialist who understands the landscape really well. So quality advice, need-based, and being there through the entire journey, from a claims standpoint as well. And perhaps this touches upon the insurance industry as such: they view claims as just a payout, whereas they are in the business of paying claims. Claims are an investment rather, I feel, because trust is such a profound thing in this industry, because it’s such a powerful tool. […] I don’t know how effective a premium rate reduction is, to be honest, when it comes to demand. I have not seen, oh, there’s a 5% cut in insurance premiums, and people are very happy about it and going to buy insurance. That will never happen.

Shrehith Karkera

We spend 3 months training our advisors, and we pay those salaries because they’re on our payroll. And you don’t get any money back for the first 6 months if you want to do need-based advice. And I would say that our advisors are perhaps not the most qualified in some sense. They’re really, really good. But you could argue that they should be even more qualified, as Ishaan pointed out, because this is financial planning when it comes to term. They should be robust, but there’s a cost associated with it. You’re taking somebody on, and then you’re investing 3 months, and then they pay off in the 7th or 8th month. Hopefully they stick around.

Shrehith Karkera

Look, a cap is simply a number on a sheet. There’s an argument to be made that you may go back to the pre-2023 days because there’ll be leakages in other ways. The only cap that will work is the cap on the insurer’s expense, because that’s where you can fix the cap. The second thing is that you cannot police outcomes with a cap. You can police outcomes by making sure that there’s a strict penalty on conduct, or there are incentives for better conduct. You have to design conduct-based incentives. So even if you were to stick with the caps, because a regulator isn’t just acting in the interest of the policyholder, they should be seen as acting in the interest of the policyholder as well. And it’s very important. And in that context, I don’t think the cap should go away. And I’m going to make that argument despite being a distributor in the space.

Shrehith Karkera

First-year health insurance renewal rates are 95%. An R1 renewal rate of 95% is unheard of. And the reason why we can maintain that rate is that this is what need-based selling looks like. And if you have incentives designed around conduct, saying if there is a mis-selling complaint, you impose a hard penalty on it if it goes in favour of the policyholder, which is what the paper says as well. Most outcomes are rewarded in favour of the policyholder. Then you have to have strict penalties, but you also have to have incentives around that. Because otherwise you’ll have petrol pumps and vegetable vendors selling insurance without need-based analysis, and produce exactly the different kinds of outcomes. So you have caps, but you incentivise distributors whose conduct is actually good. So for instance, if the quality of the book is very good, if the persistency ratio is very high, if you qualify for this, then you are entitled to a higher reward from the insurance company. And you make this public, so that nobody can game the system, and you police it also. […] It’s like at a traffic light. You can put a fine structure, but the only way to actually get compliance is to enforce it. You have to have a camera looking at that guy, and then he’ll wear the helmet.
