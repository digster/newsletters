Dear Ishan, namaskaara! I’m doing another refresh of my Kannada, and this time I’m trying my darnedest to use it in speech as much as possible.

We adopted Gabru, our bandit-eyed Indie, in 2018 from CUPA’s Second Chance kennel (it’s a great place to hang around with dogs, even if you aren’t ready to adopt one yet). He was a three-month old puppy. 

Today, he and I are almost exactly the same age. I’ll enter my sixth decade soon, and so will he. The broad calculation for converting (Indie mixed-breed) dog years to human years is 15 human years for their first year, then 9 for their second, and then 4.5–5 of ours for every subsequent one of theirs.

Which means he, at 8.5, and me, nearing 50, are around the same age. 

It’s a sobering thought. 

Without anthropomorphising too much, I wonder how he’d consider his life thus far. If he could (I’m aware dogs don’t have long term memories like ours), how might he reflect on the decades gone by? The toddler, the baby, the boy, the teenager, the young adult, the prime adult, the middle-aged adult. 

Might he have memories of sticking his head out of the car, taking in the rushing wind and sights and smells? The anxiety when we left him in boarding kennels without being able to tell him we’d be back for him? The boundless joy at finally seeing us back and being driven back to his own home? The curiosity and awe at seeing an endless beach and sea and waves for the first time? The joy of chasing his humans up and down the beach, the waves lapping at his paws?

What were his best years? Is he dealing with his own mid-life crisis? I wish we could talk.

The best decade of our lives

As Gabru and I ponder the second “halves” of our respective lives, an evergreen question pops into my mind. Which decade of my life was I the happiest in? Can you guess?

There have been few topics as subjective and vague, but also as scientifically studied, as the correlation between our age and happiness.

Happiness and satisfaction aren’t merely subjective and personal, they are also ephemeral. Our recollections of happiness change over the years. Our memories fade and play tricks on us. The few vivid experiences crowd out the many that really mattered.

And yet, scientists the world over continue to study this. They too are, after all, human.

I remember when I first got hooked on this topic as a journalist. It was in 2018 when I came across the wonderful WSJ article titled, “What is the perfect age?”

“If you had a pill that could stop biological aging in its tracks, when would you take it?” asks Dr. Olshansky, a professor at UIC School of Public Health in Chicago.

He asks his university students this question. Many think 30 is old, so they would take the pill in their 20s. He asked his father, then 95 years old. His father said 50 was the best year because the kids were grown and he was in good health. Dr. Olshanky, 63, says life is good for him now, but if he had to pick a perfect year, it would probably be 50, too, because that was before he started having little aches and pains.

What Is the Perfect Age? By Clare Ansberry, WSJ

I was in my early 40s. The Ken was just two years old. Our son was in school. 

I don’t remember what my answer might have been back then. But I’m clear now that the 40s have undoubtedly been my favourite decade till now. I was 40 when Seema, Sumanth, and I started The Ken. There is the obvious explanation of recency bias, because many studies show that people tend to rate their current decades as their best ones too.

But if you asked a friend to guess what most people (not them) thought was the best decade, they’d probably say the 20s. Yet, data says otherwise.

Laura Carstensen’s favorite lecture of the year is the one when she tells undergrads at Stanford University that—contrary to what they’ve probably been told and believe—these are not the best years of their lives. Those come much, much later.

“I look out at a sea of 100 students sitting in a classroom, and I love the look on their faces. It’s just such relief,” says Carstensen, a professor of psychology and founding director of the Stanford Center on Longevity. “I can visibly see it, because not only are the late teens and 20s the worst time in our lives—with the highest rates of loneliness, anxiety, and depression—but people are constantly telling [young people] that they’re the best years.”

The best years of your life are probably still ahead, Time

Perhaps the best way to understand this is through the widely-acknowledged U-shaped curve of happiness that comes up decade after decade, country after country. 

Life is not a long slow decline from sunlit uplands towards the valley of death. It is, rather, a U-bend.

When people start out on adult life, they are, on average, pretty cheerful. Things go downhill from youth to middle age until they reach a nadir commonly known as the mid-life crisis. So far, so familiar. The surprising part happens after that. Although as people move towards old age they lose things they treasure—vitality, mental sharpness and looks—they also gain what people spend their lives pursuing: happiness.

The U-bend shows up in studies not just of global well-being but also of hedonic or emotional well-being. One paper, published this year by Arthur Stone, Joseph Schwartz and Joan Broderick of Stony Brook University, and Angus Deaton of Princeton, breaks well-being down into positive and negative feelings and looks at how the experience of those emotions varies through life. Enjoyment and happiness dip in middle age, then pick up; stress rises during the early 20s, then falls sharply; worry peaks in middle age, and falls sharply thereafter; anger declines throughout life; sadness rises slightly in middle age, and falls thereafter.

The U-bend of life, The Economist

There’s also a wonderful book that expands upon this concept. I just ordered it. It’s called The Happiness Curve.

If we look at ages from the POV of health, things sort of change. 

A widely-cited study from Stanford found that our bodies do not age very smoothly or linearly. The two big shift years are 44 and 60. Coincidentally, the first time I felt “middle-aged” was when, at 45, I got a rotator cuff injury, and then, in short order, plantar fasciitis and various types of tendinopathies. Healing wasn’t as fast as I was used to. I ended up becoming a reluctant fan and student of physiotherapy.

A new Stanford Medicine study shows that many of our molecules and microorganisms dramatically rise or fall in number during our 40s and 60s.

Researchers assessed many thousands of different molecules in people from age 25 to 75, as well as their microbiomes – the bacteria, viruses and fungi that live inside us and on our skin – and found that the abundance of most molecules and microbes do not shift in a gradual, chronological fashion. Rather, we undergo two periods of rapid change during our life span, averaging around age 44 and age 60. A paper describing these findings was published in the journal Nature Aging Aug. 14.

“We’re not just changing gradually over time; there are some really dramatic changes,” said Michael Snyder, PhD, professor of genetics and the study’s senior author. “It turns out the mid-40s is a time of dramatic change, as is the early 60s. And that’s true no matter what class of molecules you look at.”

Massive biomolecular shifts occur in our 40s and 60s, Stanford Medicine researchers find By Rachel Tompa, Stanford Medicine

I’m happy to report that although I’m already past the critical decade to take control of my health (36–46), I think I did a decent enough job through it. No regrets.

Enough about me. Let’s talk about you.

Dig, dig, find the big meteor from the sky

“If you’d never seen a meteorite before it’s just a little dark crusted rock,” Johnson said. (Tanim here)

When the big rock blazing through the sky hits the ground, only remnants remain. 

We kind of knew that. But how many of us imagined it. 

If you do not have any plans for this weekend, select a cosy reading spot or just pick up this book and read wherever you are, comfortable, or not. It makes you forget that you are not in the best of places, whether it’s the summer heat or the pile of laundry you haven’t yet attended to. If you are reading another book, share that here with us. 

The Meteorites by Helen Gordon has many interesting facts strewn all over as the writer tells the story of its namesake, such as this one:

In 1915 an amateur archaeologist digging in the ruins of a Native Pueblo-style dwelling near Camp Verde in Arizona found what appeared to be a child’s stone burial cist dating to around ad 1100–1200. Instead, lying inside was a 61.5-kg iron meteorite swaddled in a blanket of turkey feathers.

Or it makes you web-search more things that lead you to pictures like this:

Source: Wikipedia

If this doesn’t look futuristic, then sue me (not literally ._.) 

This brings me to another of my favourite observations from the book:

Space feels like the future. (And it feels like the future even when it is arguably the past, no human having trodden on a celestial object since 1972.) And perhaps because of this a paradox arises. Though meteorites are so very old, they seem ultimately to speak more to this future than to the past. “Something,” as Golia puts it in Meteorite, “beyond our grasp yet still within reach … they inspire a striving for discovery, the ever-new.”

While the book is about meteorites, the contextualisation done by the author, whether through examples or the many dialogues, adds a lot to the book. Here is one:

Humans don’t do well with large numbers. There is something unhomely about them. Try to visualise a heap of pebbles. Tens of pebbles are easy enough to imagine but what do a thousand pebbles look like? Could you distinguish between two thousand and three thousand? I have a sense of what twenty-four hours feels like but I have no sense – not emotionally, bodily – of what twenty-four million years would feel like. The brain rebels.

Would you say this is from a detailed book about meteorites? 

As Ganesh Chaturthi approaches and lights decorate many street corners, I can’t help but imagine we looked at the stars, found them pretty, and decided to make our own. 

And predynastic Egyptians may have seen a streak of light falling through the sky and named that object Bia-n-pet “iron from the sky” once it landed. 

The ever-expansive sky, as it always is above us, is also always among us in the ground, whether through lights, words, or the now very expensive metals and museum relics.
