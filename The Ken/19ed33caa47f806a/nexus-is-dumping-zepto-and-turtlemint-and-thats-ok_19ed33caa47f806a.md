Long and Short
Wed, 17 Jun 26
The story behind the numbers and motivations driving India's stock market.
Good Morning Ishan,
Having spent a decade covering Indian businesses, I have noticed that venture capitalists talk about “DPI” the way England fans talk about winning the Fifa World Cup.
First comes the unwavering optimism. Then there are elaborate theories about why “this time is different”. Finally, there is a miraculous ability to postpone judgment until the next cycle.
This DPI is called distribution to paid-in capital. Essentially, it’s the ratio of actual cash returned to limited partners (LPs) to actual cash invested. It is the only number that matters, in the sense that it is the number that represents money that has actually left the fund and arrived in an LP’s account, as opposed to a spreadsheet valuation that represents money that theoretically exists somewhere.
There is a whole other number, IRR, which represents what the return would be if you could somehow convert your illiquid stakes into cash right now at current marks. IRR is fine. People use it to raise their next fund. But if you are an LP and your endowment or pension has been waiting since 2011 for actual dollars to arrive, IRR is not very satisfying. DPI is the thing.
This is Nexus Venture Partners’ DPI moment. [Turtlemint](https://the-ken.com/story/policybazaar-made-insurance-e-commerce-turtlemint-is-betting-against-it/), the insurance distribution platform Nexus has backed since 2015, is set to open its Rs 883 crore IPO this week, with Nexus leading the offer-for-sale (OFS) by offloading 9 million shares. It is the latest entry in what has become a busy stretch for the firm.
Nexus is also the dominant seller in the Zepto IPO, a lot of shares, about 77% of the total offer-for-sale by count, which we will get to, and it has spent the better part of two years doing rolling block deals in Delhivery.
Naturally, the media coverage has been warm.
Nexus closed its eighth fund at $700 million in December, the largest venture fund raised globally that year, and the exit activity and the new fundraise together have produced a narrative in which Nexus is harvesting its alpha after two decades of patient India investing, and everything is going rather well.
While the exits are real, what do they actually represent? A well-engineered harvest machine delivering on a decade of promise, or a cohort of long-held assets finally reaching liquidity in a receptive market window, more or less simultaneously, for reasons that have as much to do with the IPO calendar as with Nexus’ particular genius?
The two things are not mutually exclusive, but they have different implications for what you should conclude about Nexus as an institution. So, it is worth trying to tell them apart.
