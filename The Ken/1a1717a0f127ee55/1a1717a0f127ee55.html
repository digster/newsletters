Tell me, how do you make your tea? I know, it sounds like the most pointless question I could ask. But think about it for a minute. 

Within just your immediate family, how many variations are there? Include your close friends and see the variations increase. Move a few states or cities, and there’s more. 

Every cup is a recipe: how much water and milk, which tea, which spices and in what form, and above all the order. Does the milk go in before the tea or after? Do you boil the leaves or steep them off the heat? Two people with the same ingredients can end up with different cups just by changing when things go in.

And we swear by our method and style.

Mine starts with just water coming to a boil. In parallel, I dry-roast cloves, split cardamom pods and a few pieces of cassia bark (yes, every day). And in parallel, I peel and dice fresh ginger into tiny cubes. The roasted spices and diced ginger go into the water as it’s heating up. Once it boils, in goes a splash of low-fat milk; bring it to one more boil (the classic Indian “almost boil over” point), then switch off the heat. It’s only then that the tea goes in (a roughly 60:40 mix of Darjeeling and CTC tea), gets a stir and then rests covered and undisturbed for five to seven minutes. No sugar, no sweeteners (tea, especially Darjeeling tea, has a natural sweetness of its own that’s best tasted sans the added variety). 

This is how I’ve been making tea every single morning for too many years for me to remember exactly how many.

How did I get to this? A lot of experiences, recommendations, and nudges (pre-mixing two teas, the Darjeeling for flavour and the CTC for body, came from my wife many years ago) over the years.

Now, I’m sure some of you think I’m nuts. Many others must think I have too much time, or that I’m OCPD. But I hope most of you realise that most of us have our own such quirky chai (tea) recipes that we swear by.

So, aren’t you curious about them? And aren’t you eager to tell us what your secret recipe is? Come on.

If you must vacillate, vacillate consciously

Ambidexterity—the ability to use both hands interchangeably for most tasks—is an incredible ability and skill. I’ve been trying to teach myself very basic versions in a few areas (I use a horizontal mouse with my left hand at work, and a vertical one with my right hand at home), but I am still fundamentally right-handed and only clunkily left-handed.

But would you rather be known for being ambidextrous or for vacillating? Ha ha.

Because vacillating is a bad thing, we’ve been taught, scolded or mocked from school through to our careers. Make a decision and stick to it! Don’t swing from one point of view to another! Be decisive!!

But what if vacillating is actually a great strategy, provided you use it consciously? 

Let me switch from people to organisations for a bit. Now, the world’s most successful and lasting organisations do two things really well over time—exploration (searching for new capabilities) and exploitation (refining what already works and generating more profit from it). 

It’s almost impossible for an organisation to do both at the same time over a long period. Thus, researchers who have studied resilient organisations usually bucket them into two types, based on the strategy they use to balance the two asks.

  • Ambidexterity: doing both at the same time, usually through separate structures inside the organisation.
  • Vacillation: switching between being an exploring organisation and an exploiting one over time, by reshaping itself periodically.

Do you want to guess which kind of organisations produce better long-term performance? The ones that vacillate.

Vacillation, when done deliberately, is called time pacing or temporal ambidexterity (the ability to alternate between two modes over time).

For most managers, event pacing constitutes the familiar and natural order of things. Companies change in response to events such as moves by the competition, shifts in technology, poor financial performance, or new customer demands. Event pacing is about creating a new product when a promising technology comes out of the R&D laboratory, entering a new market in response to a move by a competitor, or making an acquisition because an attractive target becomes available. Managers who event-pace follow a plan and deviate from it only when performance weakens. In markets that are stable, event pacing is an opportunistic and effective way to deal with change. By definition, however, it is also a reactive and often erratic strategy.

In contrast, time pacing refers to creating new products or services, launching new businesses, or entering new markets according to the calendar.1 Even though time-paced companies can be extraordinarily fast, it is important not to confuse time pacing with speed. By definition, time pacing is regular, rhythmic, and proactive. For example, 3M dictates that 30% of revenues will come from new products every year, Netscape introduces a new product about every six months, British Airways refreshes its service classes every five years, and Starbucks opens 300 stores per year to hit the goal of 2,000 outlets by the year 2000. Time pacing is about running a business through regular deadlines to which managers synchronise the speed and intensity of their efforts. Like a metronome, time pacing creates a predictable rhythm for change in a company.

Time Pacing: Competing in Markets that Won’t Stand Still by Kathleen Eisenhardt and Shona L. Brown (HBR, March–April 1998)

Organisations that vacillate strategically end up “like a metronome, creating a predictable rhythm for change in a company”.

In the companies we studied, time pacing had a powerful psychological impact. Time pacing creates a relentless sense of urgency around meeting deadlines and concentrates individual and team energy around common goals. As one manager says, “It’s like running a marathon in 100-yard bursts.” Although the tempo may be fast, it is predictable and so gives people a sense of control in otherwise chaotic markets. People become focused, efficient, and confident about the task at hand, which leads to enhanced performance.

In addition to creating a sense of urgency, time pacing disciplines managers to excel at two critical, but often neglected, processes essential to success in changing markets. The first is managing transitions, or the shifts from one activity to the next. The second is managing rhythm, or the pace at which companies change. Companies that march to the rhythm of time pacing build momentum, and companies that effectively manage transitions sustain that momentum without missing important beats.

Such “bimodal” rhythms aren’t just for organisations.

“Jung’s approach is what I call the bimodal philosophy of deep work. This philosophy asks that you divide your time, dedicating some clearly defined stretches to deep pursuits and leaving the rest open to everything else. During the deep time, the bimodal worker will act monastically—seeking intense and uninterrupted concentration. During the shallow time, such focus is not prioritized. This division of time between deep and open can happen on multiple scales. For example, on the scale of a week, you might dedicate a four-day weekend to depth and the rest to open time. Similarly, on the scale of a year, you might dedicate one season to contain most of your deep stretches (as many academics do over the summer or while on sabbatical).”

From Deep Work by Cal Newport

One of the best short Paul Graham essays also talks about this.

There are two types of schedule, which I’ll call the manager’s schedule and the maker’s schedule. The manager’s schedule is for bosses. It’s embodied in the traditional appointment book, with each day cut into one hour intervals. You can block off several hours for a single task if you need to, but by default you change what you’re doing every hour.

When you use time that way, it’s merely a practical problem to meet with someone. Find an open slot in your schedule, book them, and you’re done.

Most powerful people are on the manager’s schedule. It’s the schedule of command. But there’s another way of using time that’s common among people who make things, like programmers and writers. They generally prefer to use time in units of half a day at least. You can’t write or program well in units of an hour. That’s barely enough time to get started.

Maker’s Schedule, Manager’s Schedule by Paul Graham

Dear reader, this is Tanim with the FP books section. Have you read anything interesting recently? Tell us about it here. 

There are these two young fish swimming along and they happen to meet an older fish swimming the other way, who nods at them and says, “Morning, boys. How’s the water?”

Thus starts a keynote by David Foster Wallace, whose Infinite Jest we have talked about in earlier editions of First Principles. 

That doorstop of a book can feel like climbing a mountain. This one doesn’t. This Is Water is the easy entry point. Alternatively, if you want to listen to the book, you can do that here. 

It began as a speech and was published as a book only after his death. And according to Wikipedia, it received less recognition than the speech itself. I guess that’s one rare instance where the movie was better than the book. 

Beyond being a short read, you should pick it up because it talks about living a compassionate life, since it’s a speech given to graduating students. As its full name goes: This Is Water: Some Thoughts, Delivered on a Significant Occasion, about Living a Compassionate Life. I am sure many books have been written on this subject, but Wallace’s backstory makes me want to read this more. His biographer noted that he was editing the write-up right up until he stood on the podium. 

Either way, it’s a good Sunday afternoon read or listen, whichever suits you more.
