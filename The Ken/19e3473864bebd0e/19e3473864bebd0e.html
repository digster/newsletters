Jumper is Prince’s brood brother (they’re both around six months old). Yes, he’s got springs in his legs and is always jumping for treats.

Finally, there’s Picky Eater, who’s Prince’s brood brother (they’re now about a year old). He’s introverted, quieter and a fussy eater, often ignoring the treats that others are jumping, rolling and falling over each other for.

3. Loss functions and self-authoring

The origin of today’s edition traces back to late 2025. Chaitanya Chokkareddy, the CTO at Ozonetel and a good friend, posted a link to the importance of loss functions in our lives.

Loss functions are essentially mathematical models commonly used in machine learning and data science to evaluate the “loss” in the output of a predictive model. Loss in these contexts simply means the difference between what the model predicted or generated, versus what the actual ground reality was. If you’d like to go deep, take a detour here.

To go back to the post Chaitanya linked to, here it is.

In AI, the loss function doesn’t just measure mistakes—it also guides improvement. It tells the model how to get better. For students, the loss function is usually exams. They measure performance but rarely show how to improve. No feedback loops. No fine-tuning. Just a score. Exams measure output. Learning systems measure improvement. Maybe it’s time we update the loss function for education—one that rewards reflection, feedback, and continuous growth.

– Naga Supraja Yarava

What are our most used loss functions as adults? Peer comparison, parental expectations, and prestige hierarchies? Or perhaps, the lingering hum of “what I should have done by now?”

Loss functions are feedback loops, not discrete checkpoints. When the result of a model is significantly different from what was expected, the variance is taken as a signal to “backpropagate” through the many tens of thousands or even millions of steps it took to get to the result. So that it can “tune” those steps to produce a more accurate result the next time. 

This is a continuous process, so that models improve with each pass. 

It should be no different for us, too. We expect or desire a certain outcome in our lives, but rarely follow through with the discipline to build a model that will get us there. And even among those of us who do, fewer still design loss functions to tell us what went wrong and what we need to correct. 

We don’t notice this on a regular basis, but only during certain “milestone” moments in our lives, like a promotion, parenthood, leaving a job or reaching midlife. 

“I should be happy, but I am not” arises in these moments as disquiet.

As I read more on this, I realised that such life transitions are forced “re-authoring” moments. 

Wait, what? Re-authoring what? What was the original authoring?

Of our selves.

We understand that we are a person, with thoughts, feelings and beliefs that are independent from the standards and expectations of our environment. We can now distinguish the opinions of others from our own opinions to formulate our own “seat of judgment”. We become consumed with who we are — this is the kind of person I am, this is what I stand for.

We develop an internal sense of direction and the capacity to create and follow our own course.

How To Be An Adult— Kegan’s Theory of Adult Development – Natalie Mallel

Per Kegan, only about a third of adults reach what he calls the “self-authoring mind.” The rest of us are organised, even at high levels of performance, around external expectations.

Now, I’m a founder, and I’ve interviewed a lot of founders at length, too. Not just for the First Principles podcast, but also over the years as a journalist. 

Most founders have incredibly strong self-authorship for their company. You hear it through phrases like “fake it till you make it”, “reality distortion field”, “true believer”, “drinking the Kool-Aid”, or the Gen-Z version, “Delulu is the solulu.”

But, ironically (or perhaps consequentially), most founders don’t have as strong self-authorship for their own lives. Which means that the metric they apply at work (“build something I’d be proud of”) doesn’t always transfer home to “be a person I’d be proud of.”

Now, I’m not implying this as a norm, or letting myself off the hook. But it exists. Maybe it’s because there’s only so much self-authorship heavy lifting you can do in your life?

Coming back to loss functions in our lives, here’s a long essay written in 2006 by Y Combinator founder Paul Graham that strikes virtually all the right notes on how to think about it. 

With such powerful forces leading us astray, it’s not surprising we find it so hard to discover what we like to work on. Most people are doomed in childhood by accepting the axiom that work = pain. Those who escape this are nearly all lured onto the rocks by prestige or money. How many even discover something they love to work on? A few hundred thousand, perhaps, out of billions.

It’s hard to find work you love; it must be, if so few do. So don’t underestimate this task. And don’t feel bad if you haven’t succeeded yet. In fact, if you admit to yourself that you’re discontented, you’re a step ahead of most people, who are still in denial. If you’re surrounded by colleagues who claim to enjoy work that you find contemptible, odds are they’re lying to themselves. Not necessarily, but probably.

How to do what you love by Paul Graham

It’s incredible how Graham has managed to not just keep writing his signature deep and meaningful essays over decades, but also how he seamlessly connected and extends them. 

A loose “follow-up” to the earlier piece is “When to do what you love,” written in 2024.

And if you’re interested in a more hands-on, direct approach, consider “Designing your life,” the bestselling book by Stanford designers Bill Burnett and Dave Evans.

3. “Only Humans”

Thirty of you replied to last week’s poll about “human writing”. Roughly half sent their own writing, while the others sent something they had found. I am not excerpting the own-writing entries, but the reasons you gave for believing in your own writing are interesting.

When defending someone else’s writing, we seem to reach for craft. Wodehouse’s invented names. Wallace’s irony. Naval’s contradictions. The argument is stylistic and borderline forensic.

But when defending our own writing, we reach for biography. I was there. I felt it. I observed it. You were inside the moment the writing came out of. Several of you added a second move: this is rough, this contradicts itself, this is unpolished, no AI would leave these edges. Lack of polish, not craft, became the evidence.

The same FP community, answering the same question, with two completely different theories of what humanness in writing actually is 🙂

4. Boring books

“For someone who found the book very boring, you have a lot to say.”

Do you also have a book that you found boring? Share your thoughts here. 

The quote above was addressed to me (Tanim) and is from Rohin during one of our training sessions, after he asked us to read a book called Systems Thinking.

That caught me off guard. I had done nothing but complain to my friends while reading the book because the writing style wasn’t fun and the content was abstract, even when rooted in examples (though I was skipping due to a time crunch). 

But when asked questions that made me reflect on what I read, I had to admit there was a great deal of learning we could all benefit from. Even if some of the lessons seem obvious, seeing them written down and explained with proof of how they apply in the real world creates different associations with that “thing” you already know and makes it more concrete. 

That’s how you learn something best: through a combination of not just theory and practice, but also evaluation. 

And a question-answer session that helps you do just that makes a big difference. Is that how you feel, Vishnu, when you attend multiple quizzes? You start a cycle of connections to retain knowledge, and as you get some right and some wrong, it helps you better understand and retain that knowledge, regardless of the outcome. 

This mustn’t be a summary which (because of our familiarity with the text) would not receive as much attention or thought. When active recall or reflection is involved, anything that requires an “output” from you is where the magic happens. 

If boring books do not have the charm our writers bring, it’s hard to keep reading (not that we ever cover boring topics, but not every topic is everyone’s jam) and to understand. 

Do you have a book that you found boring to read, but that made you think a lot? Please share here. 

For peaceful purposes, please keep religious and school books (if that’s what you thought of) aside.

5. Art form in an art form

Hey there! 

It’s Vishnu, back again after a week’s break, with the movies section.

I’ve come with an absolutely wild story for you:

In 2009, Art historian Gergely Barki was at home, watching Stuart Little on TV with his three-year-old daughter, Lola.

But out of nowhere, something caught his eye:

For any untrained eye, this would be just another frame in the movie. But as for Barki, he could not believe it when he noticed the painting hanging behind Geena Davis and Hugh Laurie.

It was Sleeping Lady with Black Vase by Robert Bereny, considered lost after World War II:

The painting had gone through a wild ride:

It was sold for $40 at a charity auction in San Diego in the mid-1990s and then sold to an antique store in California for $400.

Later on, it was purchased by a set designer, on behalf of Sony Pictures, for $500.

Barki could not record his TV screen to make sure it was the painting he thought it was, but since it came on screen several times in the movie, he was able to confirm his intuition.

He then wrote to several Hollywood production companies to try to track the (now very valuable) painting. It had, by then, ended up on the walls of a set designer’s house.

Barki met her at a park in Washington DC, where, apparently, he borrowed a screwdriver from a hot dog seller to get the painting out for inspection – and confirmed that it was genuine.

Ultimately, it was brought back to Hungary, put on auction, and sold to an unnamed collector in 2014 for $285,700.

Imagine having a painting worth a quarter of a million dollars on a movie set, let alone your home, eh!?
