1. Cybernetic AI feedback loops and the Socratic Method

A few days ago, I had my first true cybernetic AI feedback loop moment, a concept postulated by the late Norbert Wiener—high school grad at 11, college grad at 14, and Ph.D at 18. Wiener was born in 1894.

“Wiener realized that almost all complex systems are driven by feedback loops of information. That is, communication isn’t linear, flowing just from a sender to a receiver. Rather, in many systems it forms a loop. A thermostat senses the temperature in a room, compares it to its setting and activates the furnace. As the room heats up, it cancels out the thermostat’s alert, and the furnace deactivates. And so forth. The system becomes “intelligent” if it can retain memories of past performances and use them to improve over time.

Nobody before Wiener had ever categorized that this information-based mechanism of feedback and adjustment is what drives many systems and keeps them stable. From this insight grew the field of cybernetics.

Feedback loops are also at work in the brain, Wiener recognized. Neurons process sensory signals (the foot lands on loose gravel), initiate processes (muscles in the leg activate to keep the balance), then monitor and adjust the output. By individually strengthening connections between certain neurons, the brain can encode past experiences for a better performance in the future. Today, this forms part of the theoretical foundation of neural network-based deep-learning circuits, and artificial intelligence itself.”

From Cybernetics to AI: the pioneering work of Norbert Wiener

On Thursday I had an impromptu hour-long brainstorming meeting with a bunch of colleagues over a hairy, unsolved problem at The Ken. We went in with zero preparation or assumptions, fully prepared to “just discuss”.

Fortunately, one of my colleagues had the presence of mind to record the discussion. He sent me the transcript shortly after.

I scrubbed it of my colleagues’ names and gave it to Claude, simply asking, “What do you see?”

Here’s what its first few sentences were:

This is a transcript of an internal meeting or discussion, likely at a company called The Ken (an Indian media/journalism company). Here’s what I see:

The conversation centers on <the hairy problem>— and it’s a rich, Socratic-style discussion led by what appears to be a senior leader (possibly the founder) coaching a product/design team.

It followed that up by defining the core problem, the arguments against our earlier approaches, the benefits of the new approach I had in mind, and key design principles.

I was supposed to present a pre-read proposal for this project early next week. So I asked Claude to generate it based on the hour-long chat I had with my colleagues.

The document it produced in a few minutes would not only have taken me hours of effort, but was also much sharper and more execution-oriented.

I know what you must be thinking. “So, finally boomer Rohin discovered that AI tools can summarise meetings and generate briefing documents.”

You’re only partially right.

Remember I’d said that I unlocked my first major cybernetic AI feedback loop?

I’d entered the meeting with zero expectations, other than to spit ball a very hazy, very early-stage proto idea. I thought it was a discussion. And right up until I saw Claude’s response to my question, “What do you see?”, that’s what I’d thought we had. A discussion.

Instead, Claude saw me coaching my colleagues via the Socratic method.

The Socratic method is a form of cooperative dialogue whereby participants make assertions about a particular topic, investigate those assertions with questions designed to uncover presuppositions and stimulate critical thinking, and finally come to mutual agreement and understanding about the topic under discussion (though such mutual agreement is not guaranteed or required).

In more formal educational settings, the Socratic method is harnessed by teachers to ‘draw out’ knowledge from students. The teacher does not directly impart knowledge, but asks probing, thought-provoking questions to kickstart a dialogue between teacher and student, allowing students to formulate and justify answers for themselves.

Socratic Method: What Is It and How Can You Use It?

Of course! How could I not have realised this? Over the years I’ve gone through iterations of solving one of the most vexing problems faced by founders or managers: how do you avoid falling into the inevitable trap of becoming the person who has the answers?

In startups, this begins with founders needing to have all the answers, right or wrong, because they are the only ones. Which means they are the ones who develop the most hypotheses and convictions over time, each time strengthened through success or failure. As new colleagues join, they look to founders for answers as they’re finding their own feet. But in many cases, the early “answer vacuum” filled by founders ends up perpetuating over time too. One reason is because founders pay less by way of failed experiments as opposed to others.

Founders, including me, are not blameless either. We pride ourselves on ideas, execution, conviction, brainstorming, experimenting, and problem solving. We think we’re helping our colleagues when we roll up our sleeves and offer help on the above. Instead, we’re merely preventing our colleagues from gaining the necessary learning experience by doing all of that on their own, mistakes and all.

Which is how I ended up with the Socratic method, without ever realising or calling it that. In that method, you ask sharp, simple, and provoking questions that force your colleagues to think and answer in categorical ways. Then, you build upon their answers and proceed to layer on more questions and answers.

What you’re doing is a collective thinking experiment—building contexts, discarding assumptions, imagining situations, and predicting reactions—till you’re left with a clear idea of what the real problem is.

That is exactly how I work.

More important was the fact that Claude saw the discussion as me coaching, even though I myself wasn’t consciously aware of that.

My standard method of working on ambitious ideas is usually this.

View in Browser

Many of the hairiest problems also have the haziest answers, so they remain “stuck” in my head till I have the time to refine them and hand them over to my colleagues.

What I did this week was something new altogether.

I took the germ of an idea, and coached my colleagues on how to think about its problem space and potential solutions. All of us debated and ideated collectively, moving forward in an hour that might have remained stuck with one of us individually for weeks or months.

Then, we used the transcript of the discussion to generate the hypothesis and experiment, perhaps even timelines. And because the entire team was involved, everyone has context on what is proposed.

I was coaching, brainstorming, and rubber-ducking all at the same time!

Why is this a cybernetic feedback loop?

Because I now realise what I was doing, and how I can do it better consciously. Better yet, I can coach and brainstorm and hypothesise as part of the same process. That is better because coaching as a discrete element usually suffers because managers and founders often don’t find the time for it.

Nvidia’s Jensen Huang does something similar too. (The emphasis is mine)

It has also helped him maintain an unconventional management culture—particularly for a company that employs more than 30,000 people. Huang has 60 direct reports and is known, as Haas put it delicately, for “reaching down into different layers of the organization” (or, to put it less delicately, micromanaging). This flat structure can make Nvidia a tough place to work, but Huang sees it as critical to ensuring the organization is strategically aligned and nimble enough to stay at the cutting edge of rapidly evolving chip development and AI progress.

Huang says he is allergic to hierarchy and corporate silos. He doesn’t believe in one-on-one meetings. Instead, he prefers mass gatherings of his leadership team: He says all Nvidia execs should be able to learn from the feedback he provides to any one of them, and they should all benefit from watching him together as he puzzles through a problem.

60 direct reports, but no 1-on-1 meetings: How an unconventional leadership style helped Jensen Huang of Nvidia become one of the most powerful people in business (Fortune), Jeremy Kahn

My new experiment is this: pick interesting problems or bets, then do a “zero-expectation” discussion (important, so you don’t get weighed down by expectations), recorded, transcribed, and reimagined by AI.

That last part is important. Sophisticated AI tools like Claude are able to not just summarise a discussion, but truly zoom out and “see” what really went on.

In my case, something even I did not see.

1. “Being a conduit” (#16, 15 Oct 2023) – The Socratic Method edition reveals that Rohin’s role in that brainstorming meeting wasn’t to generate answers but to channel his colleagues’ thinking through sharp questions. “Being a conduit” explores the same posture from a different angle: leadership as stewardship rather than ownership, acting as a pass-through for others’ interests rather than imposing your own. Both editions argue that the best leaders don’t originate—they transmit, shape, and amplify what’s already there.

2. “Value problems more than solutions” (#25, 24 Dec 2023) – The entire Socratic Method session was an exercise in dwelling in the problem space. Rohin didn’t walk in with a solution; he walked in with a “hazy, early stage proto idea” and spent an hour helping his team understand what the real problem was before anyone reached for answers. This edition makes that philosophy explicit: organizations systematically over-reward solutions and under-reward the patient, upstream work of truly defining problems.

3. “Emergence” (#35, 3 Mar 2024) – The Socratic Method edition describes an outcome that nobody planned: a pre-read document, a coaching session, and a collective hypothesis all emerged from what was supposed to be “just a discussion”. “Emergence” is the theoretical framework for this—when you create the right conditions (peers, passion, play, projects) and relinquish control over predetermined outcomes, unpredictable but valuable things surface organically. The brainstorming session was “emergence” in action.

4. “Select your ecosystem” (#41, 14 Apr 2024) – The Socratic discovery happened because Rohin was in a room with the right colleagues—people who could push back, ideate, and build on each other’s thinking. This edition argues that peer ecosystems are the single most underrated force in development. The quality of the Socratic dialogue depended entirely on who was in the room, not just the method used.

5. “Showing up, asking questions, and getting lucky” (#45, 12 May 2024) – This is the most direct thematic ancestor. It contains the Jensen Huang quote about giving fewer answers and asking more questions, and argues that luck is cultivated through consistent presence and genuine inquiry. The Socratic Method edition is essentially Rohin discovering he’d been doing exactly this—showing up without expectations, asking questions, and stumbling into an insight that Claude then made visible.

6. “Attracting feedback” (#62, 22 Sep 2024) – The cybernetic feedback loop at the heart of the Socratic Method edition is about recognising a signal you were previously blind to. Claude saw coaching where Rohin saw discussion. “Attracting feedback” argues that most feedback problems are actually attention problems—the signals are there, but we’re not present enough to receive them. The AI acted as an attention amplifier, surfacing what was already happening.

7. “Conviction is a muscle” (#63, 29 Sep 2024) – The Socratic Method works precisely because it forces participants to take positions and defend them — to exercise conviction in real time. This edition warns that conviction atrophies when we outsource thinking to external systems and optimization frameworks. The new edition’s insight is that AI should amplify your thinking, not replace it—the same tension explored here between decisive commitment and endless deferral.

8. “Reconciling yourself” (#75, 22 Dec 2024) – The Socratic Method edition is fundamentally a reconciliation moment: Rohin discovers that what he thought he was doing (having a discussion) was actually something different (coaching via Socratic method). The gap between self-perception and reality gets closed. This edition explores that exact practice—examining incongruencies between your actions and your understanding of them, and restoring internal alignment.

9. “Pulling as a group” (#98, 1 Jun 2025) – This is the cautionary counterpart. It confesses that group brainstorming without structure is “entirely useless, if not actually destructive”. The Socratic Method edition shows what happens when group discussion does work — because there was a method (Socratic questioning), a recorder, and an AI to distill the output. Together, the two editions form a before-and-after: unstructured group talk fails; structured Socratic dialogue succeeds.

10. “Yes? Or no?” (#105, 27 Jul 2025) – This edition argues that our tools shape our thinking—the medium affects the quality of the output. The Socratic Method edition demonstrates this vividly: the tool (Claude) didn’t just summarize the meeting, it reframed what had happened entirely. Choosing to feed a raw transcript to AI and ask “What do you see?” was a tool-choice that unlocked a completely different understanding of the session.

11. “Rubber Ducking” (#107, 10 Aug 2025) – The Socratic Method edition explicitly names rubber-ducking as part of what happened in that meeting. This earlier edition provides the concept’s origin: solutions emerge from the process of articulation itself, not from external answers. The new edition extends it—Rohin wasn’t just rubber-ducking for himself, he was facilitating rubber-ducking for his entire team through Socratic questions.

12.  “Attention is all you need” (#109, 24 Aug 2025) – Claude’s response to “What do you see?” was an act of attention that revealed a pattern Rohin couldn’t see himself. This edition argues that attention is the gateway to everything—care, accountability, and moral engagement all begin with genuinely paying attention. The cybernetic feedback loop in “Socratic Method” was triggered by an AI paying closer attention to the conversation than the humans in it.

3. To IMAX or not to IMAX

Hello there!

It’s Vishnu—with the Movies/TV/Music section for this week.

Last Thursday, I watched Project Hail Mary with a couple of my teammates. 

We’ve been holding out for an IMAX screen, but most screens were still packed with the rather popular sequel that-shall-not-be-named. 

So I settled for the next best thing—the Dolby screen at AMB Kapali. (Which, as I found out later, happens to be South India’s first Dolby Cinema screen. Not a bad consolation prize.)

Loved the movie! Probably the best visual experience I’ve had in a theatre in a long time. I just envy people who got to watch it on a 70mm IMAX screen, though:

Credit: IMAX 70mm projectionist @taylor.umphenour.film (Instagram)

(Note: For folks in Bengaluru—IMAX tickets are now open. Don’t let this one slip.)

The very next day, back in the office, I was telling a colleague to catch it on IMAX. And they asked the most reasonable question in the world: “Why IMAX, though? Is it actually worth it?”

I started to answer, and then it went on for a while. So I figured: why not bring this here too?

Because it’s a genuinely good question, and the answer is a bit more interesting than “bigger screen”.

The next time you see IMAX on a movie poster, notice what is written alongside it. It could mean two very different things:

From Posters of 'Dune: Part Two' (2024), 'Michael' (2026) and the trailer for 'Project Hail Mary' (2026)

1. ‘Experience it in IMAX’ – The film was shot on regular cameras, then digitally reprocessed to fill an IMAX screen. Bigger image, better sound. But you’re not seeing anything extra, just a louder, larger version of what everyone else is watching.

2. ‘Shot on/Filmed for IMAX’ – The film was actually shot with IMAX-certified cameras. This is a different beast entirely. These cameras capture a taller frame, so when it plays in an IMAX theatre, the image literally expands—the black bars at the top and bottom of the screen disappear, and you’re seeing parts of the picture that simply don’t exist in the regular cut.

Project Hail Mary is the second kind, btw. It was shot on an ARRI Alexa 65 camera, and nearly two hours of it play in IMAX’s expanded aspect ratio.

You don’t need IMAX to enjoy the movie, though. The story works on any screen. But there are several sequences—I won’t say more—where the camera holds on open space for just a little longer than you expect. On a regular screen, it’s a nice shot. On an IMAX screen, it’s probably the kind of thing that makes you forget you’re sitting in a chair.

I liked it enough in Dolby that I went back for IMAX on Saturday. Worth every rupee.
