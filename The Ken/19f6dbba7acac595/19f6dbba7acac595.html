My views about AI and Indian IT service companies have oscillated as much as Argentina’s fortunes in the World Cup. At first, I was quite sceptical that IT service companies—the likes of TCS, HCLTech, Infosys and Wipro—could take advantage of the AI revolution that’s playing out right now. 

I’ve been quite critical about them, both on Two by Two and on Zero Shot, the other podcast where I cover AI with my wonderful co-hosts Brady and Rohin. 

But of late, I’ve started to slowly, cautiously, start to hope. 

Maybe there’s an opportunity that presents itself here for Indian IT service companies? 

Frankly, I’m not even sure if they did anything to earn this strategic position, but here is the strategy as I see it, which I wrote a couple of weeks ago—I called it the non-aligned layer.

In fact, seen one way, perhaps it’s better that India has no frontier model to sell. Because now it can truly claim neutrality. Of course, it’s a different matter that this emerged out of inaction (which we’ll overlook for now). 

Essentially, the pitch looks something like this for every major enterprise company in the world. “We are the neutral party that makes any model: American frontier, Chinese open-weight, or your own sovereign stack run safely inside your walls, your jurisdiction, and your risk tolerance. We’ll ensure you can keep running when one of them is switched off. Oh, also if you’re not sure about hiring us, we have been doing projects like this for the last 30 years across a range of companies and geographies.”

I mean, that’s a pretty compelling argument. All of these enterprises have to hire someone to make this new world work for them. I’m struggling to think of better alternatives than Indian IT-service companies. Who would they go to?

The non-aligned layer, The Ken

The more I thought about it, the more I started to believe that there are two really important assumptions on which this premise of mine rests. 

The first one is geopolitical. The idea that India’s businesses can claim to be independent, disinterested parties without a dog in the fight is something that it can convince itself about. It remains to be seen whether the rest of the world will take India’s word for it. Plus there are also the complications of the US-China relationship, which involve a combination of chips, technology, AI models, rare-earth metals, and access to the biggest consumer markets in the world. This is what makes this different from IT services, where geopolitics was an afterthought, if at all. 

The second consideration is, well, what China wants from all this. This is a big unknown. The idea that India can play the role of this orchestrator works only if it aligns with China’s vision that it’ll continue to invest and build on creating frontier-level models and giving them away for anyone to use, with little in return. This assumption is going to be severely tested, especially because China has also started to make noises about blocking the export of some of its models, both open and closed. 

And to test both of these big assumptions, I got two wonderful guests on this week’s edition of Two by Two. 

Pranay Kotasthane is the deputy director of the Takshashila Institution, and he chairs its high-tech geopolitical program. He also co-writes Anticipating the Unintended, which is a newsletter on public policy, and co-hosts Puliya Bazi, which is a Hindi-Urdu podcast on politics, policy, and technology. Also, Pranay is uniquely qualified because he came on Two by Two earlier (almost exactly a year ago) for an episode where we discussed why India does not have an open-source AI strategy.

Brady Ng, who is The Ken’s deputy editor and my co-host for Zero Shot. He’s written multiple times about the US-China relationship, specifically with respect to AI technology and how it actually plays out on the ground. I wanted him because he had also reported on AI and China. 

To both of them, I started with my hypothesis, which I described thus: 

India’s AI edge is that it has no AI of its own. It has no frontier model. And everyone looks at India and says that “you have lost the AI race”. In fact, not having a model is the precise qualification for the job, because it’s very hard to be both a competitor and a neutral broker. 

So, India might as well become the neutral broker and make money from it.

Well, let’s just say they both had very different ideas about it.

India was never non-aligned

Even before I’d introduced Brady, Pranay went into a full-on attack mode. I was quite proud of the ‘non-aligned layer’, but well, he had his reservations. 

In his words, India is less non-aligned and more multi-aligned, something that multiple geopolitical experts have already expressed. As Pranay said on the episode, “Whenever people ask which camp is India, India is always in its own camp. A country which is continent-sized will always be in its own camp.”

But Pranay also agreed with me, especially on the commercial side of my argument.

“The important play that India has is that with IT services, you will not be able to capture a lot of value in the model segment because now they’ll be commoditised over time. But the actual challenge will be to orchestrate them to get the results that you want to make your company enterprise AI-enabled, which will take time, effort, and humans, and it’s not just us running plot code and finishing it.

And if that requires you to use Claude, you’ll use Claude. If that requires you to use Deepseek, you’ll use Deepseek. You can integrate anything that you want and you will give them [clients] a value proposition which will be different for them to beat.”

Why China opened its weights

This has been a question on my mind for a long time. One year ago, Pranay and Kailash illuminated me to the answer when they spoke to me about China’s relentless focus on open-source as a strategy—mostly as a response to how they got walled off (and walled themselves off) and had to build as a community to gain results. 

Brady, in this episode, took the opportunity to provide some historical context. He said how China had always had a strong open-source culture, and that open-source was an outlet.

“After the Great Firewall went up, Chinese engineers were kind of trapped within their own corner of the internet. They were cut off from Google, Facebook, and a lot of different platforms that we kind of take for granted everywhere else. And a lot of things had to be built from scratch at home for their own users. And as they were doing this, they also wanted to get some recognition from their counterparts in Silicon Valley or wherever else, really. And the way to do that was to release some of their software through open source.”

Also, Brady makes the point that China had other motivations for releasing the models as open-weight—specifically the desire to be seen and branded as a legitimate and recognised Chinese export.

India and the history with TikTok and Huawei

The way I saw it, I thought that there were three conditions for open-weight models that needed to be true for the non-aligned future to play out: 

They need to continue to be cheap

They need to be available and open.

They need to be capable, at striking distance from frontier models. 

Pranay actually was quite pessimistic about the third one and expected that the gap between open-weight and frontier models would widen, but despite this, he was confident that open-weight models would still be viable because of the threat they pose. He used an analogy to describe this.

“The US had many nuclear weapons, thousands. Russia, thousands of nuclear weapons. But you don’t need thousands of nuclear weapons, right? You have 200, they work, they are going to deter people, right? So in that sense, the open idea is that you need to have a good, reasonable alternative which gives you a backstop against someone denying you things. Will the open-weight models of the future be reasonable backstops? I think yes. Will they be the best? I think not.”

But then I put forward a fourth premise, which I got after speaking to leaders and CXOs in the wake of the Fable ban. For me, the open question wasn’t about capability but separability. Can a company convincingly know that nothing is leaking back to Beijing? I drew the Huawei parallel: the danger was never the handsets but the infrastructure, and one day somebody woke up and decided it leaked. 

And in this case, it’s hard to prove a negative. Even if Deepseek or Beijing were doing nothing, there is no mechanism by which they could demonstrate it.

Brady put the obvious challenge: isn’t the point of open weights that any user can inspect what’s going on?

Pranay separated China’s dumb exports from its smart ones. In his words, steel is steel, but anything digital carries a perception problem, whether or not it’s founded. On the technical question, he sided with Brady that data leakage should be detectable. The larger worry, he argued, is cognitive: nobody knows the training corpus, and if it was largely Chinese, the concern becomes what the model tells you.

I guess this is the worst of both worlds for China—a Huawei-style infrastructure suspicion at one level and a what-is-it-teaching-our-population suspicion at the other. 

No technology has carried both.

Missing five-year plan

The Indian government is currently trying to build capability across the layers—the semiconductor mission, three operational OSAT plants—but none of these are even five-year projects. As Pranay said, “India has a two-year plan and a fifteen-year plan. It does not have a five-year plan.”

Brady made the same point earlier: the gap between the five-year and the fifteen-year plan matters enormously, and a country that cannot articulate the five-year version will not end up owning three or four layers of the stack, let alone five.
