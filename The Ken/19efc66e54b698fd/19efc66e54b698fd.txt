In May, the Reserve Bank of India (RBI) sent https://www.reuters.com/world/india/india-tightens-checks-overseas-flows-currency-pressure-mounts-sources-say-2026-06-03/ query letters to at least 10 family offices and corporates, asking about their overseas investments. 

What had they put money into? 

Were valuations independently assessed? 

Was the purpose a genuine business expansion or personal wealth management?

These do not allege any wrongdoing.

They seem like routine regulatory housekeeping, or another chapter in India’s long-running discomfort https://economictimes.indiatimes.com/news/economy/finance/rbi-tightens-scrutiny-of-overseas-investments-as-outflows-surgeto-27-billion-in-fy26/articleshow/131161162.cms?from=mdr with money leaving the country, possibly following the rupee’s slide. A family office that The Ken spoke to says there’s more to it than meets the eye. 

In 2022, the country overhauled its outbound investment framework under the Foreign Exchange Management (Overseas Investment) Rules, creating automatic approvals through Authorised Dealer (AD) banks so that investors no longer needed to approach the regulator each time they moved money abroad. Gift City was developed as a separate jurisdiction to ease things further. Overseas Direct Investment (ODI) equity flows nearly doubled between FY24 and FY26.

India was ready to let go of that discomfort and was finally trying to make outbound investing easier. 

The recent questions to family offices and scrutiny suggest otherwise. “Family offices which previously operated with relatively limited regulatory engagement are now finding that transactions are being examined with far greater granularity,” says Sonam Chandwani, partner at KS Legal. 

This scrutiny is rooted in definitions that were never set for family offices, and now the RBI is trying to make space for them in its dictionary. 

Grass is greener on the other side

The Indian family office ecosystem is no longer nascent. Between 2018 and 2025, the number of family offices in India grew sevenfold to around 300, with assets under management expanding to an estimated $30–35 billion. 

That growth is reflected in the numbers. RBI’s Fy26 data shows https://economictimes.indiatimes.com/news/economy/finance/rbi-tightens-scrutiny-of-overseas-investments-as-outflows-surgeto-27-billion-in-fy26/articleshow/131161162.cms?from=mdr $27 billion invested abroad during the year, of which 70% was in equity, by family offices and corporates operating through corporate structures. 

The nature of these investments has changed too. It used to be about hedging rupee exposure—parking some wealth in foreign-currency assets as insurance against a weakening rupee. Now it is about accessing opportunities that India does not yet offer.

“Google, Facebook, Netflix… these are companies that came out there,” says Nitesh Aggarwal, CIO of the Baldota Family Office, a 1961-established group with operations spanning mining to aviation. “These represent different trajectories of growth which seem unprecedented.” The family office had originally planned to allocate roughly a third of its Rs 1,000-crore corpus overseas. Indian family offices are increasingly looking at technology trajectories like AI infrastructure and global venture funds that they simply cannot access at home. 

So what might look like opportunities that family offices aren’t able to access,  instead widens the net for questions on capital outflow, whose boundaries the regulator needs to redefine, especially with the exponential growth of family offices in India. 

That is what the 10 letters are trying to do. Scouting out where the boundaries are. 

Screening red signs 

To be clear, the regulator is not acting outside its brief. Its mandate is to ensure that the ODI route is used for genuine business activity and not as a vehicle for personal wealth migration, regulatory arbitrage, or disguised capital flight. 

The structural problem is that family offices don’t fit neatly into any existing category under Indian law. The current framework’s relative newness means there is still limited precedent for how these rules apply to family office structures—leaving investors and their advisors to work through ambiguity that the regulations themselves don’t resolve, says a senior executive at a Mumbai-based wealth management firm, with over a decade in the industry.

They invest as companies, LLPs, trusts, or individuals, each governed by different rules with no consolidated framework built specifically for them. That granularity doesn’t end at the point of investment either. Fema lawyer Arpita Choudhary, who deals with cross-border financial flows, says, “It’s not just entry that has to be a complaint.” 

Deloitte India’s K. R. Sekar and Parthiv Kamdar told The Ken that “genuine business purpose” tests exist in Singapore, the UK, and Australia and are unavoidable when cross-border investments is a sensitive topic, especially in a country with a weak currency. 

But when family offices themselves don’t have a rigid or well-defined framework, grey zones are bound to arise. 

Grey zones

Consider what happened to one family office investment. The Baldota family office mentioned earlier put money into a US-based early-stage startup. The company subsequently wound down. There were no records to show when the RBI came knocking. The family office explained the company had shut down, and there were no books to show. “There is no solution,” says Aggarwal. “We remain stuck.” 

Now, the same family office that planned to deploy a third of its corpus abroad invests only closer to 10–15% annually overseas. 

Despite the overall growth in ODI numbers, such factors impact investors in a peculiar way. 

This loop of scrutiny starts much earlier, at what Choudhary calls “soft scrutiny” by AD banks. Even though automatic approvals speed up remittances on paper, the overseas investment process takes shape only by working with these banks, which now hold a lot more decision-making power after the 2022 overhaul. 

Another common misinterpretation was regulatory permitting overseas financial investments through corporate structures. The regulator, however, later clarified to AD banks that investments can be made only for genuine business expansion. “None of my clients would take an interpretational chance, especially where the RBI is concerned,” Vishesh Narang, an executive at a Delhi-based multi-family office, told The Ken.

The real issue, then, may not be that RBI is trying to block outbound investing. It may be that neither investors nor regulators have fully worked out where the boundaries are, and that a framework built for an earlier era of more modest ambitions is being stress-tested by a new scale of capital. 

India has been clear about wanting ambitious family offices. The liberalised OI regime, Gift City, and the Liberalised Remittance Scheme all point in that direction. But as family office wealth grows and larger pools of private capital look overseas, the oversight challenge grows alongside it. 

The question the May query letters leave open is whether this is a temporary response to currency pressures and global uncertainty or something more structural. 

Are Indian regulators beginning to find that the volume and complexity of private wealth heading abroad is outpacing the frameworks designed to oversee it? And if so, will questions become more frequent, or will another regulation be issued to address that issue? 

For now, neither side has a clear answer.
