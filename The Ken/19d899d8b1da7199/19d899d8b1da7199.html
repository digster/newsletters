A two-day indoor fitness race doesn’t usually scream “romance”—except at Hyrox Bengaluru, held this past weekend on 11 and 12 April. Amid 8,200 participants navigating sled pushes and pulls, burpee broad jumps, and sandbag lunges, alternating with eight kilometres of running, billboards advertised the event’s official dating partner, The League. 

“Wouldn’t it be nice to find a goalmate? Wouldn’t it be nice to stay with the one who doesn’t quit halfway? Wouldn’t it be nice to find someone who keeps going when it gets hard?” ran the messaging for this largely invitation-based dating app for the “ambitious” launched in India in November 2025. 

Creating a dating app profile is usually straightforward—a few flattering photos, witty answers to prompts, a selfie to verify that you’re not looking to phish, and you’re ready to go. 

The League tries to be in one of its own. Once you download the app, you’re asked not to set up a profile but to fill out an application. Instead of a real-time selfie, verification requires either your Linkedin profile or an official email from work or university. And the prompts are long and phrased to make you stop and think.

Once you’re done filling out every field, from what you’re interested in to whether you’re ready for prenups, you wait to be approved, which can take anywhere from a few days to months. Or you pay Rs 5,000 for a month, Rs 11,000 for three months, or Rs 18,000 for six months to fast-track entry into the app.

And this is just the basic subscription tier. Its most exclusive option, Omni League, charges over Rs 2 lakh per month in exchange for “instantly matching you with any person you like”, a dedicated dating coach, full control over age and height preferences, and the option to hide your own age and height, among other features (it’s now a pop-culture refrain how girls value height in men above most else, while men try their best to avoid revealing that they’re shorter than 6 feet). 

The League is owned by US-based Match Group, the parent behind dating apps like Tinder, Hinge, Okcupid, and 42 others. In spite of this, the group has been struggling. In FY25, its revenue remained largely flat year-on-year at $3.5 billion, though its profit jumped 11% to $613 million. 

Its India arm, Mtch India, has fared much worse. Its FY25 revenue has more than halved to Rs 18 crore from Rs 44 crore a year earlier, and so has its profit, touching just around Rs 2 crore. 

A likely reason for this is a growing dating-app fatigue among the younger generation. They’ve seen opportunities for physical meet-ups shrink even as online spaces have mutated to breed low-quality, low-intentioned interactions, fake profiles, harassment, and an algorithm that keeps them forever swiping, never satisfied. Romance, itself, is under attack, as Gen Z wonders if it’s uncool to have a boyfriend (turns out, it is). 

Apps like The League offer a more exclusive proposition. But exclusivity has its own flipside.

Unnatural selection

Twenty-eight-year-old Faridabad-based S first heard of The League from her friend in January. She had been on “the apps” for nearly two years by then and was fast approaching burnout. So when she heard of this new dating app that collated “the hottest singles in the city, [including] founders, actors, and creative powerhouses who know what they want, move with purpose, and refuse to settle”, she was intrigued. 

She signed up using a friend’s referral code and was approved a few days later. 

At 5 p.m. every day, at what the app calls “Happy Hour”, S would receive five prospective profiles. She had a day to “like” them, or they would be replaced with five new ones at the stipulated hour the following day.

Of these, she could only chat with those who liked her back, which further limited the pool to around three people a day. This was a very different proposition from apps like Tinder and Hinge, which offer a near-unlimited pool of prospects. You may tire of swiping, but the app wouldn’t tire of showing you options. 

For a while, it worked for S. “The people were more academically sound,” she said. What she meant was that profiles often specified the universities people had attended and the companies they were currently working at—it was mandatory to mention at least one educational institution and either your current or former role. So instead of a generic profile saying something like “nature lover” and employed at “student”, she’d see profiles like “University of Manchester, Partner at EY”. 

She never encountered anyone who “creeped her out”. That was the benefit of dating within a privileged, previously vetted circle.

Think of any community today, and chances are it has its own dating app. 

In the US, Bikerkiss’ tagline is “two wheels, two hearts, one road”, Farmersonly is for singles in the countryside, and Ugly Bug Ball enables dating for the “aesthetically average”. India has its own equivalents. Polo is for gay men from tier-2 and tier-3 towns, Matchable connects those with special needs, and Gleedeen allows for discreet extramarital dating. 

The extreme specificity of these apps is supposed to be an antidote to free-for-all apps like Tinder, which have been struggling to retain users. 

In the last two years, Tinder’s paying users have fallen from around 10 million in 2023 to 9 million in 2025. Match Group, at large, has seen a similar fall in payers during this period, from around 15 million to 14 million. The silver lining, though, is a marginal jump in revenue per payer. In 2025, for instance, Match Group’s revenue per payer increased 5% year-on-year to $20.09.

The group is increasingly focusing on high-intent users with more disposable income, according to its latest earnings call. And The League fits into that plan.

Primarily active in metropolitan cities in the US, Europe, and Australia, the app’s expansion to India likely comes as a way to target the country’s growing number of high-net-worth individuals (HNIs). The segment is expected to double to over 1.6 million by 2027, of which 20% fall under the age of 40—the kind likely to be on apps like The League. 

For S, though, prospects in her preferred age group of 28–32 gradually tapered, and soon enough, she was being shown people who were either 23 or 37. At some point, her group of friends, all of whom had joined The League together, started getting on a call at 5 p.m. every day just to make sure they weren’t all chatting with the same 28-year-old boy.

“The pool started seeming very, very limited,” she said. 

That’s the quandary of being an “exclusive” app—you have to balance selectivity with a pool large enough to ensure continued engagement.

Networking is the new romance

The League was first launched in 2015 by Amanda Bradford, a Stanford business school alumnus. Her idea was to model it along the lines of Soho House, the members-only social club founded in London. 

It started off with a few thousand members drawn from Bradford’s own network at Salesforce and Google, companies she’d previously worked at. Bradford followed this up with some carefully curated networking across private clubs in the Bay Area, fundraisers for underprivileged kids, and Ivy League alumni holiday parties, leading to 300,000 active users and a 500,000-person waitlist by 2018.

But right from the start, the app was dogged by allegations of elitism. 

Critics accused it of making America’s wealth gap worse. Excessive emphasis on where someone went to university or their professional success meant those with similar credentials clustered together. For instance, around 30% of the app’s users come from Ivy League universities and are twice as likely to match with one another.

When the app was acquired by Match Group in 2022, it ran a “Be a goal digger” campaign that, once again, ushered in similar controversy. 

In a blatantly unequal country like India, where the richest 1% control 40% of the total wealth, apps like these risk entrenching inequality even more. Apart from sponsoring niche events like Hyrox, which charges upwards of Rs 6,000 to even register, The League has also been hosting its own eye-catching events across Mumbai, Delhi, and Goa—from mini-golf and bar crawls to art weekends and skiing. 

Since the app has launched in India, legions of men across Reddit and Instagram have been scrambling, and failing, to get in.

“You have no idea how many people are chasing me for a referral,” said Arnav Goel, a 29-year-old from South Delhi who has started his own tennis academy. 

Goel pays for a subscription to The League and mainly uses the app for networking. 

His bio is clear about his intentions: “Former tennis professional, now coaching and running Thirty40 Tennis Academy’s career consulting across new countries. Built a life around freedom of choice. Turns out that’s what keeps me sane. On here to meet people who are building or thinking about interesting things, open to friendships, collaborations, new fields, and if it naturally ends up being something meaningful long term, I’m not allergic to that either.”

He claims he’s met around 15 people through The League who’ve collaborated with him professionally. 

The bigger picture is less optimistic. Dating apps are going through an existential crisis. Generic apps are deemed not selective enough, and selective apps are struggling with widening their user base. Surveys reveal that some people have started looking for romance elsewhere, including on Linkedin, Duolingo, fitness apps like Strava, and through in-person interactions. 

Unless dating apps act fast, they may lose the monopoly on love.
