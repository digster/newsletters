Good Morning Ishan,
A few months ago, a space startup in India was preparing to launch a satellite when it learned that the satellite and the rocket’s deployer—the mechanism that carries the satellite up and releases it into orbit—were incompatible.
The mismatch meant the satellite’s kill-switch mechanism could not work as intended. The switch was supposed to keep the satellite’s battery inactive during launch and turn it on only after separation from the rocket, once the satellite reached orbit. The startup also had not been properly informed about the deployer’s dimensions and technical specifications.
The launch was postponed, according to an industry executive and a consultant who worked with the startup. The technical problem was fixable. But there was another issue.
Even before the startup went to the US for launch, the cover available for insurance was too expensive. Its satellite had cost around Rs 35 crore to build; two insurance brokers quoted Rs 4.2–5.25 crore to insure it. That’s 12–15% of the satellite’s value.
For insurers, the problem is easy to understand. “It is highly capital-intensive and highly risky,” said a former executive at the insurance provider Tata AIG. A single mission can carry an enormous ticket size, meaning an insurer needs enough capital on its books to absorb a potentially catastrophic loss.
For a young space company, the problem runs the other way. The newer the rocket or satellite, the less flight history insurers have to work with. Less data makes the risk harder to price, which can push premiums higher.
This puts companies such as Skyroot Aerospace, Pixxel, Agnikul Cosmos, Digantara, Dhruva Space, and Galaxeye—which have raised just about Rs 530 crore so far—on one side of a market still finding its feet. On the other are a handful of insurers, including New India Assurance and Tata AIG, reinsurer GIC Re, and the specialist capacity that Indian brokers tap in London through Lloyd’s and international reinsurers.
