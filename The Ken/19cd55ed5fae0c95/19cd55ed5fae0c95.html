India is as irresistible as it is unforgiving for global consumer brands. 

It is the world’s most populous country. And the fifth-largest economy (even if the government claims it is fourth). Every second Indian is under 30, and two in three are connected to the internet. 

What’s equally true is that the average income in India is lower than in El Salvador and Lebanon. Millions of graduates are unemployable, and delivering food or groceries is the best many of them can hope for. 

Starbucks is one of those brands caught between these realities. Debuting in India in 2012 through a joint venture with Tata Consumer Products, it is now the country’s largest coffee chain, both by revenue and footprint. 

But making money is still very much a fantasy for the coffee major. 

The business, already India’s largest coffee chain by store count, will prioritize expansion over profitability as investment accelerates, Tata Starbucks Pvt.’s Chief Executive Officer Sushant Dash said in an interview. 

“India remains a focus area as much if not more,” Dash said, noting the market ranks among Starbucks’ top five globally. The unit has turned cash positive, he added, while declining to give a timeline for profits. 

Starbucks Boosts Investments in ‘Focus’ Market India Undeterred by Mounting Losses, Bloomberg

In fact, the joint venture has never turned a profit, except in FY19, when a tax refund helped it stay in the black. In FY25, its revenue was up 5% to Rs 1,300 crore, but its losses surged 70% to over Rs 135 crore, its highest ever. 

In January, Starbucks launched “protein cold foam” drinks in partnership with Superyou*, a food brand owned by actor Ranveer Singh. The chain is also eager to adapt more closely to Indian palates, as a Bloomberg story pointed out.

Taking a leaf out of its global playbook — it offered egg rolls in Singapore for Lunar New Year — Tata Starbucks is localizing menus with regional flavors. It is selling Malabar egg roast and keema variants in southern India, mutton dish Kosha Mangsho-inspired wrap in Kolkata and Vada Pao-style buns in Mumbai.

You’re also likely to find “pav bhaji pull apart” and “curried chicken bagel” on its menu, depending on where you are. 

Starbucks is already priced a notch higher than homegrown rivals such as Blue Tokai Coffee Roasters and Third Wave Coffee, but it still needs to find ways to persuade customers to spend more, and for two reasons. 

Anyone familiar with cafés in Mumbai knows that Starbucks has the most spacious outlets in the most sought-after locations, including airports and high-income neighbourhoods. The city, which has the country’s priciest real-estate market, is home to at least one in every five Starbucks outlets in India. 

This has to be seen alongside the chain’s desperation to squeeze more out of its stores. Its revenue per café has largely been flat over the years.

The fact that its per-store sales are already more than twice as much as that of Blue Tokai and Third Wave means that they couldn’t possibly rise further, or at least not by much. These chains don’t make money either, but unlike Starbucks, they trimmed their losses in FY25. 

Two years ago, Starbucks introduced smaller, cheaper cups of its hot beverages to draw price-conscious consumers. But no amount of menu tweaking is enough to make up for the slog that is selling Rs 300–400 coffees at scale, and profitably. Never mind all the chatter around the rise of India’s “affluent class”. 

In November, Starbucks sold a majority stake in its China unit to a private-equity firm. Even though Starbucks is credited with popularising cafés in the country, where it set up shop in 1999, it has in recent years struggled with the impact of the pandemic, an economic slowdown, and cheaper, nimbler rivals like Luckin Coffee. 

The sale is part of chief executive Brian Niccol’s efforts to return the coffee giant to its glory days, in part by closing hundreds of unprofitable stores in the US and making the in-store experience “warmer” for customers. 

On a recent earnings call, Sunil D’Souza, Tata Consumer’s CEO, said the company is “immensely focused on making sure we constantly tweak our business model to make sure it appeals to Indian consumers and continue to drive business.” 

All the tweaking so far has yielded little. But such is the power of the “India is irresistible” narrative that it sometimes obscures a company’s own cold, hard numbers.
