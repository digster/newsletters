Good Morning Ishan,
When an AI company goes shopping for a GPU cloud, there are two questions it asks. “Can you get me the chips right now?” and “Will they still be working tomorrow?”
Price, surprisingly, is the third consideration. Orchestration software—the layer deciding– falls even further down the list.
“If tomorrow our product team says, ‘We just signed a telecom customer, and we need another 256 H100s by Monday’,” said an ML engineer at the voice-AI startup, Gnani, “I honestly don’t care if somebody else is 20% cheaper six weeks from now.”
That’s why Gnani pays three GPU cloud providers at once, in any given week, for three unrelated reasons.
…
For Gnani, production traffic—the voice agents actually talking to customers—stays with Yotta, a major GPU cloud and data-centre provider.
On the other hand, a job like retraining a model on fresh data to improve response time might need eight GPUs sitting next to each other on a fast interconnect for three uninterrupted days. That job goes wherever that capacity happens to be free that week—maybe Neysa.
An engineer’s afternoon experiment, like testing a new accent, lands on E2E Networks, India’s first listed cloud provider, spun up through a self-serve portal in minutes, with no procurement forms or sales calls.
Multiply that across every bank, hospital, e-commerce platform, and AI startup in India, and that’s the market: three companies—Yotta, Neysa, and E2E—competing to be the one holding the chips when the phone rings.
All three, though, buy their chips from the same company: Nvidia. But the latter has stopped being just the seller.
