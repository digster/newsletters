Good Morning Ishan,
In February 44 BC, during the last days of the Roman Republic, Mark Antony offered Julius Caesar a crown in front of a Roman crowd, and Caesar pushed it away.
Antony offered it again; Caesar refused again.
Undeterred, Antony offered it a third time. For the third time, Caesar refused and instead asked that the crown be sent to a temple because, in his words, “only gods can wear crowns”.
The crowd cheered approvingly. Later historians have suspected that the whole exchange was a charade staged by Caesar and Antony to gauge the mood of the Roman people and see whether they would accept Rome having a king. As it turns out, they would not.
But back to Uber.
Uber has now been offered a way out of India three times in eight years — by Softbank and Ola in 2018, through merger-and-acquisition talks with Ola in 2022, and by Rapido this May.
And three times, it has refused.
So what does Uber want from India?
In today’s episode of Two by Two, I try to answer that question by tracing Uber’s past and present, how it changed as business models changed, and ultimately, what its doctrine with respect to India is. Eventually, I do, and it consists of five line items—these are the terms Uber has always used to decide whether it’s worth staying in India.
Here they are:
The first term is opacity: India matters enormously, yet nobody gets to see what it costs. Uber has been public for a while now, but it has never officially reported what India costs to run or how much revenue it generates there. It prefers to describe India in terms of gross bookings, or other vanity metrics. Never in terms of revenue or cost. In fact, just last year, in an [episode](https://the-ken.com/podcasts/two-by-two/uber-india-has-run-the-race-and-stayed-the-course-can-it-change-the-game/) of Two by Two with Prabhjeet Singh, Uber India’s former head, he stuck to the same message when I asked him about Uber’s India numbers.
We are a very disciplined company. Remember, as a public company, even though we don’t kind of disclose India numbers separately, the rigour with which we run the business is as much as of a public company… But right now, India is such an important market; you’ll continue to see us invest for a very long period of time.
The second is control: Uber will not be a minority in someone else’s company, because it has watched what those stakes are worth. Historically, it has exited markets and taken minority positions in companies like Didi in China, Grab in Southeast Asia, and Yandex in Russia.
Let’s just say those bets haven’t really worked out, not financially, not strategically.
So why should Uber make the same mistake again?
The third is ranking: Uber will only be first or second, and it measures the market by value rather than volume.
Again, here’s Prabhjeet Singh: “You can get a volume of trips at 80 cents on the dollar with tons of two-wheeler trips, or you can actually get revenue from a much larger cars business… We are clearly the market leader. We are not the market leader on a two-wheeler-based business. Other players built that business and have also taken a lot more regulatory risk.”
The fourth is the level playing field: Uber will compete on a model it considers inferior only for as long as everyone is taxed the same.
This is where Rapido comes in, with its subscription model. Once Rapido made clear that it would forego commissions to chase growth through rider subscriptions, Uber was forced to respond in kind and change its own structure. The fact that Uber realigned is a testament to the company’s flexibility and willingness to give up older business models for newer ones, as long as others are doing the same.
In an interview with Nikhil Kamath on his podcast, Uber CEO Dara Khosrowshahi had this to say:
Rapido’s not making money, so the real test of the business isn’t how fast you can grow if you’re spending. It’s actually how fast you can grow while you’re profitable. And I think Rapido’s a long way away from that. But they’ve been innovative. They’ve been scrappy. They move fast… We’ve got a lot of respect for them. And we intend to compete really hard with them.
And the fifth sits underneath the other four, and is the one Uber has never bent:
India is core, so Uber will not leave.
But what does core mean? And what’s Uber’s calculations as it turns down acquisition offers one after another?
That’s the episode.
