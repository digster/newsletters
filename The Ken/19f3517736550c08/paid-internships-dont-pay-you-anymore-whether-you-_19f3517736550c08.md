Husna, a 20-year-old undergraduate, completed her fourth semester in May 2026. She then had to find a 15-day internship, not because she wanted to, but because it’s a mandatory component of Kerala’s Four-Year Undergraduate Program (FYUGP).
Of all the things that Kerala is known for, industrial base and business concentration aren’t high on the list. Left with few other options and time running out before the next semester, she searched internship portals and asked her seniors for references. In the end, she registered for a “paid internship” that cost her Rs 2,500. But her expenses did not end there.
With limited opportunities in her home district of Malappuram, she interned in Kozhikode, about 50 kilometres away, making commuting out of the question. She spent another Rs 3,500 on accommodation for that duration. She spent around Rs 6,000 in total for a grand total of two credits. Almost half as much, or more than what most students spend for one semester.
But Kerala’s push for internships follows a national pattern. India has been on a mission to aggressively upskill its human capital, and internships are a key piece of the skilling puzzle. The National Education Policy’s (NEP) FYUGP mandates internships for undergrads, which is what left Husna with no choice but to pay.
This predicament is not exclusive to Husna. In many ways, she fared better than her peers caught in the same bind. “There was some hands-on experience that you don’t get in college,” she says.
And every summer, the cycle repeats when students like her go on an internship hunt only to end up with “paid internships” (sometimes costing as high as Rs 10,000), often with minimal practical utility or industry experience.
Long story short, this is a result of a government mandate that demands a supply of internships which doesn’t exist, and certified course providers jumping in to fill the gap.
