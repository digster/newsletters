What India is doing, will do, and should do—to not just survive but thrive in the chaos unleashed by Trump.

When I began consulting for IIIT-Dharwad, I expected to see anxious students. Technical campuses are built on deadlines, grades, rankings, and interviews. Anxiety is almost part of the weather.
What I didn’t expect was the time it took for those anxious students to reach my office. And when they finally arrived, the problem had usually stopped being a problem. It had become a crisis.
That referral would often come wrapped in administrative language. The same language was also used in last month’s report presented by a fact-finding committee on the two deaths at IIT Kharagpur, as [reported](https://www.telegraphindia.com/west-bengal/kolkata/academic-stress-behind-iit-kharagpur-student-death-report-prnt/cid/2159387#goog_rewarded) by The Telegraph. The document described students as facing “academic issues” and noted that it was “difficult” for them to handle pressure, and hence recommended “enhanced security” as a preventive measure.
Such language helps cover mismatched expectations.
Faculty and parents spend years telling students that entry into an elite technical institution will solve life’s problems. When the student arrives on campus, she realises that admission was merely one round in a series of many. This nuance is often missed in public conversations about student suicides in IITs, IIITs, and other elite technical campuses.
To be fair, that language isn’t wrong, but it isn’t complex enough to capture the institutional ways that “academic issues” snowball. Grading systems turn small setbacks into identity crises. Attendance rules that punish withdrawal before anyone knows why she withdrew. Placement calendars that compress self-worth into a few brutal weeks. Peer cultures where everyone appears to be ‘managing’, even when many are not. Families who have sacrificed too much to allow the student to admit she is struggling. Counselling systems that exist but are limited only to reported issues or as a last resort.
All these problems are not new. Student suicides in elite institutions is also not new. All the more reason that the system risks becoming a broken one that cannot be fixed. That day hasn’t come yet.
Last week, the Central Information Commission [asked](https://www.deccanherald.com/india/dire-need-for-corrective-measures-cic-flags-persistent-crisis-of-suicides-in-iit-campuses-suggests-high-level-panels-4017005) IITs to form high-level committees to address student suicides. This was against the backdrop of the National Crime Records Bureau (NCRB) recently releasing a report that shows disturbing numbers, and IITs refusing to disclose information about the deceased.
The NRCB report says that 14,488 students died by suicide in 2024, as opposed to 13,892 in 2023. That 4.3% rise also corroborates the reported increase in deaths in elite institutions.
Within IITs, at least 65 students have [reportedly](https://timesofindia.indiatimes.com/india/65-student-suicides-at-iits-in-5-yrs-alumni-group-calls-for-accountability/articleshow/127054283.cms) died by suicide between 2021 and 2025. And half as many have already been recorded in the past two years.
These numbers tell you that students are dying, but they do not tell us how an institution slowly loses sight of a student before the crisis becomes visible.
And that story goes beyond numbers and reports.
