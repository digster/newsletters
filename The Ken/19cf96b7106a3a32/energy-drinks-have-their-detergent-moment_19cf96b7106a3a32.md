Tue, 17 Mar 26
A weekly newsletter about the biggest changes in commerce—focusing on shifts that matter to you.
Good Morning Ishan,
Monday blues hit harder when your weekend was unforgettable. That’s what Priyal, my colleague in our Delhi office, was telling me as she scrolled through videos and photos from the concert she attended in Chandigarh.
She was one of the 42,000 fans who turned up to see Punjabi popstar Karan Aujla. But now, she was back in Delhi, reporting on semiconductors and finishing her draft.
Aujla’s hold over young India is hard to ignore. Across Instagram, Youtube, and Spotify, he has built a following of over 36 million. Scroll through social media and you’ll likely find 20- and 30-somethings dancing to his tracks like “Mexico” or “For a Reason” at his Delhi concert, which kicked off his India tour on 28 February.
That show alone drew more than 72,000 people, making it [reportedly](https://dailypioneer.com/news/karan-aujlas-show-indias-2nd-biggest-concert) the second highest attended single-day concert in the country, behind only Coldplay’s January 2025 performance.
And here’s something else that Priyal told me that I found even more interesting. How Aujla is turning his crazy following into revenue. Beyond ticket sales, he used the tour to launch his own energy and sports drink, Zyro. Strategically placed on stage and with stalls spread across the venue, the brand was impossible to miss.
At the Delhi concert, a [clip](https://www.instagram.com/reel/DVVDI4IExa7/?igsh=dTRvY21qOHF5ZzNw) captures Aujla taking a direct swipe at Coke. “Whenever you are thirsty,” he tells the crowd in Punjabi, “don’t drink Coke, have Zyro, alright?” The audience roars back: “We will only drink Zyro from now on.”
Aujla co-founded this brand with Viraj Bahl, the founder of Veeba Foods, known for its sauces and condiments.
The logic of leaning on Aujla’s star power is straightforward. In recent years, celebrity-backed launches have given several young consumer brands the initial life they needed, especially when marketing budgets are tight and legacy players dominate the shelf.
In a recent Two by Two [episode](https://the-ken.com/newsletters/two-by-two/did-gen-z-hand-consumer-brands-a-blueprint-to-beat-the-giants/), Adarsh Menon, who is a partner at Fireside Ventures said, “Distribution excellence has shifted from retail presence and availability to algorithm availability, to feed availability.” And collaborating with celebrities is one way to crack that.
Take Diljit Dosanjh (Punjabi popstar) and Mokobara. At one of his concerts, an [image](https://www.instagram.com/p/C6vmoHXNSaQ/) of the singer with his foot resting on a Mokobara trolley became instantly iconic, giving the D2C travel accessories brand a visibility boost in a category long ruled by VIP Industries.
Or consider Ranveer Singh, co-founder and face of protein brand Superyou. Within a year of launch, the brand hit an annual recurring revenue of [Rs 150 crore](https://retail.economictimes.indiatimes.com/news/food-entertainment/personal-care-pet-supplies-liquor/superyou-records-rs-150-crore-arr-in-first-year-functional-snacking-gains-traction/125381844).
For Zyro, the stakes are similar. It’s entering a category stacked with heavy hitters—Pepsico’s Sting, Red Bull, and the rest. Against that kind of competition, a moment like that on stage might be just what it needs to squeeze in.
Zyro is as much about Karan Aujla as it is about food entrepreneur Viraj Bahl. His company Veeba, already a profitable food business with a top line exceeding Rs 1,000 crore, is now making its first move into beverages.
“With Zyro, we step into beverages for the first time,” Bahl wrote in a Linkedin [post](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/virajbahl_about-a-decade-ago-we-set-out-to-build-a-activity-7433716153530667008-ZSgj/). “This move is not about expansion for the sake of it. It is about building a complete food and beverage company with intent. Beverages were always going to be part of that story. The only question was when and how.”
Food categories like sauces and condiments typically operate at gross margins of 30–35%. Beverages, especially energy drinks, can be as high as 75–80%. If Zyro gains traction, it could significantly boost both Veeba’s top line and bottom line. For context, Veeba’s Ebitda margin stood at 1.5% in FY25, per Tofler data. Compare that with Varun Beverages, which posted margins of around 24% in the same period. And the industry incumbent owes a significant portion of that to the sale of carbonated soft drinks, which also includes their energy drinks.

Bahl is entering the category at a moment of transition. Until now, energy drinks in India have grown through a “value over volume” model, largely driven by low-cost options like Pepsico’s Sting, which did wonders for the latter in terms of numbers as we [reported](https://the-ken.com/story/red-bull-is-the-energy-drink-of-the-world-how-did-pepsico-make-sting-the-energy-drink-of-india/) previously.
Earlier in May, when Varun Beverages, with a US$23.4 billion market cap, released its results for the year ended March 2024, Sting once again found its place among the top contributors. It helped the company’s revenue and profits rise by about 22% and 35%, respectively.
But the market is shifting. As one investor in Varun Beverages put it, energy drinks are having their “detergent moment”, just as consumers [upgraded](https://the-ken.com/tradetricks/hindustan-unilevers-detergent-powered-decade/) from powder to liquid detergent, they are now moving from Rs 20 PET bottles to a Rs 50–70 cans.
Health is part of that story. More consumers are reaching for drinks with less or no sugar. Varun Beverages’ 2025 annual report notes that 46% of its India sales mix now consists of low- sugar or no-sugar drinks. (Although, it counts drinks like Sting and Mirinda in this category as well.) Multiple [studies](https://www.ipsos.com/en-in/rise-conscious-eating) done in recent years also indicate that a growing number of Indians are trying to ‘eat and drink healthy’. So Zyro’s “no sugar” positioning makes sense.
On pricing, Zyro sits somewhere in between. Its energy drink is priced at Rs 90 for a 250-ml can, below premium players like Red Bull and Monster Energy, whose no-sugar variants range from Rs 125 to Rs 250 for similar volumes. Its sports drink, at Rs 60 for a 400-ml bottle, is priced slightly above Pepsico’s Gatorade (Rs 50 for 500 ml).
If you flip the bottle, you can see that the carbonated energy drink is produced by Axiom Food and Beverages in Himachal Pradesh, while the non-carbonated sports drink comes from Mahodar Beverages. Both are marketed under Bahl’s Veeba Foods.
For a challenger consumer brand, beverage manufacturing is capital-intensive, especially energy drinks, which require specialised cooling and licensing. The launch may have star power behind it, but the rollout itself is measured.
What Bahl and Aujla pull off with Zyro will only become clear in the months ahead. Globally, the energy drink category has seen its share of [turbulence](https://fortune.com/2024/04/27/logan-paul-prime-energy-drink-ksi-gen-alpha-rise-fall-sales-lawsuits/), remember Prime, the drink from Youtubers-turned-entrepreneurs Logan Paul and KSI?
For now, though Zyro has done what matters most at the start: brand visibility.
