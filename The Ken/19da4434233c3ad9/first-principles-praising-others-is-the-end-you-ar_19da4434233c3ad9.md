If ever there were awards for the thing that makes most people extremely uncomfortable doing, regardless of their hierarchy, gender, age or even geography, what do you think the winner might be?
I think the clear winner would be complimenting or praising others. (If you feel you had a better candidate, email me with your recommendation and reasoning.)
First, praising others is [excruciatingly hard](https://www.dumblittleman.com/giving-compliments/) for most of us in normal life. At the workplace, it’s [even harder](https://zengerfolkman.com/articles/why-is-it-so-difficult-for-leaders-to-give-positive-feedback/). It’s so bad that managers and leaders think that critical feedback to others is what makes them better, whereas everyone else around them thinks it’s [their praise](https://hbr.org/2017/05/why-do-so-many-managers-avoid-giving-praise) that makes them better!
It gets worse because receiving praise is even harder for most of us.
If recognition sometimes makes you uncomfortable, you aren’t alone. Many of us can’t take a compliment, and our responses are often as awkward as the examples above. In a study of more than 400 people that I conducted in Boston a few years ago, nearly 70% of people associated feelings of embarrassment or discomfort with recognition or receiving a compliment.
…
After the initial surprise, we start to look for answers. We may try to understand “why” someone said what they did, and it can be confusing to reconcile if someone else’s positive view conflicts with our own (negative) view of ourselves. According to Luna and Renninger, this is confirmation bias: a tendency to seek information that confirms our views and ignore views that challenge them. So, when someone congratulates you on a great presentation that you think you bombed, it can feel jarring.
The interplay of surprise and self-image can make it harder to process the nice things we hear about ourselves. “People may divert praise as a way of protecting from future failure, disappointment, or rejection from others,” Denise Marigold, associate professor of social development at the University of Waterloo, Canada, told me. “The fear is that if I allow myself to let in a compliment, and feel good about it, and end up disappointing others or myself in the future, I risk taking a bigger bite out of my self-esteem.”
All this to say, many of us respond awkwardly to compliments as an unconscious act of self-protection. Unfortunately, this unconscious self-protection often robs us of human connection. It keeps us from letting in the kind words and gratitude of others.
“[Do Compliments Make You Cringe? Here’s Why](https://hbr.org/2021/04/do-compliments-make-you-cringe-heres-why)” by Christopher Littlefield, Harvard Business Review
Receiving compliments is also [a social minefield](https://www.bbc.com/future/article/20240403-why-its-so-hard-for-some-people-to-accept-compliments), once you take into account factors like gender, race, ethnicity, etc.
Finally, don’t even get me started on asking for praise.
Who does that, you ask? Hmmm, let’s think. Are you implying that the only reason to ask someone for feedback about yourself or something that you’ve done is to get the critical variety? Never the appreciative type?
Let me guess. Do you also feel annual appraisals must always be about identifying and fixing your weaknesses, never [your strengths](https://the-ken.com/newsletter/first-principles/dont-compete/)? And that good feedback is always about what you could have done better, never what you did really well?
You see where I am going with this, right?
And yet, great compliments are one of the best things in life and at work.
“The happy phrasing of a compliment is one of the rarest of human gifts, and the happy delivery of it another.” – Mark Twain
“In two independently conducted lines of research, we asked participants to estimate how another person would feel after receiving a compliment. We then asked those same participants to actually compliment another person, and we compared how that person actually felt after receiving the compliment to how participants imagined that person would feel. Using the same basic experimental paradigm both with friends and strangers, we consistently found that people underestimated how good their compliment would make the recipient feel. Compliment-givers tend to believe the other person won’t enjoy their interaction as much as they actually do; in fact, they often believe that their exchange will probably make the person a little uncomfortable. Yet, consistently, receiving a compliment brightens people’s day much more than anticipated, leaving them feeling better, and less uncomfortable, than givers expect.
From the outside, it seems obvious that receiving a compliment would make someone feel better. Who doesn’t like when someone praises their way of handling a tense situation at work, their choice of attire, or their presentation skills? Indeed, when asked, nearly 90% of people believe that they should compliment each other more often. And yet we tend not to give them in practice. In fact, only 50% of people in one experiment who wrote down a compliment for a friend actually sent the compliment along when given the chance, even though they’d already done the hardest part — coming up with something nice and thoughtful to say. That is, despite the widely shared desire to give more compliments, when faced with the decision people still often forgo low-cost opportunities to make others feel appreciated and valued.
These experiments highlight a critical psychological barrier to creating more positive organizational cultures: your mistaken expectations. When it comes to deciding whether to express praise or appreciation to another person, doubt creeps in. We find that people are overly concerned about their ability to convey praise skillfully (“What if my delivery is awkward?”), and their anxiety leaves them overly pessimistic about the effects their messages will have. Sadly, people’s pessimism causes them to refrain from engaging in this behavior that would make everyone better off.”
“[A Simple Compliment Can Make a Big Difference](https://hbr.org/2021/02/a-simple-compliment-can-make-a-big-difference)”, by Erica Boothby, Xuan Zhao and Vanessa Bohns, Harvard Business Review
It’s incredible how hard it is to do something that costs us virtually nothing, even if we do it many times a day.
An “aha!” moment for me, personally, was when I realised that praising people well isn’t just about them, but about me too.
Selfish as that may sound, it’s true. I see a good compliment as the outcome of a good process. While the good compliment may be for a friend or colleague, the good process that leads up to it benefits me.
To understand that, let’s see what experts agree constitutes good praise.
Authenticity. Specificity. Timeliness. Warmth. Articulation.
For a compliment to be all of that, what do I need to be?
I need to overcome my awkwardness about praising others. I need to be observant to notice and compliment the details. I need to spot good things around me, in friends or colleagues, as they happen, so I can praise them for it. I need to know how to structure my praise in a way that feels warm and deliberate.
Yes, these are the reasons that also hold us back from praising others. Why take on these onerous self-development tasks when most people think compliments are “cringe”?
Why indeed.
Because the composition and delivery of timely and authentic compliments is the exercise I use to build those muscles in myself.
I want to be a person who is enthusiastic, generous, authentic, and articulate with their praise.
I think we get too caught up when it comes to praising others, thinking about how they’ll take it. Instead, my personal philosophy is to expect nothing in return. I’m praising them because I want to. Because of [how I felt](https://hbr.org/2021/04/do-compliments-make-you-cringe-heres-why).
Know that the compliment is about the giver, not you (the receiver)
When someone recognizes you, they share the experience of what you did and how it impacted them. You may have thrown that report together at the last minute, missed a key section of your presentation, or overcooked the risotto. But when someone says they loved it, understand that it is their experience, not yours. They are telling you how it made them feel.

Yup, I went and found some interesting album art, and the trivia-shaped rabbit holes behind each of them (some of which, you might already know, or maybe not):
1. “Viva la Vida” by Coldplay (2008)
The painting on the cover is called ‘[Liberty Leading the People](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberty_Leading_the_People)’ (La Liberté guidant le peuple), by French artist Eugène Delacroix.
Delacroix painted it after personally witnessing the July Revolution in Paris, and when the French government caught wind of it, it bought the painting and then quietly hid it for years. It now lives in the Louvre.
Here’s one more trivia:
Coldplay’s leading man, Chris Martin, while at the Frida Kahlo Museum in Mexico, saw the legendary painter’s last piece:
| "Viva la Vida, Watermelons", Oil on masonite, by Frida Kahlo (1954) |
He noticed the words “Viva La Vida” on one of the watermelons, thought it was interesting, and added it to his list of potential title names on his phone. Months later, he comes back to it and writes the song that we now know (and my favourite track of theirs!)
Chris talked about this on an [episode](https://youtu.be/vUnWFsc5WBU?si=-4s2rwnlY9fLuHxQ) of The Howard Stern Show and then also gave an amazing acoustic rendition of the song. You should check it out!
2. “Rage Against the Machine’” by Rage Against the Machine (1992)
Anyone who’s heard a RATM track would know what their politics are and what they stand for.
All the songs in their debut self-titled album, which came out in 1992, have strong political messages. When it came time to select the album cover, they wanted something that “captured the integrity and the power that we were striving for in our songs”, according to their guitarist Tom Morello.
They turned to the Pulitzer-winning photograph by Malcolm Browne, of Thích Quảng Đức, a Vietnamese Buddhist monk who set himself on fire in 1963 to protest persecution during the [Buddhist crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhist_crisis).
The image and the continued protests shook things enough that the government’s own generals turned against them, and the regime was overthrown in a US-backed coup later that year.
3. “Abbey Road” by The Beatles (1969)
Did you know that this iconic and probably the most re-created group photo of all time was originally supposed to be shot in the Himalayas!?
The working title of the album was “Everest”, after their producer, Geoff Emerick’s favourite cigarette brand. But none of the Beatles wanted to fly to the Himalayas, and ended up shooting it on Abbey Road.
The photo was taken by Iain Macmillan, John Lennon’s friend.
From what I read, a policeman held up the traffic as Macmillan, from a stepladder positioned in the middle of the road, took six shots as the group walked across the zebra crossing just outside the studio.
It was apparently over in 10 minutes.
Oh, by the way, there’s a permanent live camera feed to the zebra crossing at Abbey Road. You can watch it [here](https://www.earthcam.com/world/england/london/abbeyroad/?cam=abbeyroad_uk)!
