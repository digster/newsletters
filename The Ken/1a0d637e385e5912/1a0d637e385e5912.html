A few months ago, I asked subscribers to tell me about the most annoying, frustrating, or repetitive things in their lives they wish a personal assistant would take away. Here are some of the most interesting answers we got: 

“I sold a vehicle, and getting the ownership transferred was a difficult process because my aadhar address had changed to a different state, this apparently is a big issue with the government & RTO, and an ‘agent’ of a different kind needed to be hired.” — Respondent #412, 18–24, Gurugram

“Reading a 300 message group chat and deciding which of those messages would require my response and what was just two people discussing something which does not require my input” — Respondent #67, 18–24, Mumbai

“With my Golden Retriever, Winnie, one of the recurring hassles is managing her walks and daily routine. There are days when everyone at home assumes someone else has taken her out, and then we realise she has been waiting for her walk. On other days, we have to coordinate who will feed her, take her out, or manage her grooming and other needs. It sounds simple, but keeping track of all these small things around everyone’s schedules can become surprisingly chaotic.” — Respondent #56, age and location undisclosed

August has been an eventful month when it comes to personal assistants. People have been obsessed with Instinct, an app you can text on Whatsapp that can go and do things like cancel subscriptions, book a trip, buy the groceries. Investors valued it at around $2.5 billion within weeks. Last week, Meta followed with Muse, which is taking over the US right now. And now, depending on where you look, people are going around trying to find all kinds of reasons and ways to build agents to solve things from booking tickets, saving money on subscriptions, and much more. 

When we asked subscribers what they wanted digital assistants to solve, we discovered that they cluster around sixteen spaces, all of which are uniquely Indian problems.

You can play with what subscribers said, and how these problems can be solved on our website here. As I’m writing this, over 6,000 student teams have come together to handle some of these problems and build AI agents to address them. 

In today’s episode though, I ask a different question—if personal assistants are the rage, how come India isn’t seeing the kind of adoption you’d expect? After all, India has UPI and Whatsapp on every phone, and a culture of delegation from drivers and cooks to RTO agents, CAs and the human “agent” who knows someone at the passport office. 

And yet personal AI agents haven’t taken off here.

To understand why, I brought two returning guests to the show. 

Shreyas Srinivasan, co-founder of Alt Inc, which is building a social layer for AI so people can find others like them through how they use it. He’s the founder of Insider, which Paytm acquired and later sold to Zomato, and former chief product officer at Paytm.

Dharmesh Ba, founder of 1990 Research Labs, a consumer-intelligence lab that helps companies with engagement and retention problems through primary research. He previously built Business Hero, a Whatsapp automation tool for small businesses. He also writes the popular Substack titled The India Notes on Indian consumer psychology. 

In the course of the conversation, I discovered both of them are power users of AI assistants. Shreyas runs Openclaw, Hermes, and Instinct and says he’s “dated all of them”, yet struggles to find uses for them. Dharmesh uses his in-between therapy sessions and to mine eight years of Indian consumer interviews. 

When I brought up some of the answers and problems that subscribers stated in the survey, Shreyas argued that most of these are solvable today on a general-purpose model, that power law will leave three or four general agents standing, and that UPI gives India no real edge over credit cards. 

Dharmesh, on the other hand, argued that in India trust sits with people, like a hardware-store owner in Erode who takes ten calls an hour, and that specialists will win wherever social standing is at stake.

In the end, we also discuss the companies themselves—if an agent has no attention to capture and no friction to charge for, what happens to India’s marketplaces and companies like Uber, Flipkart, Zomato, and others?

However, if listening to a podcast isn’t your jam, I’ve picked out the top themes that we discussed in the episode and published them below. The text below has been extracted using an AI transcription tool and may contain slight edits for readability. 

Living with a personal agent

Shreyas Srinivasan

The personal agent category, it’s funny. They all seem a lot more useful by definition. But once I have them, I struggle to find use for them. […] On Sunday, I used Instinct. […] I was in Bangalore, and I wasn’t sure I wanted to go or not go. So I told it, every 20 minutes, check every show near me and tell me which one is filling, which one is not filling. So it gave me an update every 20 minutes about how many seats are left, which show, which theatre.

Shreyas Srinivasan

Here’s the funny thing where it breaks. Claude did it perfectly fine. I said, go to my email, remove the policy I bought last time, look at what I had and then do some research on whether I need anything else. Then go to these three websites, get me a quote, tell me which is the best quote, fill in my phone number and send me a UPI request on this ID. It did everything end to end. I bought the GoDigit one, which I usually do. Perfectly fine. It works for me. But the problem is, because it filled in my phone number on 14 different sites, I now get 14 different phone calls from everybody. Claude just went to 14 different websites, filled in my phone number, and got me a quote, which is what I expected it to do. But the real world broke that. Those people are like, “Hey, you haven’t bought insurance yet.”

Dharmesh Ba

I’ve been very reluctant to put any of these AI agents on WhatsApp because Whatsapp feels too personal to me, an extension of my identity. I’m okay putting it on Telegram, Slack, wherever it is. Whatsapp is just too—I’m still not there yet. But one place where I definitely would, other than the association, is the monthly GST payments and communicating with my CA, those OTPs. That’s definitely something I just want to give away.

Is India ready for them?

Shreyas Srinivasan

We have a history of assistance. As a country, culturally, having help at home and drivers at the office has been the norm. It’s not in the West. If you go explore this, there’s still a lot of pride in doing things yourself, in being self-sufficient in terms of cooking for yourself, taking care of your kids, doing the whole thing. India has always been okay with offloading some part of it. So culturally, we’re already there. We already offload.

Dharmesh Ba

Two days back, a friend of mine who runs a hardware store in Erode came and sat with me. In the one hour we spoke, he got at least 10 phone calls. Everything is just running on him. Did the new stock come in? What is the price for it? People are asking him to negotiate. He was negotiating with somebody who had a bill of one lakh twenty thousand, and the customer is coolly asking him to just round it off to one lakh. And he was talking about bills that haven’t been settled for the last six months. All of it. Some of the stuff happens only when he’s present, when he goes and shows his face and puts his word, and that’s when things start moving.

Shreyas Srinivasan

If we go down to intentions, the intentions of the majority of India are very value-driven, because we are still constrained in terms of wealth in this country. If you ask the majority of this country what AI agents could do, they would say, if they can earn me more money, that would be life-changing for me. If they can get me a bank loan, that would be life-changing to me. That’s where most of the country is. Automation is really far away from most people’s minds. That’s the 1% problem. There are interesting companies helping people use AI agents to improve their credit scores: how do you build a credit score in India so you can get a loan, so you can get working capital for your business? From a needle-moving point of view, that’s a great use case. That’s what most of this country needs. They don’t need automation. They need an ability to grow businesses.

Dharmesh Ba

What are we excited about? That the technology can do this automatically? At the end of the day, if my life is not improving in some meaningful manner, then it doesn’t matter whether it’s done automatically or manually. Even in this discussion, we are very excited about the technology itself. But I am more interested in the people side of things: what’s going to improve. Yes, we were all excited about a lot of work that can be done. But at the end of the day, if I am more stressed and more worked up because of all of these automations, then is my life meaningfully improving?

Who do Indians trust?

Dharmesh Ba

But with something like ChatGPT, I am asking it. So the relationship is more that I am asking it, and it is telling me. There is no inherent bias, as opposed to, let’s say, a branch relationship manager telling me something, because there’s always a doubt that there is an underlying incentive for him to suggest something or the other. Here I feel like I have more control. The next question was whether you would take the recommendations with a pinch of salt or go ahead and invest directly. There it’s more like, no, I will do further research. So going from unknown to some direction to known, at this moment ChatGPT seems like a bridge.

Shreyas Srinivasan

It’s gone up the trust chain. You can see the same thing happening with personal AI agents. They start with low trust, they build trust, and then you offload high-trust things to them. And before you know it, you’re offloading pretty much your most high-trust decision-making to them.

Shreyas Srinivasan

Agents probably allow you to disintermediate those platforms. You can see a world where you tell an AI agent to book a cab, and it has a network of cabs, and it can call 10 cabs to figure out which cab you want and save the 20% they give Uber. You can see this with commerce, with restaurants. But the problem there will be trust. Because we are a country that is going through growth in per capita [income], we also have low trust. That’s why marketplaces are big in India, because marketplaces enforce trust. I don’t see how AI agents enforce trust.

Dharmesh Ba

I remember once talking to a saree shop owner, and he told me the only trick for him to actually sell a saree is that he will show them hundreds of sarees. But he knows when to actually tell them, “This looks better on you”, and that is what convinces them to move from one thing to the other. I’m just thinking in people terms: will they trust him on something else? So I am putting a lot more trust in the person who is telling me what is good for me.

One agent, or many?

Shreyas Srinivasan

The intelligence in agents comes from the model. The agents have no intelligence at all. The agents are usually a harness. That’s my learning from building agents: they’re a harness on top of the intelligence of a model. And because the agents are able to test, and you are able to ascertain that they can do a particular task well, the agents are more predictable and accurate than the model itself. So the agents are efficiency multipliers and probability multipliers on top of model output. […] What is the limiter for this? As in the old world, access to capital and access to talent who can make this eval harness really strong for a given use case. And power law dictates that the most well-funded companies will have the most resources and talent to tackle the widest number of use cases. It’s a question of how businesses get built. The reason Instinct can go and raise whatever money is not what Instinct has today. It’s because Instinct already has a seat at the table of personal agents. And Instinct is then able to have 5,000 people and AI agents work on individual vertical use cases to make Instinct better than a single company that is only tackling insurance.

Shreyas Srinivasan

Lux Black and Lux Gold are the same soap. They’re targeted at different people and sold differently. […] You are already paying about Rs 1,000 to Claude. Claude does 350 things, and one service does one thing. At some point, Claude does 80% of what the service does at Rs 350, and you’re likely to see churn.

Dharmesh Ba

Something like sharing an OTP from one person to another, you don’t need a lot of intelligence. It’s just transferring data from one person to another. If I can replace a person, a person can just take the OTP from you and give it to her. It doesn’t matter which person it is. But when you’re asking for specialised use cases where there is some social status involved, like how do I get my daughter dressed up as this Disney character, […] I strongly believe there will be vertical agents specialised in doing a specific thing, especially where there is a little bit of social status involved.

Dharmesh Ba

Let’s say a daughter is not properly dressed, and then she goes and presents herself at school. Then the question is not just about the daughter. It’s also, what did your mother do, or what did your father do? Or if I am going to a wedding and I don’t appear well in the Instagram photos, then it’s also something that other people will look down upon me for.

Is India really different?

Shreyas Srinivasan

The majority of America has credit cards. India’s problem was that we didn’t have enough credit card adoption. We were still a cash economy, and hence UPI really moved the needle for us, because it moved us from a cash economy to a digital economy. Today, if you look at agentic payments, agents are capable of using credit cards to make payments everywhere. UPI gives us no fundamental advantage in agentic payments. NPCI itself has launched an agentic payments layer now. […] And it is no different from going to one of the card companies in America, where you can get a closed-loop credit card just for your agent. You can say, okay, my agent can only spend $1,000; here’s a credit card number I can give my agent. UPI has done UPI Circle, which is the delegation you were talking about. So I don’t think the payment rails give us any traditional advantage for why a personal agent should work here and not somewhere else.

Shreyas Srinivasan

It’s romantic to say India is this way, India is so unique, our patterns are so unique, nobody in the world has patterns like this. I don’t think it’s true. There are plenty of parts of the world which have similar patterns to India. There’s a reason why payment apps are that big in South America: trust was a problem till the government built a payment network. There’s a reason why Orkut was big in Brazil and big in India. You can see all of these patterns. I don’t think the patterns are that different. […] We sometimes overstate how different we are, simply because we live our lives and realise how different we are. The 5% feels like 50% because we live it every day.

What happens to the platforms

Shreyas Srinivasan

You’re going to see the entire UX form factor change. You’re already seeing fights come about, like Amazon just banned Muse, because all of these people who, over the last 20 years, built interfaces for people to use, interfaces that gave them engagement and retention, get disintermediated by these personal agents. And then there’s going to be a fight back saying, hey, you can’t use my interface without asking me.

Shreyas Srinivasan

The day that percentage becomes very big, they’re all either going to shut it or make sure there’s an incentive to order directly. You might get fares here, but if you order here, you get some discount, some loyalty points. Make our rails better.

Shreyas Srinivasan

Anywhere fulfilment infrastructure is standardised, or is digital, I think it is going to be a problem. I’ll give you an example: e-commerce. Fulfilment infrastructure is now standardised in this country. There are enough people who can fulfil something and ship it to you without requiring any single company’s rails. Amazon, Flipkart, Myntra, […] they’re going to be in trouble, because a lot of these brands can just go direct to customers using personal agents, and the brands have the pricing power to go direct. […] I’d apply the same thing to digital products like insurance and mutual funds, anything with digital fulfilment where the rails are also standardised.

Dharmesh Ba

If there is a way for me to hold my data and context and any AI agent can plug in, so it’s plug-and-play, then you’ll see the sudden emergence of a lot more competition, because the preferences are not just sitting at one company or one marketplace, and the inventory is not sitting at one marketplace. Even if I go to a smaller town, there might be a Rapido equivalent there which can plug into what I want and solve for me. That, I feel, is the opposite end of the spectrum from what you’re saying. It can become a level playing field, where not just a highly funded company can play, but even a smaller team in a smaller town with their own set of markets.
