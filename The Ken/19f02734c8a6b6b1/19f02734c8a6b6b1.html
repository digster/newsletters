The many contradictions of NSE going public. And what our reporting shows about the competition, governance, derivatives effect, and the valuation frenzy around its unlisted shares

India’s stock market listed itself this week.

Not literally, of course. But when the National Stock Exchange (NSE) filed its draft IPO papers with the regulator earlier this month, it did something almost recursive. The institution that has hosted nearly every major Indian IPO of the last decade, that has processed the paper on Zomato and LIC and Paytm* and a few thousand others, finally put its own paper in. The exchange that built India’s capital markets is now asking them to price it.

The number everyone reached for first was Rs 30,000 crore. Which is the estimated IPO size, probably the largest in Indian history. The number everyone reached for second was 60. That’s the percentage of NSE’s revenue that comes from a single product: equity options. 

The two numbers is possibly enough to show both the opportunity and the anxiety in this filing.

NSE, for instance, is legally prohibited from giving its employees stock options. The SECC Regulations, which are the rules governing market-infra institutions, bar the exchange from offering any equity-linked compensation. In a market where every fintech from Groww to Zepto uses ESOPs as their primary tool for hiring data scientists and AI engineers, NSE competes for the same talent with its hands tied behind its back. 

In fact, its own filing flags this, noting intense competition for people in “data science, cybersecurity, artificial intelligence”. The attrition rate among key managerial personnel was as high as 30% in FY24. The irony is that the institution that makes everyone else’s equity liquid cannot use equity to keep its own people.

Then, think about where NSE will actually list. Not on NSE, of course regulators prohibit self-listing, but on BSE. Which is fine, except that BSE is now NSE’s most aggressive commercial rival in the exact segment where the latter generates 60% of total revenue. BSE has been eating into NSE’s equity-options market share. 

So the exchange hosting NSE’s shares, responsible for fair price discovery in NSE stock, has a structural, commercial incentive to erode NSE’s core earnings. Of course, Sebi regulates both exchanges and would take a dim view of any creative gamesmanship.

Then there is the question of who owns NSE and who regulates it. LIC, the government’s insurance arm, holds 10.7% of the exchange, the largest single stake, and has a board nominee in place. Sebi issued the NOC that made this IPO possible. Several PSU insurers are selling into the IPO. After listing, the Indian government will simultaneously be NSE’s regulator, its largest shareholder via LIC, and a beneficiary of the transaction via the selling PSUs. 

What this means for future decisions on STT, on weekly expiry rules, on derivatives framework design, all of which directly affect NSE’s revenue… the draft IPO doc does not say.

None of this makes NSE a bad business. Only interesting. With a 51% global market share in equity derivatives, a 66% Ebitda margin, and over Rs 2,700 crore in annual income from broker-margin money it is legally required to hold, NSE is, in the language of markets, a toll booth at the only bridge. 

As it turns out, that is a thing we have been circling for years, sometimes through stories on derivatives, sometimes through governance, sometimes through regulation, and sometimes through the peculiar economics.

Business of being the market

The thing with monopolies is that they usually don’t realise they are becoming ordinary businesses until the day investors ask them to justify a valuation.

Sure, NSE’s filing looks like the listing of a highly profitable exchange. It is also the listing of a company whose biggest competitive advantage, and biggest vulnerability, was the same thing for years. India’s obsession with derivatives.

But that wasn’t always obvious.

Back in 2023, when most investors were worrying about a slowing stock market, How the NSE turned a bad year for India’s stock market into a great one for itself showed something fascinating. 

Cash-equity trading had weakened, but NSE’s profits kept climbing because options trading had become the exchange’s economic engine. Before the pandemic, options contributed less than a third of transaction revenue. Today, the prospectus shows equity derivatives account for roughly 60% of its operating revenue. 

Essentially, the exchange was making more money because India was trading more than it was investing. For a while, that was an unbeatable business model. We became the world’s largest derivatives market. 

But monopolies built around a single product eventually discover that concentration works both ways.

That was the premise of NSE’s monopoly is on borrowed time as Sebi squeezes its star player. Sebi’s decision to curb weekly options was more than an investor-protection exercise. It struck at the business that had transformed NSE’s economics over the previous five years. Our calculations then suggested the new framework could wipe out roughly a third of options volumes. 

Even if the final impact proves smaller, the larger point remains. That when 60% of revenue depends on one regulatory framework, regulation itself turns out to be a big business risk.

The intriguing part is what happened next.

For years, BSE was treated as the exchange that survived largely because history insisted it should. NSE had better tech, liquidity, and it had deeper derivatives markets and stronger network effects. So, the competition was mostly on paper.

In BSE was almost invisible. Then, a new CEO challenged NSE’s monopoly, we looked at how Sundararaman Ramamurthy did something surprisingly old-fashioned. He listened to brokers and tweaked contract sizes. He shifted expiry days and treated traders like customers instead of assuming they would always turn up.

Meanwhile, Sebi’s attempts to curb speculative excess in derivatives inadvertently gave BSE more room to compete. BSE one-ups NSE, again again showed how quickly sentiment can shift the rules of the game. The challenger suddenly had momentum, while the incumbent found itself defending the business that once looked untouchable.

That said, competition isn’t the only thing this IPO forces investors to think about. There is also governance.

One of the peculiar things about exchanges is that they are both marketplaces and market referees. They earn more when people trade more. They are also expected to monitor those trades for abuse. Those incentives generally coexist peacefully, until they don’t.

That tension ran through Did NSE sleep at the wheel in the Jane Street saga? The question of whether Jane Street manipulated markets is for the regulator to gauge. But why did India’s first-line market watchdog appear reactive rather than proactive?

Sebi had to prod NSE into examining the trading activity. The exchange took months to respond. Later, it reportedly closed its own probe shortly before Sebi acted decisively. None of that necessarily proves regulatory failure, but it illustrates the awkward position exchanges occupy. Every additional trade generates revenue and every suspicious trade demands scrutiny. And those incentives don’t always pull in the same direction.

The IPO brings another irony.

Retail investors have spent years trying to buy a slice of NSE before it listed. The prospectus finally gives them that opportunity. The question is whether many have already paid too much for the privilege.

That was the warning in Is NSE next in line after HDB and NSDL to burn unlisted-market investors—under Sebi’s watch? The unlisted market increasingly looks like a market driven less by valuation than by anticipation. 

Investors buy because they expect an IPO and others buy because they expect more investors to buy before the IPO. And so, the company’s actual business becomes secondary.

NSDL offered a recent reminder that IPO prices don’t necessarily validate unlisted-market exuberance. HDB Financial Services told a similar story. NSE’s own unlisted shares tripled in a year, driven largely by renewed confidence that the listing would finally happen. That confidence may well be justified, but the valuation is another matter.

For years, NSE was discussed as infrastructure. But infra isn’t supposed to have strategy, competitive threats, concentration risk, questions about growth, and so on and so forth. But public markets can change that. Once NSE lists, investors will move beyond questions on how well it runs the markets. 

And the queries around margins, profitability, governance, and more may be ordinary questions, but for an exchange that has spent three decades helping investors answer them about everyone else, they are quite new.
