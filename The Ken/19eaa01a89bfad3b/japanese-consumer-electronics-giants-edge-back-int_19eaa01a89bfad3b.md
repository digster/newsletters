A weekly newsletter about the biggest changes in commerce—focusing on shifts that matter to you.
Good Morning Ishan,
It has been ridiculously hot in Delhi. So, while browsing for an air conditioner, I found myself stalked by the usual barrage of targeted ads. One brand stood out: Akai.
The name felt oddly familiar. Akai was one of the Japanese brands that rode India’s first consumer-electronics boom before disappearing from the market after its partnership with Videocon ended in 2009.
Curious, I did what most shoppers would do. I checked Croma, Reliance Digital, and Vijay Sales. Akai wasn’t there. For a brand trying to re-enter India, it seemed oddly absent from where Indians actually buy electronics.
Akai wasn’t alone.
Japanese consumer brands are returning to India through focused product bets rather than the retail-heavy playbook that once made them household names.
JVC, once a familiar television brand in Indian households, re-entered the market in January last year through a licensing partnership with Super Plastronics Pvt Ltd (SPPL), launching a range of made-in-India smart QLED televisions.
OM System, the camera company that emerged from Olympus’ imaging business, formally returned to India in September last year with a range of cameras and lenses, betting on the rise of creators such as wildlife enthusiasts, and hobbyist photographers after exiting around 2020 following the latter’s global restructuring.
All in all, India is very popular with the Land of the Rising Sun, ranking first with “81.5% of Japanese companies willing to ‘expand’ their local operations over the next 1 to 2 years”, according to Jetro’s (Japan External Trade Organisation) 2025 data.
That trust is also reflected in the import bill. In the last two years, its growth rate has more than doubled.

This period has also seen several Japanese brands re-enter India with leaner ambitions and pruned product portfolios rather than chasing volumes.
Sharp, another Japanese electronics heavyweight whose visibility in India’s consumer market had waned over the years, re-entered the air-conditioner business last year, before announcing plans to deepen local manufacturing.
Then there is Mitsubishi Electric. Unlike the comeback brands, Mitsubishi is doubling down. In February this year, the company inaugurated a Rs 2,100-crore air-conditioner and compressor manufacturing facility in Chennai.
Their timing is strategic.
India’s $75-billion consumer electronics market is projected to nearly double to $130–150 billion by 2029, according to Redseer. Yet Japanese brands account for less than 5% of the market, it estimates.
Akai’s own return reflects that shift.
“Akai’s original India play was broad and undifferentiated. We competed on price and distribution without a sharp identity. That era is behind us,” Sharma said.
Instead of attempting to rebuild a full consumer-electronics empire, Akai is focusing on air conditioners and positioning itself in what it sees as an underserved middle ground.
“The crowding is real. But crowding at the extremes—ultra-premium on one end, race-to-the-bottom pricing on the other—has left a significant middle ground underserved. That’s our space,” he explained.
It is perhaps no coincidence that many of the returning Japanese brands are gravitating toward cooling.
India is expected to add 130–150 million new air conditioners over the next decade, according to estimates from the India Energy and Climate Centre at the University of California, Berkeley. Rising incomes, urbanisation, worsening heatwaves, and growing aspirations are fuelling India’s cooling boom—and companies are scrambling to piggyback on it.
Air conditioners have emerged as the preferred beachhead: Akai and Sharp are betting on the category, while Mitsubishi Electric is expanding manufacturing around it.
The challenge, however, is formidable.
Many of the returning brands have come back without the retail muscle that once made them household names. While they are present online and through selective distribution channels, they remain largely absent from the shelves of large-format electronics retailers such as Croma, Reliance Digital, and Vijay Sales.
In consumer electronics, distribution is often as [important](https://the-ken.com/podcasts/daybreak/after-a-blockbuster-ipo-lg-faces-a-tougher-test-selling-luxury-without-losing-loyalty/?searchTerm=After%20a%20blockbuster%20IPO%2C%20LG%20faces%20a%20tougher%20test%20%E2%80%94%20selling%20luxury%20without%20losing%20loyalty&utm_source=search&utm_medium=page) as the product itself. On that front, established players retain a considerable lead.
Chinese brands such as Xiaomi, Haier, and Hisense have spent years building scale through aggressive pricing and manufacturing efficiencies. Korean giants Samsung and LG have responded by broadening portfolios and pushing deeper into the mass market without fully dipping into irrational discounting.
Sharma is optimistic about Akai because legacy is no longer the only path to winning.
That means Japanese brands can no longer rely on nostalgia or engineering reputation alone. “Legacy is only a conversation-starter—it is not a reason to buy,” he said.
So those who never left are having to rewrite the playbook.
Panasonic, for instance, has exited refrigerators and washing machines as part of a strategic restructuring, choosing instead to focus on higher-growth and higher-margin businesses such as air conditioners, home automation, electrical solutions, and B2B offerings.
In air conditioners, which remain Panasonic’s strongest consumer category and grew by over 47% in FY25, smart models account for nearly half of its sales.
Sony and Hitachi are similarly concentrating on premium segments, large-screen televisions, and profitable categories while avoiding price wars that have defined much of the consumer-electronics market over the past decade.
Add to that, today’s consumers are younger, more trend-driven and more willing to switch brands. Product cycles are shorter, and durability is no longer the deciding factor it once was.
For Japan’s consumer-electronics giants, the challenge in India is less about reclaiming lost glory and more about finding a sustainable foothold in a crowded, fast-changing market.
The real test is whether those old moats—engineering and reliability—still matter to a generation that values upgrades and quick delivery as much as staying power.
