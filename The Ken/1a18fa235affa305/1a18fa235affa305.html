One morning at a factory on the edge of Bengaluru, an engineer put an in-house electric motor through a torque test. The results matched a standard motor. The one almost every Indian EV runs on. The one that depends on rare-earth magnets, most of which come from China. This motor needs no magnets at all.

My former colleague Keshav went to see it because China had restricted rare-earth exports, and Indian carmakers suddenly needed exactly this. Bajaj Auto, TVS Motor, Ather Energy had already cut production targets and faced revenue hits.

Yet this company, which had been around for more than five years and had the answer, did not win the orders as one would expect. Indian carmakers, Keshav found, are risk-averse and have underinvested in R&D… so most stockpiled magnets or bought them from other countries.

Keep that factory in mind.

On Thursday, at a conference in Delhi, Commerce Minister Piyush Goyal said that RCEP “would have been the death-knell of manufacturing in India”. RCEP is the 15-country trade pact also known as the Regional Comprehensive Economic Partnership, including China, that India helped negotiate and then walked out of in November 2019. Had it stayed in, Goyal said, “we would have been a nation of salesmen and salesgirls marketing Chinese goods in India”.

The companies in Keshav’s story are not salesmen. But all of them can’t build vehicles in India without a part from China, and a made-in-India substitute went unused. So I wondered whether “salesman” and “manufacturer” are the only two options, and whether the treaty had much to do with which one India ended up as.

I should say first that Goyal is describing a world that didn’t happen, and I can’t test it. RCEP might well have made things worse. What I can look at is the world that did happen, because India has now been outside the pact for nearly seven years.

In FY20, the year India walked out, it bought $65 billion of goods from China and sold ~$17 billion, a deficit of $48 billion. In FY26, it bought $132 billion and sold $19.5 billion, a deficit of $112 billion, according to government data. Imports have roughly doubled. China is now India’s largest trading partner, at $151 billion of two-way trade, ahead of the US at about $140 billion.

I’d put the conclusion modestly, since nobody knows what imports would have been otherwise. But it’s hard for me to read these numbers as showing a country that avoided depending on China.

The key question, then, I think, is what is actually coming in. The trade body Global Trade Research Initiative (GTRI) found that in 2023–24 about half of India’s imports from China were capital goods and machinery, and 37% were intermediate goods such as chemicals, drug ingredients, and plastics. The electronics numbers are sharper. A report in September found that China supplied at least 80% of India’s imports in 71 electronics product lines in FY26, up from 44 in 2018–19. Electrical machinery alone accounted for $43 billion of the deficit, about 38% of the total, while India’s exports in the same category to China were $3.2 billion. China supplied 83% of India’s lithium-ion battery imports in FY26, worth $4 billion, more than double the level of 2021–22.

The dependence, of course, is old. Olina wrote in November 2020, days after the other 15 countries signed the pact, that China already supplied about 45% of India’s smartphone-component imports and 37% of its electronic-component imports in 2019–20. The dependence was there before the exit, and as far as I can tell, it survived it.

Phones are a good test because we all own one. The IT Ministry says over 99% of the phones used in India are made in India, and phone exports were Rs 2.6 lakh crore in FY26. So India doesn’t really import phones. It imports what goes inside them. Domestic value addition, meaning the share of a phone’s price that is made in India, is 18–20% according to the head of the industry body ICEA, and 22–23% according to the IT secretary. 

ICEA says the biggest producing country manages nearly 40%, though it doesn’t name it. Components were just 9% of India’s electronics output in 2023–24, Business Standard reported in July.

A salesman sells goods made somewhere else. India isn’t quite doing that with phones. It assembles them and ships them out. As I understand it, RCEP’s main effect would have been lower tariffs on goods coming in from China. If almost no finished phones are imported, cutting the tariff on them would have changed little. What comes in are the parts, and I don’t think a tariff cut is what stops India from making those. RCEP would also have lowered duties on some parts, which might have deepened the dependence, but that isn’t what “salesmen” describes.

How India ended up here is partly a policy story. Priyal reported in January that Dixon, Syrma SGS, and Amber each spend under 1% of revenue on R&D, and that the joint ventures bringing in design and technology include Dixon with Longcheer, Syrma SGS with Shinhyup, and Bhagwati with Huaqin. 

The incentive scheme for phones paid 4–6% on incremental sales, and Anushka reported in 2023 that most of the firms claiming it were foreign-owned contract manufacturers, among them Samsung, Foxconn, Wistron, and Pegatron. A scheme that pays for sales volume gets volume. 

I don’t think that was a mistake exactly, since phone exports did grow, but it didn’t pay for much else.

India’s own tariffs may also push in the wrong direction. The Economic Survey said in January that higher duties on intermediates and capital goods than on final products can lead to “inverted duty structures”. The electronics industry told the finance ministry that finished equipment already enters at nil duty, while the components and sub-assemblies to make it face 5% to 25%. 

If that’s right, an Indian parts maker pays more on its inputs than an importer pays on the finished part. I’m not a customs expert, so please see that as the industry’s claim. If it is true, it’s a choice India makes in its own tariff schedule, and India could change it without any trade pact. 

RCEP would have cut duties on some Chinese inputs, which might have eased the problem for those goods, but I haven’t seen anyone work out by how much.

Bhagwati Products, which I wrote about in April, shows how far this goes. It made about Rs 650 crore of phones in FY24 and was expected to make Rs 15,000 crore in FY26, a 25X jump in two years in a flat market, with no hit product and no big Indian customer. What it had, as Priyal reported in October 2025, was a Chinese partner. Shanghai’s Huaqin bought 49% of it for Rs 514 crore, and orders from Oppo and Vivo followed. 

Bhagwati assembles about 8% of India’s smartphones and employs about 10,000 people, and I wrote then that this looked like India being plugged into the Chinese system rather than leaving it.

The trade deals India did sign don’t look much better. The Print reported in May that India’s deficit under the ASEAN agreement was $43–46 billion in FY24, with Korea at about $15.2 billion in FY25 and Japan at about $10.7 billion in FY26 through November, and that India’s use of its trade pacts is estimated at about 25%, against 70–80% in developed economies. 

Those aren’t China numbers, so I wouldn’t lean on them too hard. But Goyal himself has complained that Chinese firms route goods through ASEAN countries to claim tariff concessions, and the above-cited report says Chinese-origin goods also reach India through Hong Kong, Singapore, and Vietnam. 

A treaty with China was never the only door.

Then there is what the government is doing now. In April 2020, India’s Press Note 3 put investment from China under government approval. This March, the Cabinet loosened it. As I understand the reports, investors with under 10% non-controlling Chinese ownership can use the automatic route, and in five sectors (electronic capital goods and components, polysilicon and wafers, advanced battery components, and rare earth magnets) Chinese-linked investment will receive faster approvals, reportedly within 60 days, as long as an Indian resident holds the majority. 

The final list of products hasn’t been published, as far as I could find. Another report goes further, saying restricting imports won’t fix a gap that lies in upstream components, and recommending that India selectively allow Chinese manufacturers to produce here.

Shristi reported this in April 2025. For electronics makers that want to move up the value chain, tying up with Chinese companies was becoming inevitable, she wrote, and Dixon had already partnered with Hong Kong’s HKC on display modules.

So the government that says RCEP would have made India a nation of salesmen is now writing rules so that Chinese companies can help make the parts in India, with an Indian partner holding the majority. That may well be the right move. I just notice it isn’t the strategy the exit seemed to stand for, and it isn’t a tariff strategy.

Beyond price, China began needing licences to ship 17 rare earth metals last year. Suprita reported that no magnet shipment had reached India since April 2025, that about 30 import requests hadn’t been approved, and that Indian EV production could stall by mid-July. 

Maruti Suzuki had by then cut the production target for its first electric SUV, the e-Vitara, by nearly 70%. The Economic Times reported in August 2025 that China had agreed to ease the curbs on magnets, speciality fertilisers, and tunnel-boring machines during Wang Yi’s visit. A country that depends on another for 80% of a component can have its supply cut for reasons that have nothing to do with trade policy, and that holds whether or not it signed RCEP.

Goyal may not be wrong about everything and could be right about parts of his case that I haven’t looked at here, and I don’t know what RCEP would have done to the deficit. What I’m fairly sure of is that “we stayed out” and “we aren’t dependent” are different statements, and only the first one is true.

Salesperson or manufacturer: I am not sure either word fits India right now, and I don’t have a better one. What would you call it?
