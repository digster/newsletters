Last week I also found out something quite revealing about Gabru. We’d enlisted a dog-walker for him the week that we were on vacation. Now, I cross paths with his walker walking other dogs every other morning while I’m walking Gabru. And boy, it is a scene. Gabru lunges and barks at the other (male) dogs like there’s no tomorrow. It takes all my strength to hold him back as we pass them.

Early on, when he was just a few years old, I’d mistakenly assumed it was “alpha” behaviour. Then I realised it was literally the opposite—it was fear-based aggression aggravated by leash walking. Hence also called “leash aggression”.

I understood the theory, but it would take a driving vacation to Goa some years back to see it in action. The first day we walked Gabru nervously on the beach, alert to all the local indies around.

Then, a guy who was hanging around asked us to let Gabru off-leash. We were, naturally, very apprehensive. But the guy was like, “Don’t worry. They’ll meet each other, sniff each other, and usually back off. And even if they do fight, it won’t be serious.”

So, we tried it. And saw with our own eyes as a bunch of local dogs walked up to Gabru and all of them sniffed each other. I was tense, waiting to break up a fight.

It never happened. 

Instead, we got to see Gabru running along the beach, unleashed, jumping in and out of the gentle waves. I can swear that he’s positively smiling and laughing in all the videos and photos we have of him unleashed on the beach. It was his first (and alas, as of yet, only) beach vacation.

Coming back to a few weeks ago. The walker told me last week that Gabru was the most wonderful, friendly, and calm dog when he took him for walks each day. 

What?!

Yes. He would meet other dogs. Wag his tail. Sniff them. And generally be an all-round nice guy. 

So, I was the problem?!

3. The power of imagining the shape of great answers

Most people know the value of asking great questions. You know that in order to ask great questions, you need to first find the right problem https://the-ken.com/newsletter/first-principles/value-problems-more-than-solutions/.

But I’m also not here today to talk about questions. Instead, I want to talk about answers.

In our “standard” way of working, we don’t really think that much about the answers. Instead, we find problems, to which we seek solutions, in pursuit of which we ask questions. Many answers emerge as we traverse the many roads this process takes us through. The more complex and challenging the problem, the higher the likelihood we go down many roads, only to backtrack and then go down different ones.

There is nothing wrong with this approach. It is how we solve tough problems. It is also how we learn and grow.

But if we’re being honest, we don’t spend a lot of time thinking about the ideal answer we’re seeking. “I’ll know it when I see it,” we tell ourselves. (Yes, I know about the “obscenity” reference?)

You must have seen this play out many times at work when you take the hard-earned conclusion or recommendation of your project to a manager or decision maker, only to be told, “This isn’t what I was looking for.”

In most cases, they didn’t define it for you up front. Probably because, I bet, they didn’t define it to themselves either. Most don’t know what the ideal answer or solution they’re looking for is, but they have enough experience and judgement to know it when they see it.

I’m guilty of the same. But I’ve noticed something shift within me. I’ve started thinking about “the shape” of answers more often, especially as I’m in the early stages of grappling with complex and creative problems.

The blame or credit goes to large language models.

AI tools built around LLMs don’t have any sense of right or wrong, accurate or false. They only know how to complete the sequence of words given to them in the best possible manner. If you give them a question, they’ll try to complete it with an answer that’s likely to satisfy you. If you give them a problem, they’ll give you the sequence of words that looks closest to what you’d call a solution.

You really don’t get “answers” from LLMs, instead you get “answer-shaped responses”. That means, a sequence of words that is most likely to satisfy your definition of an answer.

It is this desire to satisfy us (through the process of extending our words) that leads to AI models built around LLMs to be sycophantic https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sycophancy_(artificial_intelligence).

It is also the reason why it’s so easy for us to be led down the path of gloriously constructed AI answers and solutions when we offer questions or problems that are thinly defined.

It isn’t something you can keep up for too long though. So, I noticed I’ve started explicitly specifying “the shape” of the ideal solution I’m looking for up front. So that, when I get an “answer-shaped response”, it is most likely to fit that shape.

The better I define my problems (in the AI world, “prompts”), the better the “solutions” I get.

I’m not making some claim of novelty or radical discovery here. This concept of “working backwards”—assume the solution has already been found, then derive backwards to arrive at the question that would produce it—traces back over 2000 years to ancient Greek philosophers like Pappus.

Perhaps you’ve read about a more contemporary version of it, credited to Amazon https://workingbackwards.com/concepts/working-backwards-pr-faq-process/?

Working Backwards is a systematic way to vet ideas and create new products. Its key tenet is to start by defining the customer experience, then iteratively work backwards from that point until the team achieves clarity of thought around what to build. Its principal tool is a second form of written narrative called the PR/FAQ, short for Press Release and Frequently Asked Questions.

Skip to management consulting, a field that bills clients for finding solutions to complex problems. StrategyU has a good summary https://strategyu.co/pyramid-principle-3/ of a similar approach from Barbara Minto, the first woman MBA to be hired by McKinsey.

Her definition is straightforward: a problem exists when there is a gap between where you are now (she calls this R1) and where you want to be (R2). R1 is your current undesired result. R2 is your desired result. The problem is the gap between them.

This sounds almost too simple. But the reason it matters is that most people are fuzzy about both R1 and R2. They have a vague sense that something isn’t working, but they haven’t clearly articulated either where they are or where they want to be. And if you can’t do that, you can’t define the problem, which means you definitely can’t solve it.

Perhaps the single best example I’ve come across of answer-backwards thinking is from AT&T. Here’s an excerpt about it from the book, Idealised Design by Russell L Ackoff, Jason Magidson, and Herbert J Addison.

Idealized design is a way of thinking about change that is deceptively simple to state: In solving problems of virtually any kind, the way to get the best outcome is to imagine what the ideal solution would be and then work backward to where you are today. This ensures that you do not erect imaginary obstacles before you even know what the ideal is.

To conclude today’s essay, I’ll bring back Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi, the late Hungarian-American psychologist who came up with the concept of “Flow”, but incredibly, in his second book, The Creative Vision, also explained why finding better problems from solutions is a higher-order skill https://thereader.mitpress.mit.edu/the-1960s-art-school-experiment-that-redefined-creativity/ than finding solutions from problems.

Csikszentmihalyi understood that creativity does not begin with a solution, or even with a clearly defined problem. It begins earlier, in a more uncertain and searching state of mind. As he wrote in “The Creative Vision”: “This ability to formulate problems seems to be a faculty of a different order. It entails a process far more in touch with the deeper layers of being than reason alone usually is; it is far more holistic in that it encompasses the person’s total experiential state. Problem finding may well be at the origin of the creative process.”

4. Trying times

This is Tanim, and hello. Do you read books in times of crisis?

I think that’s when we should read more.

Whenever unsettling events that you don’t know how to make sense of happen around you, pick up a book.

Not only something that reflects the times around us, but anything.

If you are an escapist, pick up a fantasy or someone’s memoir. If you want catharsis, pick up a dystopian novel or someone’s memoir.

There are many narratives out there, and we want to be on the right side of history. But if we only consume others’ narratives, what do we think? How personally impacted do we feel by the situation at hand?

Reading a book slows down those thoughts. Helps us think from different perspectives. 

And right now, what we desperately need is education, good ideas, and the ability to listen. 

We want someone to take action, someone to take accountability, someone to solve the problems around us. But where are the solutions? Where are the ideas? They come from reading and holding dialogue.

So tell us what you’re reading right now. Maybe someone will resonate with you and pick up that book. You can share your recommendations here https://theken.typeform.com/FPBooks?typeform-source=the-ken.com. 

Books are leisure. But they are also protest. It’s an action.

As long as we read—anything at all—there’s hope for people to keep writing, to keep believing that maybe someone will read those words someday and understand them.

Not the AI-polished narratives but raw human writing that calls to people. Raw human writing that was written to make sense of confusing times. Raw human writing that expresses emotions and feelings that were always within us but that we failed to acknowledge.

5. Documentary pick(s) of the week

Hey there!

This is Vishnu—and this week, I thought I’d switch things up a bit and give you a couple of documentary suggestions.

PSA: I’m a firm believer that you should go in cold-turkey for a documentary, unlike movies. So my suggestion would be to not watch any trailer or read up about them. Just hit play.

1. Three Identical Strangers (2018)

Other than all the mini-documentaries on Discovery or Nat Geo, I think this was the first full-length documentary I watched.

I remember watching it in my second year of college—and being absolutely amazed by the story, and how well-made it was. Like I said, do not read up on it. 

Streaming on Prime Video/Netflix. Depending on where you are.

2. The King of Kong: A Fistful of Quarters (2007)

I stumbled into this during a random Youtube session, thanks to my wonky taste and unpredictable suggestions.

It was about an obscure Arcade game that I hadn’t heard of and about two players’ attempt at beating the high score. On paper, it was a classic David-Goliath battle, but within 20 minutes, I realised this was much more, and I was HOOKED.

Lying, scheming, cheating, obsessive competitiveness, luck, bad luck, and an absolutely epic showdown at the end. This has it all!

Better yet, you can watch it on Youtube https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lXLQqcHcJDQ!

3. Icarus (2017)

What began as an experiment by a high-level amateur cyclist (also the director) to test the efficacy of doping in cycling ends up exposing one of the biggest sports scandals of all time.

It’s a rollercoaster ride that can easily get slotted into a Mission: Impossible movie, and the aftermath—the Russian athletes being banned from the Olympics—is enough reason for you to not miss it.

In 2018, the documentary won the Oscar for Best Documentary Feature.

Icarus is streaming on Netflix.
