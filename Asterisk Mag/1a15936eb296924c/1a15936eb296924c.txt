In September of 1900, Abraham Jacobi, a German emigre whose medical activism made him a prominent figure in the States, made note [ https://substack.com/redirect/a314d8b6-dab6-4980-958c-f33cf222ce19?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] of a peculiarity of American medicine: The United States boasted far more practicing physicians than the countries in Europe.
Per capita, the United States had twice as many physicians as Great Britain, four times as many as France, five times as many as Germany, six times as many as Italy, and 14 times as many as Russia. Modern data [ https://substack.com/redirect/2de05f94-0adc-44e6-9fe6-9666e8e0b7d6?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] that those in 1900 weren’t fortuitous enough to access make this gap even more curious, as the disparities in wealth and living standards were not large enough to account for the gap in the number of doctors.
A century and a quarter later, the script has entirely flipped and the doctor gap has reversed [ https://substack.com/redirect/f9d5a2e6-7aba-4e43-bd15-1e71f9127131?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ]. The United Kingdom and France have 20% more practicing physicians per capita than the United States and that number increases to 50% and 66% when looking at Italy and Germany, respectively. We are currently in the midst of a physician shortage that is only set to become worse.
The story of America’s relative pittance of physicians shows how prudent policies can transform into regulatory capture. 
Origins in the Progressive Era
It would be hard to exaggerate just how lacking medical regulations were in the 1880s, especially as compared to European countries. As one chronicler of the age put it, states “were unwilling to enact laws which would have seriously deterred unlicensed practitioners…the penalties for unlicensed practice were generally nonexistent or insignificant.” This Wild West style of medical regulation allowed for all sorts of quack “cures” to endanger public health, from rabid dog saliva to mixtures containing insufficiently diluted deadly poisons.
Progressive Era reformers made it their mission to raise the standards for medical practice. In addition to banning “medicines” that were dangerous, legislatures implemented new requirements to improve public health. Between the 1890s and 1920s, states enacted reforms shutting down diploma mill medical schools and requiring practicing physicians to actually have licenses.
There was good reason for reformers to seek these changes — as Americans moved from rural communities to big cities during this period, they could no longer rely on doctors they knew personally and now had to choose one of many doctors advertising their services. In this more impersonal world, it became difficult to figure out which physicians were actually worthy of trust (and money).
In large part because of these laws, the number of practicing physicians per capita declined by almost a quarter [ https://substack.com/redirect/163774a9-1850-491a-98a4-25d5e14ff0ad?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] in 20 years. This number would stabilize as those who sought to enter the medical profession began to recognize the value (or at least the practical necessity) of proper credentials.
It also taught a lesson to the medical societies, the groups formed with a primary purpose of advocating for already established doctors. After groups like the American Medical Association succeeded in pushing for licensing reforms and heightening standards for medical schools, they learned just how much power they had in getting legislators to restrict the supply of physicians. Their sway, in no small part earned by the justified trust that Americans have in their doctors, has only grown since the Age of Reform.
The Balanced Budget Act of 1997
By the 1950s, America’s rate of physicians per capita had fallen back down to Earth — in fact, policymakers, fearing a shortage of physicians, would soon work to substantially expand access to medical school and permit a greater number of foreign-trained doctors into the U.S. This was so successful that just three decades later, the opposite fear took hold of those studying healthcare policy: a supposed excess of practicing physicians. A May 1997 New York Times headline captured the zeitgeist well [ https://substack.com/redirect/6cf1c1fc-d465-4d3f-af53-fa141c814346?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ]: “Doctors Assert There Are Too Many of Them.”
Policymakers, scholars, and especially groups representing doctors and doctors-to-be asserted that this was a problem for multiple reasons. One was that physicians with more free hours would have a higher willingness to recommend procedures that patients don’t need, wasting their time and potentially jeopardizing their health. Another was that because so many doctors receive payments from the government, a physician surplus would come at a considerable cost to taxpayers. Many were worried that the number of specialists — who made up a larger share of doctors in the U.S. than in other developed nations — would exacerbate these problems, as they had access to expensive and high-margin operations that they’d be especially incentivized to recommend.
In 1997, Congress passed the Balanced Budget Act, the most important law to contemporary discussions of medical regulatory capture. The 1997 law, which primarily aimed to reduce the budget deficit by curtailing the amount of money spent on providers, also sought to eliminate the supposed excess of practicing physicians.
Title IV, Subtitle G, Chapter 2 placed a strict cap on the number of residency slots that were funded by Medicare. Originally, Medicare funding for residency slots was open-ended but, with support from the American Medical Association, the law indefinitely froze the number of residency slots that each hospital could receive money for. 
This both reduced the federal financial burden, a major sticking point for the Clinton Administration, and made it much harder for medical school graduates to become physicians. As of just a couple of years ago, only about 116,000 [ https://substack.com/redirect/15926c11-5e0b-4517-ae7f-8a0fbedb668d?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] of about 160,000 [ https://substack.com/redirect/9d46e4f5-8d84-4628-b58f-20ab8a0aecf5?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] full-time-equivalent resident positions receive funding from Medicare, with many of the rest relying on states, hospitals, and philanthropic organizations.
The effects have been deleterious, to say the least. Although the number of physicians in the U.S has been increasing per capita, it has not kept up once you account for America’s aging and the fact that older patients require more care than their younger counterparts. Because of the relative shrinking of America’s working-age population, the increased number of seniors means we’re on track for a physician shortage of over 140,000 by 2038 [ https://substack.com/redirect/7872b7d0-c7f2-4425-a139-ba99acd39eb1?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ]. While it’s unreasonable to say this funding change is completely responsible for the doctor shortage, it has not helped.
A critic might argue, reasonably, that we shouldn't simply assume that more funding for residency slots will actually translate to more medical school graduates attaining residencies — there could very well be bottlenecks unrelated to financial causes. While this is of course true, there should be little doubt about the role the Medicare cap plays in limiting the number of residencies. For instance, one study [ https://substack.com/redirect/efd45b77-3933-49e2-8c68-783ca3ca101b?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] found that under an Affordable Care Act reform that expanded the number of funded residencies in certain hospitals, a one-slot increase in the number of funded slots was associated with 1.3 additional resident FTEs trained.
These exemptions to the residency cap have not appeared to make anyone in the medical establishment upset. In fact, virtually every organization that fought so tirelessly to limit the number of physicians in the 1990s has U-turned, throwing their support behind bipartisan legislative pushes [ https://substack.com/redirect/db7830fd-9201-461a-8641-822600e6dbda?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] to fix the problem. While the physician shortage remains a cautionary tale of regulatory capture, the current crisis is more one of legislative inertia.
Expanding coverage and the physician bottleneck
The physician shortage is bad on its own, but it’s a major problem for any initiative that seeks to expand health coverage in the U.S. 
Although the U.S. has significantly fewer physicians per capita than most [ https://substack.com/redirect/a99ff3b0-e8c4-4abc-833c-e25911d84ae8?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] of our developed peers, the average physician in the States also has far fewer consultations with patients than doctors in most other advanced economies. One reason for this discrepancy is that America does not have universal healthcare coverage. Evidence from public health insurance programs in America supports this, too: When those without [ https://substack.com/redirect/421f9622-122a-4f13-8e4c-0f2552aafe77?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] health insurance receive it, they are much likelier to go to the doctor and when those without face higher co-pays [ https://substack.com/redirect/8049c67c-f935-45de-85e3-51002bf40770?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ], they are much less likely.
The cost barriers to medical access are a moral travesty and an economic boondoggle. One of the top priorities for policymakers should be addressing the rate of uninsurance, especially in states that didn’t expand Medicaid. Any initiative to further expand healthcare access — whether through existing programs like Medicaid or new programs like one covering all children — needs to figure out how to increase the supply of healthcare providers to make sure that more patients aren’t competing for the same amount of slots offered by physicians. In addition to being bad of its own accord, such a situation risks blowback from the medical establishment if doctors are inundated with more patients without enjoying higher pay.
When faced with an impending shortage in the 1950s, the response was to ramp up the number of medical graduates. Now that we face the same problem and one of the best resposes is to ramp up the opportunities for medical graduates to become residents. Eliminating the cap on Medicare-funded residency slots won’t solve all of the bottleneck problems in healthcare, but it’s a respectable start.
Ahmed Ahmed is a freelance writer and University of Chicago alum.
