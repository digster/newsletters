Origins in the Progressive EraIt would be hard to exaggerate just how lacking medical regulations were in the 1880s, especially as compared to European countries. As one chronicler of the age put it, states “were unwilling to enact laws which would have seriously deterred unlicensed practitioners…the penalties for unlicensed practice were generally nonexistent or insignificant.”¹ This Wild West style of medical regulation allowed for all sorts of quack “cures” to endanger public health, from rabid dog saliva to mixtures containing insufficiently diluted deadly poisons. Progressive Era reformers made it their mission to raise the standards for medical practice. In addition to banning “medicines” that were dangerous, legislatures implemented new requirements to improve public health. Between the 1890s and 1920s, states enacted reforms shutting down diploma mill medical schools and requiring practicing physicians to actually have licenses. There was good reason for reformers to seek these changes — as Americans moved from rural communities to big cities during this period, they could no longer rely on doctors they knew personally and now had to choose one of many doctors advertising their services. In this more impersonal world, it became difficult to figure out which physicians were actually worthy of trust (and money). After groups like the American Medical Association succeeded in pushing for licensing reforms and heightening standards for medical schools, they learned just how much power they had in getting legislators to restrict the supply of physicians. Their sway, in no small part earned by the justified trust that Americans have in their doctors, has only grown since the Age of Reform. The Balanced Budget Act of 1997By the 1950s, America’s rate of physicians per capita had fallen back down to Earth — in fact, policymakers, fearing a shortage of physicians, would soon work to substantially expand access to medical school and permit a greater number of foreign-trained doctors into the U.S. This was so successful that just three decades later, the opposite fear took hold of those studying healthcare policy: a supposed excess of practicing physicians. A May 1997 New York Times headline captured the zeitgeist Policymakers, scholars, and especially groups representing doctors and doctors-to-be asserted that this was a problem for multiple reasons. One was that physicians with more free hours would have a higher willingness to recommend procedures that patients don’t need, wasting their time and potentially jeopardizing their health. Another was that because so many doctors receive payments from the government, a physician surplus would come at a considerable cost to taxpayers. Many were worried that the number of specialists — who made up a larger share of doctors in the U.S. than in other developed nations — would exacerbate these problems, as they had access to expensive and high-margin operations that they’d be especially incentivized to recommend. In 1997, Congress passed the Balanced Budget Act, the most important law to contemporary discussions of medical regulatory capture. The 1997 law, which primarily aimed to reduce the budget deficit by curtailing the amount of money spent on providers, also sought to eliminate the supposed excess of practicing physicians. Title IV, Subtitle G, Chapter 2 placed a strict cap on the number of residency slots that were funded by Medicare. Originally, Medicare funding for residency slots was open-ended but, with support from the American Medical Association, the law indefinitely froze the number of residency slots that each hospital could receive money for. This both reduced the federal financial burden, a major sticking point for the Clinton Administration, and made it much harder for medical school graduates to become physicians. One of the top priorities for policymakers should be addressing the rate of uninsurance, especially in states that didn’t expand Medicaid. Any initiative to further expand healthcare access — whether through existing programs like Medicaid or new programs like one covering all children — needs to figure out how to increase the supply of healthcare providers to make sure that more patients aren’t competing for the same amount of slots offered by physicians. In addition to being bad of its own accord, such a situation risks blowback from the medical establishment if doctors are inundated with more patients without enjoying higher pay. When faced with an impending shortage in the 1950s, the response was to ramp up the number of medical graduates. Now that we face the same problem and one of the best resposes is to ramp up the opportunities for medical graduates to become residents. Eliminating the cap on Medicare-funded residency slots won’t solve all of the bottleneck problems in healthcare, but it’s a respectable start.
