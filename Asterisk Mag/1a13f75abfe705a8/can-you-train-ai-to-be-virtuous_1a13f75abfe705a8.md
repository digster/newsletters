The virtues, for the great empiricist Franklin, were worth cultivating in part because of the practical advantages they seemed to confer. In studying and seeking to perfect the virtues, Franklin was joining a tradition that dates back to Aristotle. It was Aristotle who offered the most influential systematic study of virtue as a locus for moral thought. Virtue enjoyed a central role in Western thought for roughly two millennia afterward, and then began, in the 18th and 19th centuries, to fade; it was only in the second half of the 20th century, through the work of philosophers like Elizabeth Anscombe and Alasdair MacIntyre, that virtue reemerged, now under the aegis of “virtue ethics” — a primarily neo-Aristotelian project critical of the wayward bent of moral theorists of the time. Decades have passed since their seminal contributions, and it would seem, in our age seemingly so bereft of it, that “virtue” is having its moment once again. Before we get into what this means, I want to adopt a useful distinction from alignment and ethics researcher Iason Gabriel. In ensuring that powerful AI is reliably aligned with human values, we have a technical and normative problem. The technical problem, exemplified by approaches like RLHF, focuses on how to encode values in agents so they do what they ought to do. The normative problem — exemplified by CEV — asks what values we ought to encode in agents, and whether we want to encode values at all. In reality, it is difficult to fully separate the normative and technical alignment problems. Our normative commitments and beliefs about how models learn bear on the technical alignment procedure we should adopt. On the other hand, the alignment researchers who have embraced character training are not, necessarily, making a normative commitment to virtue ethics — rather, they believe that virtue concepts can provide robust mechanical tools for alignment. Without committing to a full-throated “virtue ethics,” then, we might think that virtue language is useful for describing alignment and developing a technical alignment procedure. Amanda Askell and Joe Carlsmith, two authors of Claude’s Constitution, have expressed this in different ways. Askell is more direct, arguing that forming a model’s judgment seems less brittle and generalizable than asking it to follow lists of rules. We cannot specify what we want a model to do in every possible situation. Therefore, if we want to build a model we can trust to act as we would wish in situations we had not predicted, forming its character is a more robust approach. Carlsmith is not straightforwardly a virtue ethicist, but his work Other researchers think virtue approaches correct an inadequate picture of agency. Peli Grietzer, an alignment researcher with Arb Research, offers an alternative picture of rational agency that emphasizes participation in specific practices. Grietzer’s notion of practices recalls Alasdair MacIntyre’s in his 1981 book, After Virtue. For MacIntyre, a practice could be any kind of established, cooperative human activity with internal standards of excellence, from mathematics to art to ping-pong. To develop an account of how we might solve problems in AI safety, Grietzer extends this account to domain-general, “adverbial” practices such as kindness and honesty, which shape how an agent participates in its other activities (kindly, honestly). His normative objection to standard alignment approaches is that treating kindness as an outcome to maximize leaves out an important part of what we care about in valuing kindness: the kind manner in which the outcome is pursued. He thinks this matters for how an AI develops. Strategies that help an agent succeed can reinforce themselves. So, an agent that repeatedly advances its aims through manipulation can become better at manipulation, even if those aims are something like kindness. Grietzer hopes that training models to act kindly can instead build the capacities and conditions for further kindness, so successful action strengthens the disposition we wanted to cultivate. But how would we tell whether training had produced the intended commitment — a model that acts kindly or, in Carlsmith’s words, “just values honesty”? Benjamin Franklin’s attempt to acquire humility shows us that the appearance and internalization of a virtue are not exactly the same. We can sample responses from the language model in various situations and grade those responses according to a standard, but this can’t tell us much about the question we really care about, which is how the model might behave in situations we didn’t think to mention. Other approaches, like mechanistic interpretability, can investigate the mechanisms behind apparently virtuous conduct, but connecting those findings to an account of virtue requires specifying what would count as the relevant motivation in the first place. To clarify further how virtuous dispositions relate to model character, consider what is being claimed when a virtue term like “courage” is said to guide an AI system’s actions. Suppose we are able to grade language model outputs for courage. There are then at least three possibilities: 1. The model learns a conception of courage: It can predict how raters classify cases. 2. The model acquires an inferential competence: It distinguishes courage from recklessness, explains relevant reasons, transfers this knowledge to novel cases, and revises its statements under criticism. 3. Courage becomes stably and practically integrated into the model’s “character” or “standpoint”: Courage organizes the system’s attention, deliberation, conduct, and commitments — even when the model is under pressure and not explicitly asked to reason about courage. These are increasingly strong claims — evidence that a model can classify cases and reason about courage does not by itself establish that courage stably governs its conduct. It is that last feature, though, that mattered to Aristotle: He distinguishes the kind of act that a virtuous person would produce from performing that action virtuously. To perform an action virtuously, one must do that action knowingly, deliberately choose that action for its own sake, and act from a firm state of character (Nicomachean Ethics II.4). Published work In the search for a more complete notion of machine morality, I find it useful to draw on another idea of MacIntyre’s: that embodying a character means occupying a social role with its own appropriate virtues. MacIntyre himself would likely argue that, unlike humans, models lack the traditions, communities, and unified life histories that make virtues coherent. But this does not mean that models cannot approximate any model of virtue whatsoever. If a human can embody the virtues of a good parent, a good plumber, or a good research engineer, is it really so unreasonable to think about the virtues of a good LLM assistant? At the same time, we should recognize that these virtues might look very different from our own. Virtues, classically understood, are what enable human flourishing in accordance with human nature — it should be no surprise that extending these notions too eagerly into the machine realm runs into limitations. If we are to make virtue ethics work for AIs, then we ought to invest more in the view of models as fallible participants in practices and shared processes of inquiry, through which we collectively attempt to promote flourishing. If we do decide to pursue character and virtue in language models, then I think we ought to heed the call from Elizabeth Anscombe, whose “Modern Moral Philosophy” inspired the 20th-century revival of virtue ethics. Just as she then called for a philosophy of psychology to understand human action and intention before developing moral philosophy, we now ought to develop a psychology of language models adequate to determine what a “character” would even be.
