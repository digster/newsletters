In this behind-the-scenes interview, Asterisk managing editor Angela Chen interviews editor in chief Clara Collier about her essay After Work, We’ll Have Each Other [ https://substack.com/redirect/921b3bd3-2215-462d-90f3-97cff0f24281?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ]. The two discuss why meaning is such a slippery concept, why European salon culture was not an intellectual paradise, and why we should fear the relational world.
This interview has been edited for length and clarity.
Angela Chen: The original version of your essay began with this long intro referring to certain conversations you’d been having about “AI and work.” I made you cut that intro because we needed this essay to be a reasonable length, but I promised you we’d talk about why you found those discussion so frustrating.
Clara Collier: There’s so much I cut — I think I cut more than made it into the final piece. And I should say, a lot of these conversations were with people I deeply respect, who have important thoughts. It’s not that everyone talking about the future of work is wrong. But I was having a lot of conversations in circles where there’s a fairly common belief that human labor will be obviated one way or another. I’m on the fence about this myself, but I think it’s an important issue to think through.
Anyway, a common answer was, “We’ll get our meaning from personal relationships.” My response was that this is how the vast majority of cultures have  worked throughout history, and it wasn’t better, it was worse!
Angela: Before we get to why it was worse, I’m still thinking about how there was a whole tangent about “meaning” that you eventually cut because it was too broad and too slippery. Can you tell me about the reading and thinking you did when you thought the piece would focus more on that?
Clara: I read Viktor Frankl — Man’s Search for Meaning — and went down a whole tangent through the meaning of “meaning” in mid-century thought. Maslow is the big one — most people know him as “the pyramid guy,” but he also wrote a book called Eupsychian Management, about how to run a business in a way that’s psychologically wholesome for employees. That book helped popularize the idea — he wasn’t the first to say it, but he helped establish — that a good management environment is one where an employee’s identity is wrapped up in their work, and the work helps them become a fuller person. Douglas McGregor, working alongside him, popularized a lot of this too.
The reason I was reading all this — and also the reason it ended up getting cut — is that I think “meaning” is a very slippery term. When someone says they lack meaning in their life, that can usually be broken down into something more concrete. Often it means they’re lonely, or that they lack a sense of accomplishment. That can be broken down further — sometimes it means they feel their work isn’t sufficiently respected by their peers, sometimes it means they don’t feel useful, sometimes it means they’re not living up to an assigned social role, like being a provider.
The idea of “work as a source of meaning” implies there’s a single, interchangeable thing called meaning — that if you’re not getting it from your job, you can get it from something else. I don’t think that’s how this cluster of psychological needs actually works, at least in modern American society. Many men with rich, fulfilling family lives but who are unemployed would still feel a lack of meaning, because it’s culturally significant for them to be able to provide. And there are plenty of people with good jobs they find useful and meaningful who are still unhappy because they don’t have a romantic partner.
Angela: In the essay, what you arrive at is that you’re not really talking about meaning. You’re talking about dignity.
Clara: I want to explain why I think this is more than a semantic distinction. The idea that work specifically is a source of meaning is a very modern one. That’s why I was reading 1950s organizational psychologists, and also why I was reading about the history of the American work ethic in the 19th century. So whenever someone asks, “What will we do without work as a source of meaning?” — the answer is: probably the same things almost every human society before about 1800 did, because that concept simply didn’t exist for them.
Angela: This essay was compelling to me because one of the arguments is that we already know what a world without work looks like, even though people often claim that we don’t. Why is it so hard for people to reach for those historical examples?
Clara: It depends who we’re talking about. A lot of post-work philosophers come out of a socialist tradition, so they’re not looking to the past — it’s more deeply ingrained in their worldview that there will be a transformative change in human economic relations that creates something incommensurable with what came before. I disagree with that, but I understand the motivation.
People coming at this more from an “AI will obviate all work” angle tend to be less interested in history, or less familiar with it, or don’t see it as relevant.
I also hear the argument that so much will change about the material conditions of society that anything from the past is a bad analogy. That’s coherent, but I still think historical precedent is better than flying blind, and that these relational patterns have persisted across enormous technological changes throughout human history. I don’t think there’s nothing to learn from patterns that recur across wildly different societies and contexts.
Angela: Yeah. We’re still just apes. We’re still chimpanzees, in a way, with the same drives around belonging and status.
Clara: Right — that goes back to what I was saying before I got sidetracked. This whole essay has been an exercise in tangent-taming.
The reason I think dignity and meaning are meaningfully distinct is that meaning is a fuzzy phrase that collapses into a lot of different things. The idea that meaning in the sense of personal self-fulfillment comes from work, or even that it matters at all, is pretty contingent.. What isn’t historically contingent — what every human culture has valued — is having an understanding of what you have to do to be a successful, functioning member of your society and to earn the respect of your peers.
Angela: Is that about status? Is that the word we’re dancing around?
Clara: I actually thought about using the word status for this and decided not to because status is positional, and I don’t think dignity is necessarily positional. Status would be things like: do you have the best job, the biggest house, the most attractive spouse, the most money, the highest-achieving kids?
But there are many paths to dignity in our society that don’t require winning on any of those axes. Some important determinants of dignity in modern American society are things like having a steady job, owning a house, being married, being a parent. Not that you need all of these to be dignified — but if you asked a random parent whether their child had a successful life, those are the boxes they’d check.
I don’t think that’s universal — there are plenty of societies where dignity and status are much more closely tied, and dignity is much more positional. Our society, compared to the broad sweep of human cultures — at least post-agriculture — is actually fairly egalitarian on this front.
Visions from the relational world
Angela: So, the “meaning” material collapsed into too many things and you set it aside in favor of the relational material. For that, most of the piece is really built on the ethnographies you were reading. Walk me through how you chose which ones. I know you started with court memoirs.
Clara: That’s right — I knew from the beginning that the memoirs of the Duc de Saint-Simon would be the starting point. If we’re thinking about a post-work society, aristocracies are a natural place to look. They’re vast and varied — military aristocracies, governing aristocracies — but broadly, there are plenty of historical examples of societies where people are mostly wealthy, and to the extent they’re not, they don’t meet their material needs through productive labor. 
They meet them by managing their network of social relationships, and they actively disdain the idea of paid work. But they still do things. So why do they do what they do? How do they spend their time? That felt directly relevant to a world where our material needs are mostly met, and to the extent they’re not, we meet them by leveraging our social networks.
Angela: Then we had this whole discussion about salons, and how people are like, “we’ll just all be talking to Voltaire all the time.” But it seems like that wasn’t the case.
Clara: This is a contested area. The historian Antoine Lilti is responding to earlier historians  who wrote about the importance of salon culture. What Lilti argues is that the ideal of the salon is largely fictional — based mostly on accounts written after the French Revolution by people romanticizing a culture that no longer existed.
The accounts are not contemporaneous — but we do have a lot of contemporaneous material: memoirs, letters. When you look at what these conversations actually consisted of, there wasn’t much high-minded philosophical discussion. Voltaire and Diderot really were invited to these gatherings, and they were influential spaces. But that doesn’t mean the salons were primarily about expanding the aristocrats’ minds. It was more of a status marker. The actual bulk of the conversation was political gossip — people talking about themselves.
This is a pattern you see again and again: bourgeois people copying elements of aristocratic culture, but with a much rosier view of it, so they end up far more invested in performing intellectualism — which wasn’t really something true aristocrats typically care about. Of course there are exceptions, but it’s not the norm.
Angela: The next book you cover is Susan Ostrander’s Women of the Upper Class, from 1984. Did you know going in you’d use this book, or did you find it along the way?
Clara:. One thing I struggled with throughout is that there are countless case studies I could have used. It was hard to narrow down. But I felt that the strongest counterargument to using the aristocratic memoirs is that it’s a very particular world — not just that they don’t work, but that they’re living under a kind of enlightened absolutism, with particular economic, social, and political conditions which aren’t even typical of most aristocracies, let alone anything that exists today. If we simply removed work from our modern American lives, the situation would look very different. So I wanted to find something that asked: what if we removed work specifically from modern American lives? That’s how I found Ostrander.
Total American cultural victory
Angela: We’re both independently reading various ethnographies, and it turns out ours cite each other. I didn’t read Ostrander, but I read  [ https://substack.com/redirect/897f2e9b-03e5-4ba7-9335-66f97013cfee?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ]Uneasy Street [ https://substack.com/redirect/897f2e9b-03e5-4ba7-9335-66f97013cfee?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ], which is fieldwork from 2009ish on rich New Yorkers. One of the biggest differences is how concerned these New Yorkers are with staying proximate to work. If they’re not working, they’ll talk about how they used to, or frame something like running the household as labor, even though it’s uncompensated. That’s not something you see in Ostrander at all.
Clara: Not at all. And it’s more than just New York. I was talking with a friend who’s a philanthropic consultant for very wealthy clients, and she told me that among the global ultra-rich today, everyone wants either a vanity job or to emphasize that something like motherhood is real work — which is, frankly, a total American cultural victory. De Tocqueville wrote about this back in 1831! Rich Americans are unlike rich Europeans in that they’re really, really anxious about the idea of being idle. They all have this compulsion to do something work-like. But of course he’s talking about men.
Angela: What do you make of the shift between 1985 and 2009?
Clara: I think several things are happening. Partly it’s the social changes of the ‘60s still working their way through American society — the women’s movement especially. In Ostrander’s sample, the women aren’t really in a work-free society in a deep sense — their husbands all work, some of them manage the family money but still go into an office to do it, while the women, with just one exception, don’t work at all. They grew up in an America where women not working was completely normal and not seen as something to overcome. 
By the time you reach the younger women in that sample, some say things like, “I kind of regret not having a paid job” — they won’t frame what they’re doing as proximate to work. Those women mark the end of a generation where women not working was totally socially unmarked in America. So this anxiety about not working has always existed in the American upper classes, but now it’s spread to the women.
Angela: And in Uneasy Street, nobody likes talking about money — people are embarrassed by how much they have and don’t want to disclose it. Was money discussed openly in Ostrander’s book?
Clara: No dollar amounts are mentioned, but there isn’t the same sense of embarrassment — I think because this is a culture with a still-intact sense of noblesse oblige. This is actually another thing I think the women’s movement changed. 
Ostrander’s central argument is that the women’s volunteering and socializing was fulfilling an important role in legitimizing their wealth and status — constructing a sense that it’s good and normal that they have these advantages, because they fulfill an important role in the community. As more women entered paid work, they stopped fulfilling that legitimating function. This isn’t necessarily about whether other people see their wealth as legitimate, it’s how people of this class think about themselves.
In a way, the past few decades have proven Ostrander’s thesis — once women stopped doing that legitimating work, it fundamentally changed how that entire class relates to and justifies its position.
Angela: Ostrander’s book is set in Cleveland, while Uneasy Street is very New York — the Hamptons, Groton. What differences do you see between New York and Bay Area wealth?
Clara: It’s very different — in SF, almost none of the wealth is inherited, and there’s been much less time for these justifying mechanisms to develop. Even the “old money” in Ostrander’s book isn’t that old in objective terms — a few generations, maybe a hundred years — so these aren’t defenses passed down from time immemorial. It’s a set of behaviors and justifications that accreted as circumstances arose.
People in SF, I think, feel less need to justify wealth generally, but there’s a very strong philanthropic drive. I think that comes less from a need to justify being wealthy and more from an extension of having goals in the world and wanting to apply resources toward them.
Angela: One reason you wrote about this was to push back on the idea that if none of us had to work for money, we’d all just become painters — and instead you show these women doing volunteer busy-work, not painting. I’m curious: in SF specifically, it does seem like a lot of people have side projects alongside their day jobs. Is there a sense that, for this particular group, the “painter” outcome might actually hold true — even if it doesn’t generalize?
Clara: I’ve had many conversations about this. There are definitely people for whom it’s true — if they didn’t have to work, they’d pursue their passion projects. I think they’re overrepresented in this conversation, which leads them to misjudge how most people would actually react to that situation. We have plenty of examples — not just among wealthy people, but across the economic spectrum — of people who don’t handle unstructured freedom well.
The Gulf states are a good example, and some Pacific Islands that had mineral discoveries — places where, because of some natural windfall, citizens don’t have to work, and governments end up inventing busy-work jobs for them. I think self-actualization is genuinely difficult, and one thing I’ve come to believe fairly strongly from this reading is that we don’t have great tools for encouraging self-actualization at a societal level. 
Broadly distributed material prosperity certainly makes things easier to follow your dreams, but even among the rich, wanting to orient your life around some inner passion is quite rare. I don’t think we’ve ever figured out how to get more than a tiny minority of people to even want to self-actualize in this way, and I don’t think it’s a very useful goal for social planning.
Angela: Later in the essay, you quote Claudia Strauss and discuss surveys and ethnographies about how people actually feel about work, even work they don’t love. Did anything in that research surprise you?
Clara: I should say up front — I know I’m lucky in my own job. It’s intellectually engaging, not too physically demanding, and I have a lot of autonomy. In many ways it’s a classically “good” job, and I understand that not all jobs are good — many are genuinely miserable, and plenty of people would be individually happier without their particular job.
That said, I talk to a lot of people in my position who assume the world is divided into people with good jobs and people who are miserable. That’s not the case. Survey and ethnographic evidence is fairly consistent, at least here in the U.S.: most Americans like working — even people in classically boring jobs generally prefer going to work over not going. That’s an important thing to understand about how people would actually react to not having to work.
I do want to add some balance here. I truly believe that the Court of Versailles was dystopian for everyone involved. But most relational groups aren’t dystopian. I don’t think it’s simply a terrible way to live. But I do think that way of living is deeply limiting, with real costs — especially for people who don’t fit that mold well.
