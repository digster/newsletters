China starts to think about RSI risksChinese AI companies can push RSI ahead without a settled term for it. However, policymakers cannot. To intervene, regulators need to know what exactly to intervene on. How RSI enters China’s policy and public debate can therefore signal whether and when China might regulate it, and whether the U.S. and China understand it similarly enough to talk about it. Policy implicationsOur findings suggest that RSI in China, so far, looks less like a state-led geopolitical race than a bottom-up, commercially driven trend. First, Chinese companies do not even share a language for it. They picked up “RSI” only recently, largely following discussions in Silicon Valley, and still use the term to mean different things. Second, one visible driver is commercial: RSI helps with fundraising, aims at easing compute constraints in the future, and shows investors efficiency gains.. Third, RSI barely features in policy. Its only appearance in an official document treats it as a trend to watch with “vigilance,” and prominent voices in party-affiliated media emphasize keeping it under human control. Fourth, Chinese companies already disclose some details about how they use AI in research, which gives outside observers more to work with than the race narrative suggests. The public evidence does not support the claim that China is racing to reach RSI first. Commercial incentives and competitive market dynamics can encourage Chinese AI companies to double down on research automation without explicit policy instruction or geopolitical racing dynamics. Therefore, regardless of geopolitical calculations, the world must prepare for a scenario in which automation of AI research happens more aggressively in leading AI labs in both countries. One should therefore focus on understanding the capabilities being developed in leading labs in both countries. Before pursuing ambitious international agreements, researchers, companies, and policymakers could take two basic but necessary steps. First, scientists and AI researchers from both countries could develop a shared vocabulary that distinguishes among the automation of individual research tasks, closed feedback loops, and systems that produce self-sustaining acceleration in capabilities. Exchanges could help establish what each side means by terms such as “automating AI research,” “self-evolution,” and “recursive self-improvement.” Using these terms to describe very different processes obscures the risks involved and makes it harder to identify appropriate safeguards. Second, companies could adopt common transparency standards. Reports should specify which research tasks AI performs, how its performance is evaluated, how often humans intervene, how much AI accelerates different parts of and overall AI research, what decisions remain under human control, and what safeguards prevent systems from altering their own records or authorizing their own deployment. Chinese and American companies have already disclosed some information about their use of AI in research, which provides a limited foundation for more systematic reporting. Independent evaluations and tamper-resistant logs would make such disclosures more credible and comparable. U.S. labs could build on what Chinese companies already do by publishing clearer, more detailed accounts of how they automate research. Chinese companies, in turn, could go beyond stating that humans remain in the loop and specify the safeguards actually in place to govern increasingly autonomous systems. Greater transparency about AI R&D automation would give researchers on both sides a concrete basis for engagement. This does not require unrestricted disclosure of sensitive research, or either side adopting the other’s standards wholesale. Converging on a common reporting baseline would support information exchange and help both sides assess emerging capabilities and risks without relying on vague claims or geopolitical speculation.
