“The United States is racing forward broadly, in part because it is worried about what happens if China gets to self-improving AI first ” — Ezra Klein, The New York Times [ https://substack.com/redirect/602d0bd1-bc77-4d3d-9d77-8eeeceabd88d?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ]
Recursive self-improvement, broadly understood as an AI system iteratively designing an increasingly capable successor without much extra input from humans, is widely regarded as a focal point in the U.S.-China AI competition. As Matt Sheehan from the Carnegie Endowment has argued [ https://substack.com/redirect/f844e6ad-d18d-4979-aff3-4ce729a759f1?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ],  parts of the U.S. research community see RSI as a way to lock in a lasting geopolitical lead.
China has not turned a blind eye. The Chinese government picked up the term in its recent top policy document, “AI Safety Governance Framework 3.0 [ https://substack.com/redirect/02b22e43-bb60-48c6-9bff-70170f560be8?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ],” and almost all Chinese AI companies have become increasingly vocal about RSI and related ideas this year.
It seems, thus, that RSI is indeed the goal that both sides are racing towards. But the reality is not so simple. For one, we don’t understand what Chinese companies mean when they talk about AI research automation or RSI — even as more and more of them adopt the term.
Chinese companies already disclose a good deal about how they use AI in their research, sometimes in more detail than U.S. labs. Based on this analysis, we argue that RSI in China, so far, looks less like a state-led race than a bottom-up, commercial trend. We suggest that the U.S. and China AI researchers should start by building a shared vocabulary and comparable transparency about how AI is used to build AI.
The many faces of RSI in China
RSI is a confusing term even in Silicon Valley, where it is used to describe everything from AI automating parts of AI research to an uncontrollable intelligence explosion. Similarly, it means many things in China — but not necessarily the same ones.  Just last year, Chinese companies discussed related ideas using varied phrases  such as “self-evolution (自我进化/自进化),” “self-iteration (自我迭代),” and “self-training (自我训练).” As the table below shows, pulling from releases from firms, even the same firm does not always use the same term.
The term RSI (递归式自我改进 [ https://substack.com/redirect/b9362d70-b2a9-4c09-be58-e953c71b4617?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ]) has only entered Chinese companies’ vocabulary in the past few months. The shift is evident at companies like Alibaba. In 2025 [ https://substack.com/redirect/df272078-b33b-4e74-890e-b64d6960a3cc?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ],  CEO Eddie Wu said AI would achieve “self-learning” (自主学习). It would be able to build its own training infrastructure, optimize data pipelines and upgrade model architectures, which would eventually lead to a singularity: “Once a certain singularity is crossed, human society will be as if it pressed the accelerate button, and the speed of technological progress will exceed our imagination.” He did not use the term RSI. 
In September 2026 [ https://substack.com/redirect/b9362d70-b2a9-4c09-be58-e953c71b4617?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ], he did use the word, defining it as the point when “the model discovers its shortcomings from real-world feedback, designs experiments, constructs data, and evaluates results, thus driving its own evolution in a continuous cycle.” The new definition focuses on the feedback loop and does not emphasize the acceleration of progress, which is a narrower but also more concrete ambition than his 2025 vision.
Z.ai founder Tang Jie’s wording followed a similar path. In a May 2026 post on X, he called “self-evolution” a “promising path.” In July [ https://substack.com/redirect/ca507674-1a18-46bb-9aae-29537c72322b?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ], he described “self-evolving” (自我进化) as “AI training AI” with models writing their own code, cleaning and synthesizing their own data, and training themselves. In a September interview with Qiushi [ https://substack.com/redirect/121eb983-efde-4949-93b1-f08cff4df5ee?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ], the Party’s flagship theory journal, he picked up the term RSI, though he called it “recursive super intelligence,” likely mixing up what the acronym stands for. He argued that if models could continuously improve and correct themselves, they might soon reach a singularity and surpass human intelligence, though they could also plateau.
The same thing happened for Xiaomi. In March 2026, Luo Fuli, head of Xiaomi’s MiMo models, named [ https://substack.com/redirect/1ca1fd8c-26e7-48f0-bd89-8a9150badf07?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] “self-evolution” (自进化) the most important development for AGI in the coming year. She described it as an agent framework with verifiable constraints and a loop that keeps the model iterating toward a goal. Xiaomi’s later MiMo-V2.6 technical report instead adopts [ https://substack.com/redirect/2599612d-45d7-4f65-8511-29179fed7ab2?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] “recursive self-improvement (RSI),” defined as models that “expand their capabilities through sustained exploration and feedback.”
Note: Each marker is one saved company document (release note, blog post, paper, model card, filing, or a founder or executive statement reported by media) that contains the term, placed at its publication date; Chinese and English spellings are pooled except for RSI, which is split by language. Corpora cover each firm’s model-lab channels from July 1, 2025 to October 10, 2026 (Alibaba 286 documents, ByteDance 249, Baidu 226, MiniMax 183, Xiaomi 164, DeepSeek 122, Z.ai 105, Moonshot 102).
Across all three examples, the idea of AI improving AI took shape before the terminology of RSI being adopted. Chinese companies have only been using “RSI” since July 2026. But what Chinese AI companies talk about regarding RSI does not appear too different from what they talk about regarding self-evolution or self-learning. Using AI for AI research is thus a long-standing practice with many loosely defined and often interchangeable labels, of which “RSI” is the newest.
Unlike in Silicon Valley, where the two concepts are closely linked, the adoption of “RSI” in China has not brought about much talk of an intelligence explosion. Chinese AI leaders occasionally mention a singularity, as Wu did in 2025’s speech and Tang in his Qiushi interview. However, when they describe where self-improving AI leads, they mostly point to real-world applications. Tang describes [ https://substack.com/redirect/88adf6b4-fb02-45dc-b937-aedca7f3b338?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] a future in which “every sector — security, finance, law, e-commerce — will be reshaped.” Wu envisioned [ https://substack.com/redirect/df272078-b33b-4e74-890e-b64d6960a3cc?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] that artificial superintelligence “may create a batch of ‘super scientists’ and ‘full-stack super engineers’” to address “medical challenges, new materials, sustainable energy and climate problems.” DeepSeek’s Liang Wenfeng imagines [ https://substack.com/redirect/b8776952-0321-4922-b6e9-f5f4f6e3e2e7?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] AI after the singularity to be embodied and “walk into the real world, can do your housework, can care for you in old age.”
Chinese AI companies can push RSI ahead without a settled term for it. However, policymakers cannot.
Why do Chinese companies mention RSI?
In September 2026, a five-hour Saturday salon [ https://substack.com/redirect/d65cf03d-b35e-41e9-add5-05a4d21dee27?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] in Shanghai gathered researchers, well-known investors, financial advisers, and incubators to discuss RSI. It was organized by HongShan, formerly Sequoia Capital China, one of the country’s most influential venture capital firms. Key investors have started treating RSI as a theme worth organizing around. In various reports [ https://substack.com/redirect/aa16460f-dc63-4922-a03c-27873f357bb1?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] surrounding this event, RSI is described as the field “VCs are chasing.”
Investor appetite may partially explain why Chinese AI companies started using the term. In June 2026, Anthropic published “When AI Builds Itself [ https://substack.com/redirect/af05370d-ddc0-4ac2-b0ed-132dceb02d88?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ],” an essay that received heavy media coverage in China. Within a month, Chinese companies began adopting the label. One plausible explanation is commercial. Having seen RSI attract attention and capital in the U.S., Chinese companies may see the concept as a way to raise money themselves.
Indeed, new Chinese startups built around AI research automation, some led by founders with close ties to the U.S. AI research community, have raised substantial funding in the second half of 2026. SuperDen Intelligence (超衍智能), founded by an assistant professor at Tsinghua in June 2026,  raised a total of nearly 400 million RMB (60 million USD) in their angel and angel+ round in September. The founder, who worked at Google DeepMind and Microsoft, said [ https://substack.com/redirect/164fb043-1e46-4b70-81cf-f1bae07ed0b2?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] that he first had  the idea of auto-research at GDM. Similarly, MetaCircle (元环智能), founded in May 2026, raised [ https://substack.com/redirect/4f573454-088b-4e99-a4e9-58a801b046eb?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] hundreds of millions RMB in their seed round in the past two months. The founder is also an assistant professor at Tsinghua, best known for the KAN neural-network architecture [ https://substack.com/redirect/4303c93d-c228-463e-8a66-c55e3259e0ae?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ], which he developed during his MIT PhD under Max Tegmark.
Z.ai’s recent fundraising shows how RSI as a label may help with raising capital. On September 13, the company announced about USD $5 billion in new financing. Three days later, it told analysts that about 60% of the net proceeds would go to next-generation GLM models and its “Fully Self Training” system. The next day, it published its blog post “Toward Recursive Self-Improvement,” which Chinese tech media reported [ https://substack.com/redirect/5c33c5f7-f7f6-40c7-877a-a892861cf551?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] as “Zhipu’s first RSI result.” 
STAR Market Daily, an authoritative financial news outlet specializing in China’s sci-tech capital markets, covered [ https://substack.com/redirect/23f6ae9b-3ff8-4cea-ac63-f90fd348df7d?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] the sequence under the headline “Zhipu bets on the RSI narrative after intensive fundraising.” While the timeline itself does not prove that the company intentionally raised RSI for the sake of fundraising, it suggests the RSI framing was aimed at least partly at investors.
While RSI may serve partly as a marketing strategy, this does not mean Chinese companies are not serious about using AI for AI research. The work is useful in its own right, especially under compute constraints. Investors increasingly care about the compute cost of developing and serving models, and reliance on foreign chips can raise compute costs and add supply risk. Chinese business media reports [ https://substack.com/redirect/c2cd0102-a161-4e85-8e1b-f7d1084e4583?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] suggest that investors examine downstream AI application companies’ “computing power procurement structure,” and projects “that rely entirely on overseas high-end A/H100 series chips” may see their valuations “significantly discounted,” or may not even make it to due diligence.
Automating AI research could help relax this bottleneck. Chinese companies have applied AI to infrastructure and kernel work, helping their models run more efficiently on Chinese-made chips. This gives them a reason to keep automating AI research, whatever they call it, and could make them more attractive to investors, even if the research itself needs more compute in the short term.
Overall, it is still hard to distinguish whether Chinese AI companies genuinely believe in RSI, or are just using the term to fundraise. In many cases, one could both genuinely believe in RSI and want to cash in on it — after all,  money is necessary to train frontier models, and Chinese companies receive much less investment and revenue than their U.S. counterparts.
China starts to think about RSI risks
Chinese AI companies can push RSI ahead without a settled term for it. However, policymakers cannot. To intervene, regulators need to know what exactly to intervene on. How RSI enters China’s policy and public debate can therefore signal whether and when China might regulate it, and whether the U.S. and China understand it similarly enough to talk about it.
RSI first appeared in a policy document in September 2026, when TC260, China’s main standard-making body on cyber and AI, released AI Safety Governance Framework 3.0 [ https://substack.com/redirect/02b22e43-bb60-48c6-9bff-70170f560be8?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ], the most comprehensive official Chinese taxonomy of AI safety risks. Its introduction mentions RSI with a slightly altered literal translation, “自我递归提升” (self-recursive improvement), accompanied by a full mention of “recursive self-improvement” in the official English translation.
Prominently noted in the beginning, however, is that RSI is a future trend to observe rather than an urgent risk to tackle: “More profoundly, AI has demonstrated a self-accelerating trend of model and algorithm autonomous learning, optimization, and recursive self-improvement. Whether the speed and direction of technological evolution may exceed human anticipation and control demands attention and vigilance.” The rest of the document does not come back to RSI when discussing AI safety risks.
While the term has only appeared in one policy document, public discussion around RSI has picked up. In China, emerging discourse —  driven by governance or technical researchers and state-affiliated media — now covers warnings of RSI risks linked to ASI (超级人工智能), recommendations on related regulations, and technical research, but with an emphasis on keeping AI progress safe rather than preventing it completely.
On September 30, 2026, Study Times (学习时报), the official newspaper of the party’s highest internal training institute, published [ https://substack.com/redirect/b67bfc7c-7bbe-4a0d-a836-76fe62f956f0?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] an article by AI governance professor Zeng Yi. The article describes RSI as “a key path to AGI” and “the main trigger point for loss-of-control risk”. It argues that once AI can improve its own architecture, “the positive cycle of intelligence gains could accelerate to the point where humans cannot keep up at all.” While the article warned against the risk of getting humans out of the AI research loop, it equally stressed that one cannot “stop eating for the fear of choking (因噎废食)”. Abandoning the research is not an option, but rather “self-improvement proposals and substantive architectural changes” so that “humans hold final control under any circumstances.”
The public evidence does not support the claim that China is racing to reach RSI first.
An op-ed in the semi-state-linked Economic Observer (经济观察报) went further, proposing [ https://substack.com/redirect/dc6150e9-bca6-4fc4-a574-aef8a0b3a574?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] a detailed framework for governing RSI. It sorts AI into five levels of “recursive capability,” from ordinary research assistance to systems that train and deploy their own successors, with obligations that scale accordingly. The lowest level needs no extra rules beyond existing product-safety and data-protection law, while the highest should never run “without external oversight” and should trigger “international notification.” It describes the danger of superhuman capabilities brought by RSI spanning cyber and military domains. It also proposes four categories — capabilities, resources, evaluation and deployment — and asks that no single recursive system holds research, evaluation, resource and deployment rights at once.
Beyond policy proposals, technical research on RSI safety has also started to appear in academic discourse. A group of researchers from leading Chinese universities and research institutes measured [ https://substack.com/redirect/1e74b2cb-a8db-4aa5-94f7-69ab8bf13a00?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] how much of AI research AI already does. They logged 769 tasks from the development of its own agentic research model and found that AI was involved in 96.5% of tasks and proposed most methods, but humans made the final choice in 85.5% of method decisions. 
These Chinese researchers also argued that authority over human safety needs to be handled seriously, and each advance in AI capability should be matched by investment in alignment and oversight, with clear rules on whose goals AI pursues and who remains accountable. Similar work is multiplying: The Chinese Academy of Sciences has proposed [ https://substack.com/redirect/0aa6e36a-51c2-43bf-9b2b-b06b7732a482?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] studying the “evolutionary safety” of recursively self-improving AI; Shanghai AI Lab has explored [ https://substack.com/redirect/323be008-22bf-422e-8ce9-485513c6c536?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] using RSI itself to improve safety (“recursive safety improvement”) and documented [ https://substack.com/redirect/d2194350-a244-4107-a782-caf6c7da5a70?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] how self-evolving agents “misevolve.”
Overall, the discussion of RSI and its safety in China resembles a bottom-up effort. AI companies, AI governance scholars, and technical safety researchers talk about it most often, while top-level policy is gradually catching up as the topic becomes more prominent in AI discourse. There is little public evidence that the top leadership treats RSI as a priority. But the broader AI community, from AI companies to AI governance scholars and research communities, is becoming more vocal about it, which may inform and influence thinking at the top.
Chinese AI companies disclose details about AI R&D automation
Thinking about RSI risks in China is still at an early stage. However, one basis for governing RSI is already partly in place: disclosure. People need to know what AI is actually doing inside AI labs to set rules. Chinese AI companies have already started to publish about where and how they use AI in their research.
Firstly, they disclose where they apply AI in the research pipeline in order to showcase their models’ automated research capabilities. MiniMax reports [ https://substack.com/redirect/321f8ef9-e9fe-4bd6-9540-df01b895fc76?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] that M2.7 is capable of constructing complex Agent Harness on its own. It has also described agents that “help with literature review, track a pre-set experiment spec, pipeline data and other artifacts, and launch experiments” in an RL scenario. 
Xiaomi introduced CodeMidas [ https://substack.com/redirect/e9a1cd98-4361-45b1-b7e8-b61013aaa391?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ], “an agentic pipeline that turns implemented functionality in existing codebases into executable RL environments using source code as its only task-specific input.” It resulted in “5,545 training tasks from 3,185 open-source codebases spanning 23 programming languages and 15 technical domains.” Z.ai’s “Toward Recursive Self-Improvement [ https://substack.com/redirect/c8e87f2a-2b73-41f0-995d-f5355f020cfc?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ]” blog post states that its GLM-5.3-powered Infra Agent helped build the inference system that serves GLM-5.3-Flash on more than 100,000 Chinese-made accelerators, chips designed for AI computation.

They also describe how the AI did the work and what it achieved. According to MiniMax’s announcement in March 2026, MiniMax’s M2.7 ran a loop of more than 100 rounds on an internal harness and reported a 30% gain on an internal evaluation set. In this process, M2.7 studied failed attempts, planned changes, edited the harness code, reran the evaluation, and made decisions on whether to keep the change. Moonshot’s Kimi K3 report [ https://substack.com/redirect/ece44edc-4e81-4975-bf6a-7576cbe80abb?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] (July 2026, revised August) says agents built its training tasks and the starting workspaces for its personal-assistant tasks, and that earlier Kimi models wrote its fine-tuning data. 
Z.ai’s blog post in September 2026, “Toward Recursive Self-Improvement [ https://substack.com/redirect/c8e87f2a-2b73-41f0-995d-f5355f020cfc?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ],” mentions the division of labor during building infrastructure: “Engineers defined objectives and system boundaries. The agent handled analysis, hypotheses, and code changes. The experimental environment provided layered, timely, and verifiable feedback.” Z.ai says the work, much of which it credits to the agent, tripled end-to-end throughput relative to the initial baseline.
By contrast, OpenAI and Anthropic mostly publish aggregate usage statistics, often oriented toward risk assessment. Anthropic says Claude wrote more than 80% of the code it merged (May 2026), and “leads” 26% of its AI R&D work (August 2026). OpenAI does break agents’ output down by research task, but only as token counts.
Of course, OpenAI and Anthropic also sometimes report more details. For example, OpenAI [ https://substack.com/redirect/33074893-8d90-4929-a982-dce56822adef?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ]’s blog post introducing GPT-5.5 this April says Codex analyzed weeks of production traffic and wrote new load-balancing rules for serving, which made token generation over 20% faster. Anthropic [ https://substack.com/redirect/573d07eb-ef97-45d8-88b4-10c35961474d?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] has mentioned that in an experiment, Claude Sonnet 5 spent 60 hours trying to fix alignment failures in an early checkpoint of Claude Opus 4.8 by creating training data.
But overall, in our assessment, Chinese AI companies seem to be a bit more detailed about AI R&D automation applications. Part of this contrast comes from the fact that Chinese AI companies are more open and thus publish more technical reports reporting the AI’s process step by step.
Policy implications
Our findings suggest that RSI in China, so far, looks less like a state-led geopolitical race than a bottom-up, commercially driven trend. 
First, Chinese companies do not even share a language for it. They picked up “RSI” only recently, largely following discussions in Silicon Valley, and still use the term to mean different things. Second, one visible driver is commercial: RSI helps with fundraising, aims at easing compute constraints in the future, and shows investors efficiency gains.. Third, RSI barely features in policy. Its only appearance in an official document treats it as a trend to watch with “vigilance,” and prominent voices in party-affiliated media emphasize keeping it under human control. Fourth, Chinese companies already disclose some details about how they use AI in research, which gives outside observers more to work with than the race narrative suggests.
The public evidence does not support the claim that China is racing to reach RSI first. Commercial incentives and competitive market dynamics can encourage Chinese AI companies to double down on research automation without explicit policy instruction or geopolitical racing dynamics. Therefore, regardless of geopolitical calculations, the world must prepare for a scenario in which automation of AI research happens more aggressively in leading AI labs in both countries.
One should therefore focus on understanding the capabilities being developed in leading labs in both countries. Before pursuing ambitious international agreements, researchers, companies, and policymakers could take two basic but necessary steps.
First, scientists and AI researchers from both countries could develop a shared vocabulary that distinguishes among the automation of individual research tasks, closed feedback loops, and systems that produce self-sustaining acceleration in capabilities. Exchanges could help establish what each side means by terms such as “automating AI research,” “self-evolution,” and “recursive self-improvement.” Using these terms to describe very different processes obscures the risks involved and makes it harder to identify appropriate safeguards.
Second, companies could adopt common transparency standards. Reports should specify which research tasks AI performs, how its performance is evaluated, how often humans intervene, how much AI accelerates different parts of and overall AI research, what decisions remain under human control, and what safeguards prevent systems from altering their own records or authorizing their own deployment. Chinese and American companies have already disclosed some information about their use of AI in research, which provides a limited foundation for more systematic reporting. Independent evaluations and tamper-resistant logs would make such disclosures more credible and comparable.
U.S. labs could build on what Chinese companies already do by publishing clearer, more detailed accounts of how they automate research. Chinese companies, in turn, could go beyond stating that humans remain in the loop and specify the safeguards actually in place to govern increasingly autonomous systems. Greater transparency about AI R&D automation would give researchers on both sides a concrete basis for engagement. This does not require unrestricted disclosure of sensitive research, or either side adopting the other’s standards wholesale. Converging on a common reporting baseline would support information exchange and help both sides assess emerging capabilities and risks without relying on vague claims or geopolitical speculation.
