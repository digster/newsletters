Why did Stu Woo of the Wall Street Journal Follow Me to Singapore?

Thirty five million people change countries every year. I'm one of them, and so is Stu Woo.

Jul 28

A writer named Stu Woo recently moved from San Francisco to Singapore. He got in touch to ask me why I’d also moved from San Francisco to Singapore, and why I’d decided to become a proud Singaporean. We talked about the reasons he and others had moved away from America — the old woman violently shoved out of a Muni bus, the gunshots and molotovs at tech CEO houses, and the simultaneous rise of Hasan Piker’s anti-capitalist far left alongside Nick Fuentes’ anti-immigrant far right. I also mentioned all the positive things that had pulled me towards Singapore, my many posts admiring the country, and my public announcement years ago that I’d moved to Asia.

I am now a citizen of Singapore, a country where my mother tongue of Tamil is a national language, where I can worship at a Hindu temple without insane people, denouncing my religion, and where Indian-Singaporeans are full citizens rather than subject to endless racial obloquy. Fundamentally, I voted with my feet for Lee Kuan Yew’s vision of tolerant meritocracy, rather than Kodos or Kang.

As we spoke, Stu Woo volunteered that he personally had no imminent plans to return to America, and indeed might never return. He further acknowledged that millions of centrist Americans had made the same difficult decision to go into diaspora, just like countless liberal Russians, capitalist Chinese, and secular Iranians…because they loved their people, but not their governments.

But — and you might see where this is going — Stu Woo didn’t think it was relevant to include his own decision to go into diaspora, or that of millions of other Americans, in his upcoming Wall Street Journal hit piece on me. The thrust of his questioning, as you’ll see, was to make me look like an odd duck who’d done something weird…as opposed to an early adopter that Stu had literally followed to Singapore. I’ve redacted some of the personal info that Rupert Murdoch’s employees were planning to use in their corporate doxxing attempt, but see the red boxed outline below:

Yes, I actually do think it’s crucially important to elaborate on the fact that the America I loved has declined to the point that millions of Americans are forced to seek new countries, as reported at his own outlet...because it completely defeats their attempt to shame me for moving to another country, as Stu himself did!

So, let’s first go through some lesser-known context on why mass migration and its discontents is the story of our age. We’ll cover (a) the idea of moving faster and escaping things, (b) how the right to exit is the true right to vote, and why (c) every emigrant is seeking a new country. With that context, you’ll understand (d) my response to Stu Woo and the Wall Street Journal.

Move Faster, Escape Things

Millions of Americans have moved from blue states to red. Millions more are moving abroad to places ranging from Canada to El Salvador to Europe to Asia. Millionaires are being forced out of New York, while billionaires are being pushed out of California and Washington. We are talking about situations that have become so intolerable that some of the most capable people in the world — the founders of Google, Facebook, Amazon, Uber, Oracle, Citadel, and SpaceX — had to move from their long-established homes, on short notice, under political pressure. And now that the billionaires have been forced to choose between expropriation and emigration, the “mere” 50-millionaires are being targeted, and soon predictably onward down the income ladder.

The same phenomenon is visible globally, with Russians/Ukrainians/Arabs/Iranians fleeing war, Chinese youth leaving techno-communism, Indians seeking opportunity abroad, and Brits moving to Dubai. The list just goes on and on. Quantitatively, we’ve gone from ~10M migrants per year in 2000 to 35M+ per year by 2025. And due to digital nomadism and internet-accelerated mobility, this figure is only set to rise.

At the same time, a nationalist/socialist backlash against both those coming (the “invaders”) and those leaving (the “traitors”) is rising, both inside the West and abroad. Qualitatively, politicians are exploiting the Curley effect to pass taxes and laws that drive out enemy classes (particularly technologists) and enemy races (particularly immigrants). Within America, this is the common thread between Democrats and MAGA; both are doing their level best via tax policy and visa policy to establish politically homogenous states.

Democrats are de facto pursuing a one-party state (”democracy”, defined as rule by Democrats), while MAGA frames it in terms of a one-ethny state (”Real Americans”, as defined by Republicans). One can make a slightly more sympathetic case for MAGA, because they didn’t really start this, and because if Democrats take power they’ve promised to prosecute them...so perhaps MAGAs see no choice but to build one-party red states in Florida and Texas.

Regardless of the causes, though, both Democrats and Republicans are converging on the same political form as China: namely, a one-party state where elections may be held, but the party always wins. And where the only true vote is the vote with your feet.

The Right To Exit is the True Right to Vote

Just to recap: we’re at the outset of a time where nationalists and socialists will push both (a) forced expulsions and (b) exit bans of enemy classes and races, while internationalists and capitalists will seek both (c) jurisdictions where they’re welcome and (d) the right to exit jurisdictions where they feel unwelcome. The nationalists and socialists will debank, denaturalize, and demonize their perceived enemies; meanwhile, the internationalists and capitalists just need to move faster, to escape things.

Fortunately (and unfortunately), we’ve seen this movie before in the 20th century. And so we have some moral, historical, and economic arguments to bring to bear in favor of the right to exit.

Let’s start with the moral. Article 13 of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights says “Everyone has the right to leave any country, including his own.” This was so important they translated it into 80+ languages. Even made a cartoon. Why?

Because the worst regimes in history impeded the right to exit, including the Nazis, the Soviets, the East Germans, the Cubans, and the North Koreans. When those states weren’t murdering their citizens, they were essentially imprisoning them as slaves. Under Nazism, arbeit did not macht frei. And under Communism, with 100% taxation, you owned not even the teeth in your mouth, and were essentially a slave. So these regimes, the fascists and communists, did not want their slaves to escape:

Luckily, there was one country that stood against fascism and communism, for freedom, for the right to exit, for the right for each man and woman to vote with their feet. That country was the United States of America. A specific mechanism was the Jackson-Vanik amendment, which shamed and sanctioned the Soviet Union for impeding exit rights:

After all, the Soviet Union ostensibly granted everyone the right to “vote” in their faux constitution, but not the right to emigrate. That was the only way to truly vote against the communist regime.

And of course one can also make the economic case via Tiebout sorting that, in the long run, voluntary migration results in better states, because terrible states (like the USSR) lose their talent and capital to the point they eventually go bankrupt, while well-run states attract it.

Every Emigrant is Seeking a New Country

Now you have more context. In brief: 35M people per year now change their countries, including millions of Americans, and including Stu Woo and me. This number is accelerating. The right to do this, to change your citizenship, is a right that America supported with Jackson-Vanik, and that only communist and fascist states historically abrogated. Indeed, the abrogation of exit rights essentially makes you a slave.

That’s why I work on what I work on. I believe deeply in the equality of opportunity that immigration gives, the defense of liberty that emigration protects, and the practical democracy that exit rights represent.

Put another way: every emigrant is seeking a new country. Right now, there are 193 UN sovereign states to choose from, up from ~50 in 1950, an increase of roughly 2 per year:

Importantly, there were far more independent jurisdictions before 1950, which was the midpoint of the most centralizing century in history. Thus, I think it’s quite unlikely that this “8-bit world” that we’ve inherited from the 20th century, which still has less than 256 nation states, will continue indefinitely. Because just about everything else humans do has far more than 193 choices. Moreover, if we triple again, from 10M emigrants in 2000 to 35M in 2025 to 100M+ in a few years from now, it will put tremendous strain on the current system.

Among other things, America no longer wants to be the nation of emigrants. So we may need to find new countries that want to be nations of emigrants. This finally brings me to Network School, and Stu Woo.

How the Sausage is Made

Ok. Now you understand the context that Stu Woo didn’t feel relevant to include. In brief: he also moved from San Francisco to Singapore, he also agreed that San Francisco’s governance was in decline, he brought up the old ladies pushed out of Muni buses, he agreed that far left and far right were rising in America, he was also seeking a new country, he agreed that millions of Americans (and non-Americans!) are seeking new countries, and so on.

That’s important because it’s the problem statement. Whether you believe it’s feasible to start new countries or not, whether you think “193 countries ought to be enough for everyone”, step one is to agree on the problem. Without that, you might think what I’m doing is causeless and eccentric, rather than a new proposed solution to the same problem that Stu Woo and I both faced: namely, the political decline of the country we both came up in.
