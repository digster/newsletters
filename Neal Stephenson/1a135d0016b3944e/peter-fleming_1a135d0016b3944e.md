I was at a conference last week where I crossed paths with I’ve been mentioning that Owen Crisp-Upjohn, the main character in Owen Crisp-Upjohn, the point of view character in D, has a number of traits in common with Peter Fleming. I won’t lengthen this post by ticking down the whole list. The most important of these is a witty and droll prose style that’s clearly aimed at a well-educated, high-social-status readership back home—the sort of chap who can be relied upon to get the joke. I have tried to emulate that in the first part of the book, set in the early 1930s. As the story goes on, however, things become more serious, and Owen’s tone shifts accordingly. There’s a connection in this to a Substack post I made last year called There’s a catch, though, which is that the writer is making assumptions about the reader’s ability to decode such pithy sentences. Peter Fleming, educated at Eton and Oxford, and writing for a like-minded audience, could get away with it. Flipping through Brazilian Adventure, I see examples on every page: We were six days in Rio, having what is known as Trouble With the Customs. A certain amount of this was inevitable, for no attempt had been made in London to get a special permit for our elaborate and heavy armament. We easily managed, however, to make matters worse than they need have been. Once you know to look for this kind of thing, you see it everywhere in literature. Perhaps the ballsiest example is this bit from Jane Austen’s Mansfield Park: Their eager affection in meeting, their exquisite delight in being together, their hours of happy mirth and moments of serious conference may be imagined… Only Austen could get away with just telling the reader to imagine a bunch of stuff. But she knows that her readership will be up to the task. Anyway, my feeble efforts to emulate Peter Fleming’s prose style in the fictional voice of Owen Crisp-Upjohn are intended to get the reader into that frame of mind—a way of setting the scene and establishing the ambience of the 1930s.
