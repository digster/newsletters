The Uranium Cube
As recently reported in Science [ https://substack.com/redirect/9c0146d4-a2e7-4a2f-85ae-f7825ad45e01?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ], the Germans during the Second World War  attempted to build a working nuclear reactor by constructing a chandelier of uranium cubes and lowering it into a tank of heavy water. As reported in that article, after the war some of those cubes “circulated on Europe’s postwar black market" and ended up in the hands of various scientists and curiosity seekers. One of them, as it turns out, made its way to the collection at Harvard:
It is black, rather than metallic, because the outer layer is oxidized.
This is unenriched uranium—it has the same ratio of U238 to U235 found in all natural samples of the metal. I don’t know what else to say about it except that, as one would expect, it is extraordinarily dense.
The German reactor project worked on the same general principle as the more successful reactor [ https://substack.com/redirect/37e78fd0-6586-4bb7-91a6-7e5f215cadf3?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] that Fermi and others constructed in a racquetball court at the University of Chicago. In order to make one of these things work with natural (unenriched) uranium, which was all that they had to work with at the time, they had to get a lot of it situated close together but interspersed with neutron moderators such as pure graphite or heavy water. The Germans just didn’t have enough to make a go of it.
The Koenig Analyzer
In one of my KdK posts I made passing reference to the Koenig Analyzer [ https://substack.com/redirect/39d338a9-16f5-40e7-818c-b12d36372e43?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ], an echt-Steampunk device that was used to analyze acoustic waveforms. The Harvard collection has one, though it’s missing a few bits.
Between the A-shaped frame on the left, and the wooden box on the right, you can see a vertical row of 8 little nozzles. This is a manifold for distributing flammable gas to the 8 brass spheres (two of which are missing) on the left. The spheres are acoustic resonators, each tuned to a specific frequency. When all of them were exposed to the same sound, some of them would resonate more strongly than others. Since they were full of burning gas, the degree of resonance would be made visible by the size of the flames. The hand-cranked box on the right was originally covered by mirrors. It is a device known as a Wheatstone mirror. To be honest, I am unclear as to the principle of its operation in this case, but apparently, by looking into the mirror as it spun around, the observer was able to obtain information about the relative size of the flames that wouldn’t have been evident simply by looking at them directly.
It’s been known for a while that all sounds—all periodic signals, really—are composed of different frequencies superimposed to make a more complex waveform. The Koenig Analyzer is a tool for making that visually obvious. As such it would have been well known to Abbé Zéphirin Carrière, the acoustics genius who was the subject of my fourth KdK post [ https://substack.com/@nealstephenson/note/p-182808947?utm_source=notes-share-action&r=7y05q ] and who was better described in its followup [ https://substack.com/redirect/db10d003-5798-4b73-9958-2a2b40471676?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ].
