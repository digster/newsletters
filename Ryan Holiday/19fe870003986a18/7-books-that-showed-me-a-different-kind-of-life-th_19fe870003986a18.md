I heard someone say that a book should be a window and not a mirror–that it should help you look in on someone else, or out into another world rather than back at yourself. I think that’s why I love memoirs. They help you get inside someone else’s life. What’s it like to be Marcus Aurelius–the most powerful man in the world? [Meditations](https://bc5923e4.click.convertkit-mail4.com/38ud9v6kreskh2pprmnsrh45533oqi7h4g0oz/n2hohvhvz097o7c6hg/aHR0cHM6Ly93d3cudGhlcGFpbnRlZHBvcmNoLmNvbS9wcm9kdWN0cy9tZWRpdGF0aW9ucy1tYXJjdXMtYXVyZWxpdXMtZ3JlZ29yeS1oYXlzLXRyYW5zbGF0aW9uLXByZW1pdW0tbGVhdGhlci1lZGl0aW9uP19wb3M9NiZfc2lkPWY0NTRlOTk5ZCZfc3M9cg==) gives us a glimpse. What’s it like to die of terminal cancer? [When Breath Becomes Air](https://bc5923e4.click.convertkit-mail4.com/38ud9v6kreskh2pprmnsrh45533oqi7h4g0oz/48hvhehm48dx27cxh7/aHR0cHM6Ly93d3cudGhlcGFpbnRlZHBvcmNoLmNvbS9wcm9kdWN0cy9tZW1vaXIxNj9fcG9zPTEmX3NpZD04MmZlNWQwMmYmX3NzPXI=) documents it. What’s it like to be a queer teenage girl with a twin? Tegan and Sara’s book [High School](https://bc5923e4.click.convertkit-mail4.com/38ud9v6kreskh2pprmnsrh45533oqi7h4g0oz/reh8hohmgzl658a2h6/aHR0cHM6Ly93d3cudGhlcGFpbnRlZHBvcmNoLmNvbS9wcm9kdWN0cy9oaWdoLXNjaG9vbD9fcG9zPTEmX3NpZD1lZDBiNzNkZjkmX3NzPXI=) is a fascinating picture of it. What’s it like to be the most powerful person in the world trying to prepare someone to take your place? [Memoirs of Hadrian](https://bc5923e4.click.convertkit-mail4.com/38ud9v6kreskh2pprmnsrh45533oqi7h4g0oz/08hwh9h23r6lm0clh5/aHR0cHM6Ly93d3cudGhlcGFpbnRlZHBvcmNoLmNvbS9wcm9kdWN0cy9maWN0aW9uMjI_X3Bvcz0xJl9zaWQ9NThhMzI0MjU2Jl9zcz1y) tells us (kidding, it’s a novel, but I would love it even more if it were real) Of course, the irony of a great memoir is that it’s both a window and a mirror because in looking out at someone else, you end up with a better understanding of who you are as well. I think that happens best in weird memoirs, not necessarily celebrity memoirs but memoirs of unsung or under-explored lives. And that’s what we’re going to talk about in this month’s email–[some of my favorite memoirs](https://bc5923e4.click.convertkit-mail4.com/38ud9v6kreskh2pprmnsrh45533oqi7h4g0oz/9qhzhnhdorv52vt9h3/aHR0cHM6Ly93d3cudGhlcGFpbnRlZHBvcmNoLmNvbS9jb2xsZWN0aW9ucy9tZW1vaXJz), ones I think more people need to read.
[Asylum](https://bc5923e4.click.convertkit-mail4.com/38ud9v6kreskh2pprmnsrh45533oqi7h4g0oz/8ghqhohomn6rg0akh9/aHR0cHM6Ly93d3cudGhlcGFpbnRlZHBvcmNoLmNvbS9wcm9kdWN0cy9tZW1vaXIxP19wb3M9MSZfc2lkPWQwOTdjN2I3ZSZfc3M9cg==) by William Seabrook
I have been raving about this book [since 2015](https://bc5923e4.click.convertkit-mail4.com/38ud9v6kreskh2pprmnsrh45533oqi7h4g0oz/vqh3hrhozr9wnvbghl/aHR0cHM6Ly9vYnNlcnZlci5jb20vMjAxNS8xMi90aGUtYmVzdC1ib29rLWFib3V0LWFkZGljdGlvbi15b3V2ZS1uZXZlci1oZWFyZC1vZi1pcy1iYWNrLWludG8tcHJpbnQtYWZ0ZXItNTAteWVhcnMv). It was very hard to find when I first got it–I tracked down an old paperback for a dollar on Amazon but thankfully it’s since found a new and much-deserved audience. [Asylum](https://bc5923e4.click.convertkit-mail4.com/38ud9v6kreskh2pprmnsrh45533oqi7h4g0oz/8ghqhohomn6rg0akh9/aHR0cHM6Ly93d3cudGhlcGFpbnRlZHBvcmNoLmNvbS9wcm9kdWN0cy9tZW1vaXIxP19wb3M9MSZfc2lkPWQwOTdjN2I3ZSZfc3M9cg==) is the very true story of the journalist William Seabrook who, in December 1933, as one of the most successful travel writers in the world, voluntarily committed himself to an insane asylum to treat a crippling addiction to alcohol. In a time before 12-step programs, support groups and rehab centers, there were no other options. What emerged was not just quite possibly one of the first modern addiction/recovery memoirs, but perhaps the most honest and haunting account of the struggle for mental health in literature. It’s written like a travel book, except the journey is through his own soul and the wreckage of his life. There are some dark secrets in here, but the writing is genuinely great. More people should know [this book](https://bc5923e4.click.convertkit-mail4.com/38ud9v6kreskh2pprmnsrh45533oqi7h4g0oz/8ghqhohomn6rg0akh9/aHR0cHM6Ly93d3cudGhlcGFpbnRlZHBvcmNoLmNvbS9wcm9kdWN0cy9tZW1vaXIxP19wb3M9MSZfc2lkPWQwOTdjN2I3ZSZfc3M9cg==).
[Why Fish Don't Exist: A Story of Loss, Love, and the Hidden Order of Life](https://bc5923e4.click.convertkit-mail4.com/38ud9v6kreskh2pprmnsrh45533oqi7h4g0oz/l2hehmhl87mxo4f6h0/aHR0cHM6Ly93d3cudGhlcGFpbnRlZHBvcmNoLmNvbS9wcm9kdWN0cy93aHktZmlzaC1kb250LWV4aXN0LWEtc3Rvcnktb2YtbG9zcy1sb3ZlLWFuZC10aGUtaGlkZGVuLW9yZGVyLW9mLWxpZmU_X3Bvcz0xJl9zaWQ9ZmU4NzhkYTM3Jl9zcz1y) by Lulu Miller
I don’t think I’ve ever read anything like [this book](https://bc5923e4.click.convertkit-mail4.com/38ud9v6kreskh2pprmnsrh45533oqi7h4g0oz/l2hehmhl87mxo4f6h0/aHR0cHM6Ly93d3cudGhlcGFpbnRlZHBvcmNoLmNvbS9wcm9kdWN0cy93aHktZmlzaC1kb250LWV4aXN0LWEtc3Rvcnktb2YtbG9zcy1sb3ZlLWFuZC10aGUtaGlkZGVuLW9yZGVyLW9mLWxpZmU_X3Bvcz0xJl9zaWQ9ZmU4NzhkYTM3Jl9zcz1y). It’s a story about a fish scientist. It’s a story of the history of taxonomy. It’s a memoir of a woman who blew up her relationship. It’s a story about finding order and meaning in life. It’s somehow about all these things, even though it’s only a short 250 pages? I don’t know how Miller does it, but I just need you to trust me: It’s great. She set out to write a book about a nineteenth-century scientist obsessed with cataloging every fish in the sea. And then somehow, it becomes this incredibly moving and personal memoir about her trying to find order and meaning in a life that has fallen apart. Very sweetly written. You’ll learn a bunch of stuff you didn’t know. Crazy good title, [very weird memoir](https://bc5923e4.click.convertkit-mail4.com/38ud9v6kreskh2pprmnsrh45533oqi7h4g0oz/l2hehmhl87mxo4f6h0/aHR0cHM6Ly93d3cudGhlcGFpbnRlZHBvcmNoLmNvbS9wcm9kdWN0cy93aHktZmlzaC1kb250LWV4aXN0LWEtc3Rvcnktb2YtbG9zcy1sb3ZlLWFuZC10aGUtaGlkZGVuLW9yZGVyLW9mLWxpZmU_X3Bvcz0xJl9zaWQ9ZmU4NzhkYTM3Jl9zcz1y). You'll like this one.
[Death Be Not Proud](https://bc5923e4.click.convertkit-mail4.com/38ud9v6kreskh2pprmnsrh45533oqi7h4g0oz/m2h7h5h3dz64o0umhq/aHR0cHM6Ly93d3cudGhlcGFpbnRlZHBvcmNoLmNvbS9wcm9kdWN0cy9kZWF0aC1iZS1ub3QtcHJvdWQ_X3Bvcz0xJl9zaWQ9MWY1ZGY5OTJhJl9zcz1y) by John Gunther
Written in 1949 by the famous journalist John Gunther about the death of his genius son Johnny at 17 from a brain tumor, [this book](https://bc5923e4.click.convertkit-mail4.com/38ud9v6kreskh2pprmnsrh45533oqi7h4g0oz/m2h7h5h3dz64o0umhq/aHR0cHM6Ly93d3cudGhlcGFpbnRlZHBvcmNoLmNvbS9wcm9kdWN0cy9kZWF0aC1iZS1ub3QtcHJvdWQ_X3Bvcz0xJl9zaWQ9MWY1ZGY5OTJhJl9zcz1y) is deeply moving and profound. It’s impossible to not be awed by this young boy who knows he will die too soon and struggles to do it with dignity and purpose. Gunther was one of the great journalists of his era and an incredible writer. But the line I think about the most comes from his mother, Frances, toward the end, reflecting on the loss of her son, who—as you will see when you read the book—she had loved as much as was humanly possible. She said that her only wish, after everything, was that “she’d loved him more.” By that she just means, she wished she’d held even less back, that she’d told him how she felt even more, that she’d been present even more, that she’d cherished every moment more…which in the end, is how we’ll all feel. It's haunting. Every parent should read it.
[Totto-Chan: The Little Girl at the Window](https://bc5923e4.click.convertkit-mail4.com/38ud9v6kreskh2pprmnsrh45533oqi7h4g0oz/dpheh0heln9pqmfmh4/aHR0cHM6Ly93d3cudGhlcGFpbnRlZHBvcmNoLmNvbS9wcm9kdWN0cy9tZW1vaXIxND9fcG9zPTEmX3NpZD1hMTdlYWI1M2UmX3NzPXI=) by Tetsuko Kuroyanagi
Fun fact about this book: it holds the Guinness World Record for the bestselling autobiographical memoir by a single author. Over twenty-five million copies in print, translated into more than thirty languages. This book describes her childhood in pre-WWII Japan as a misunderstood girl who obviously suffered from attention disorders and excess energy. It wasn't until she met a special school principal—unlike any I have ever heard of—who finally GOT her. Meaning, she was understood, cared about and unconditionally supported in a way that both inspires me and makes me deeply jealous. If only all of us could be so lucky…I love [this one](https://bc5923e4.click.convertkit-mail4.com/38ud9v6kreskh2pprmnsrh45533oqi7h4g0oz/dpheh0heln9pqmfmh4/aHR0cHM6Ly93d3cudGhlcGFpbnRlZHBvcmNoLmNvbS9wcm9kdWN0cy9tZW1vaXIxND9fcG9zPTEmX3NpZD1hMTdlYWI1M2UmX3NzPXI=) and have recommended it time and time again.
[Twelve Years a Slave](https://bc5923e4.click.convertkit-mail4.com/38ud9v6kreskh2pprmnsrh45533oqi7h4g0oz/e0hph7h78o3rkns8h2/aHR0cHM6Ly93d3cudGhlcGFpbnRlZHBvcmNoLmNvbS9wcm9kdWN0cy9tZW1vaXIxNT9fcG9zPTUmX3NpZD1lMmY2ZWY5ZTkmX3NzPXI=) by Solomon Northup
Despite the movie, not nearly enough people know about the real story of Solomon Northup–a born free man in the North who, as a traveling musician, was brought out of his home state on false pretenses in order to be captured, kidnapped and transported South to be sold as a slave. It’s harrowing and written lucidly and articulately by the person who experienced it. For 12 years, he was a slave–and not some border-state slave, but a bayou slave in the Deep South. He was cut off from his family and his freedom, and even among the slaves he was different. He couldn’t tell anyone he could read and write; he couldn’t even tell anyone that he was formerly free because they threatened to kill him if he did. This book is just as good as [Frederick Douglass’ memoir](https://bc5923e4.click.convertkit-mail4.com/38ud9v6kreskh2pprmnsrh45533oqi7h4g0oz/7qh7h8h9r52n00azh6/aHR0cHM6Ly93d3cudGhlcGFpbnRlZHBvcmNoLmNvbS9wcm9kdWN0cy9tZW1vaXIxMT9fcG9zPTEmX3NpZD1mODQ4MmIzYWUmX3NzPXI=) and I think illustrates the horrors of slavery in a much more undeniable way.
[Hollywood Park](https://bc5923e4.click.convertkit-mail4.com/38ud9v6kreskh2pprmnsrh45533oqi7h4g0oz/owhkhqhw2dmq4rfvhr/aHR0cHM6Ly93d3cudGhlcGFpbnRlZHBvcmNoLmNvbS9wcm9kdWN0cy9ob2xseXdvb2QtcGFyay1hLW1lbW9pcj9fcG9zPTEmX3NpZD0wNzM5NGZmMjcmX3NzPXI=) by Mikel Jollett
If you've heard the band The Airborne Toxic Event, you're probably not thinking, I want to read that guy's memoir. But you should and you do, I promise! Because his life (so far) is crazy! Not only did he grow up with a series of alcoholic fathers and stepfathers, but he was raised in a famous cult called Synanon. This cult separated children from their parents, so for the first five years of his life he didn’t know who his parents were. When his mother finally broke free from the cult, his life didn't get better. It got worse. Somehow, miraculously, he breaks free from this environment, ends up at Stanford and becomes this famous musician. It’s a [moving, powerful book](https://bc5923e4.click.convertkit-mail4.com/38ud9v6kreskh2pprmnsrh45533oqi7h4g0oz/owhkhqhw2dmq4rfvhr/aHR0cHM6Ly93d3cudGhlcGFpbnRlZHBvcmNoLmNvbS9wcm9kdWN0cy9ob2xseXdvb2QtcGFyay1hLW1lbW9pcj9fcG9zPTEmX3NpZD0wNzM5NGZmMjcmX3NzPXI=) by a fantastic writer.
[Conundrum](https://bc5923e4.click.convertkit-mail4.com/38ud9v6kreskh2pprmnsrh45533oqi7h4g0oz/z2hghnheqx2loeaph0/aHR0cHM6Ly93d3cudGhlcGFpbnRlZHBvcmNoLmNvbS9wcm9kdWN0cy9jb251bmRydW0_X3Bvcz0xJl9zaWQ9MjViYzhjYmM4Jl9zcz1y) by Jan Morris
Jan Morris was born James Morris—a veteran who was also part of the 1953 Everest expedition. Then in the 1970s, Morris underwent gender reassignment surgery and became Jan. I found this to be a very honest, eye-opening, and just very human memoir. It's one of the first widely read accounts of gender transition. What makes this book so good is that it was published in 1974, so there’s no political baggage, no controversy, no phobia about it—it just is. It just blew my mind when I first read it. Which is what memoirs do, they help you see someone else’s world. I wish more people who were obsessed with turning trans people into a culture war issue–and everyone spouting about it on the internet–would read this book, but honestly, everyone should read more Jan Morris because in addition to this memoir, she is also an incredible historian and travel writer.
