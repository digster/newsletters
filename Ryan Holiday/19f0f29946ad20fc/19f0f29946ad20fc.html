​Revolutionary Road by Richard Yates
​Ok, so you’ve heard me rave about The Moviegoer for years. I didn’t know that it narrowly beat out Revolutionary Road for the National Book Award in 1962. Reading them both again this year, it struck me how similar they are. In Moviegoer, Binx is single and angsty. In Revolutionary Road, Frank Wheeler is married and angsty. Both are scarred by war, grappling with the emptiness of 50’s America and modernity. Both are problematic characters, but Frank much more so–certainly much more so than I remembered. I decided to re-read Revolutionary Road after reading James Salter’s Light Years (which I also loved). I was haunted by how dark and sad it was, how delusional and yet also how close to life–the way that when a relationship is struggling you can both fool yourself with flights of fantasies, the way that hurt people hurt people. I dunno, I feel like it’s an important book to read in your thirties or forties–as I have said about The Moviegoer, it’s sort of a second coming-of-age story but with cautionary elements. Fiction is, as always, a great mirror.

​Foster by Claire Keegan
​As I’ve said, if you’re ever in a reading rut, go pick up a really short book. It’s like swimming with fins on or something–look how fast I am. There is nobody better to do this with than Claire Keegan. You just rip through these books…and then the ending of each one just guts you. I loved Small Things Like These (here’s why), I enjoyed So Late In The Day, and this one, I thought was the sweetest…until again, the ending. She is just a master of her craft and I’m not sure you can go wrong with any of her books (or short stories). The only reason I haven’t read them all yet is that, like I said, they are good to keep in your back pocket (literally and figuratively) until you need them.

​Don’t Call It Art: 10 Ways to Create Like a Kid Again by Austin Kleon
​Austin Kleon told me he thought this would be my least favorite book of his. I don’t know why he said this–I love everything he does and have for years. In fact, I just had a lovely full-circle moment with my nine-year-old who has gotten really into poetry where I was telling him about Austin’s “black out” poems (where he blacks out words from newspaper articles to create poetry). My son was amazed. You can do that? Yes, you can! And that’s exactly what Austin’s new book is about. It’s not just about the creative process (which he covers very well in Steal Like an Artist and Show Your Work and Keep Going) but a creative worldview, which children so naturally have and then we, as adults, crush in ourselves and in them. In Don’t Call It Art, Kleon is drawing on kid-inspired insights to pull creatives out of a rut (and I would say to bring back your love of the game). As he does in all of his books, he communicates the essence of writing and creating art better than anyone else I can think of. We’ve got a ton of signed copies of his books, which you can find here.

​The Road by Cormac McCarthy
​A year or so ago, The New York Times asked me for my favorite books of the 21st Century and apparently I only picked one novel. But they followed up earlier this month to ask if I would do a deep dive on The Road by Cormac McCarthy, the book I had chosen. I re-read the book (for at least the third time) in less than two days. It was as powerful and as moving as ever. After multiple reads, what I take away most is the image of carrying the fire. That each of us have this kind of goodness, morality, earnestness innately within us. Our job as a parent and person surviving in a fallen, broken, evil world is to keep that fire alive, to carry it. This book asks questions that a lot of books in today’s literature fail to ask: where are the heroic figures? Where do we go to learn virtue and hope and values? Everyone should read this one, but if you have kids you should especially read it. It’s one of my favorite parenting books in disguise.

​Joyful, Anyway by Kate Bowler
​Ok, one of my all-time favorite book titles is Viktor Frankl’s Yes To Life. I like the title but I love the subtitle, In Spite of Everything. Because of the profound, almost sacred suffering behind Frankl’s wisdom, it doesn’t feel right to say anyone could match that, but I will say that Kate Bowler has done well with her new book: Joyful, Anyway. Actually, all her titles are pretty good: No Cure For Being Human and Everything Happens for a Reason: And Other Lies I’ve Loved. And it should be said, the wisdom in them did not come easily: at 35–married and a mother to her young son–she was diagnosed with incurable stage four colon cancer. In this book–written a decade after her diagnosis–Bowler writes about the joy she found amidst the pain and the heartbreak that comes with a cancer diagnosis. It was an honor to have Kate join me on The Daily Stoic Podcast, we had a great conversation that will be out soon. In the meantime, you should really read this book.

​The Wreck of the Mentor: A True Story of Death, Despair, and Deliverance in the Age of Sail by Eric Jay Dolin
​I said on Morning Joe (in one of my book recommendation appearances) that I don’t really like boats but I love shipwreck books. When I saw the Wreck of the Mentor was coming out, I preordered it and started it the day that it arrived. It’s fascinating and well-written–the problem is that these books are so often limited by the source material (or as is often the case, the lack thereof). This one tells the story of the eleven whalemen who survived a shipwreck on a Pacific reef in 1832, only to spend years in captivity among the Indigenous people of Palau–a story that had mostly been lost to history. I will say, I wasn’t riveted by it as I have been by some of my favorites. The best one I read recently was A Marriage At Sea, but there is also The Zorg, Endurance, In The Heart of the Sea, Dead Wake and Pirate Hunters. For more shipwreck books, I talked about more of my favorites in this video.

Kids

We went straight to our own bookstore and picked up the boxset of the first five, which was one of the very first titles I picked out for the young adults section and has never been as popular as I would have guessed. I loved those books as a kid. Anyway, we’re in the middle of number two right now. He was also assigned Tales of a Fourth Grade Nothing by Judy Blume for his summer reading, which we started and are enjoying. It’s lovely when you get to revisit favorites from your own childhood. My seven-year-old basically refused to be read or read anything but Don’t Trust Fish.
