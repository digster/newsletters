​The Consolation of Philosophy by Boethius
​One of the frustrating parts about being mostly self-educated is that you don’t know about so many classic texts. The amazing part is that you are constantly discovering amazing, mind-blowing books that you didn’t even know existed. I not only didn’t know about Boethius’s The Consolation of Philosophy…I had to ask ChatGPT to tell me how to pronounce it because I’ve never even heard the name before! By the way, it was a good example of how use of AI requires a good bullshit detector (which I wrote about here) because it told me something that didn’t sound right, so I found some videos of professors talking about Boethius and they pronounced it totally differently. I showed this to ChatGPT which immediately told me, ‘Oh yeah, you’re right, sorry.’ In any case, in the Stoic tradition of the philosopher who is dealt a bad hand by fate, Boethius–living in the last centuries of the Roman empire–is brought up on false charges and sentenced to death. Angry, scared, sad, confused, he’s not sure what to do…except turn to philosophy. We are lucky he did…and lucky he wrote this dialogue/poem/fable about his discourse with Lady Philosophy. I found it very moving and a great example of what philosophy is supposed to do–which is to help us in our darkest moments. A related book recommendation that I sent to a friend who was in the middle of a similarly dark experience is Oscar Wilde’s De Profundis (also written in prison). The other book I give out in such circumstances (someone at my talk in Phoenix was in tears over the loss of a loved one) is Seneca’s Hardships and Happiness.

​Inside the Box: How Constraints Make Us Better by David Epstein
​Ok, I read this book in December and have been tortured by not being able to tell you about it. I love David Epstein’s writing. I have raved for years about Range (which I call a parenting book in disguise) and have been eagerly awaiting a sequel. In this one, he looks at the power of constraints. We think we want total freedom. We think we want to avoid obstacles and limitations. In fact, these are precisely the things we need to be creative, successful, even happy. The book is great, as expected, and reminds me of a famous line I’ve quoted before from Cleanthes, who said that the ‘fetters’ of poetry unlock creativity (the rhythm and structure are both limiting and generative). What you think is constraining you is actually directing you, channeling you into being something great. I loved this book.

​The Committed by Viet Thanh Nguyen
​I read The Sympathizer earlier this year as part of my Vietnam research. I am writing about Stockdale so I have read a lot about the American perspective and what I loved about The Sympathizer is that it captures the Vietnamese perspective–actually all the perspectives because the narrator is actually a Communist double agent serving in the South. And then he flees to America after the war so you get the Vietnamese refugee experience, too. In The Committed, well, it’s not totally clear what you’re getting at first because it is a trippy, consciousness-bending book. But in time, you find the narrator in France–giving you a very different immigrant experience. I am interviewing Nguyen next week, and I have so many questions. I will say, I love a good novel in the second person and while this is not technically that, it bends and breaks so many conventions that you really have to pay close attention. It reminded me, in a way, of one of my favorite second person novels, Lancelot by Walker Percy.

​Scum of the Earth by Arthur Koestler
​I am still reeling from reading Darkness at Noon a few months ago and when I recommended it, someone wrote in to tell me that Koestler had written a memoir I should read. In fact, he wrote Darkness at Noon (a novel about being tortured and executed in a communist prison) as he awaited being thrown into a French concentration camp (where he faced execution) in the early days of WWII. And that was after he was sentenced to death and spent three months in prison in Málaga, Spain. The point is: Darkness at Noon was based on real darkness. This book hit me very hard given what’s happening in the world right now–and the dangers we face with the dogs of hate and war loose. I don’t know if Koestler ever read War is a Racket (which I recommended last month), but I think he would have liked it. He also would have liked another book I’ve raved about and is painfully timely: Kathrine Kressmann Taylor’s Address Unknown, which is a series of letters between two business partners (one Jewish, one not) during the rise of Hitler in Germany. One is slowly corrupted by the events happening around him, his heart closing to the people and ideas he once believed in. I am reading now about his tragic death, which echoes a third book/author I have raved about: Stefan Zweig and his short book, Montaigne. In any case, if you haven’t read Darkness at Noon, you must. If you want a great memoir that has tones of The World of Yesterday, read Scum of the Earth.

Kids
​We’ve been slowly making our way through the Harry Potter series as a family for the last several years. As a reward, we took the kids to see Harry Potter and the Prisoner of Azkaban performed with a symphony orchestra at the Bass Center in Austin, which was incredible. To me, this is what reading does–it is a gateway not just to different worlds but also to different forms of art. Here they were, a 9-year-old and a 6-year-old, excited to see a symphony! Take that Timothee Chalamet! We’ve also been reading An Animal Poem For Every Day of The Year by Jane McMorland Hunter together each night and loving it. Again, anything, any way you can introduce them to these forms of art is great. My youngest is definitely what you could call a “deeply feeling kid” (they get really mad if I say that), so it was great to see Dr. Becky has a new children’s book about precisely that. We got a copy of Leave Me Alone!: A Good Inside Story About Deeply Feeling Kids two weeks ago and read it together while we waited for our food at Pool Burger.
