​The Rush: California Gold, the Civil War, and the Making of the Modern World by Nathaniel Philbrick
​This book made a liar out of me. Every year I tell myself, I am not going to read any more books about the Civil War this year. This book is about the Gold Rush in California, you’re thinking, so how did it make you a liar? Well, it turns out the Gold Rush (and California) is WAY more connected to the Civil War than I could have ever imagined. And I grew up in Sacramento! And I am obsessed with the Civil War. It’s just a great book. The other thing they didn’t teach us as a kid about the Gold Rush was how international it was. Sutter’s Fort (which I have been to more times than I can count) was built by Hawaiian labor. Some of the main characters in this book are Chilean, Australian, and Chinese. How Philbrick tracked down all the info about these different hidden figures is magical. In Right Thing Right Now, I talk about “the dark energy” in American life that goes from issue to issue. This is a great book about the battle to defeat that force and all the darkness that was swirling around in that period. I really liked this book. If you want to understand a lot of what’s happening today in our new Gilded Age–involving everything from political corruption to AI–this book is a great way to do that, as so many books about the past help us understand the present.

​Yellow Pine: A Novel by Claire Vaye Watkins
​I read this after I saw a Times review that mentioned it was a novel partially inspired by a weird evolutionary psychology book I had read years ago called Sperm Wars. Since I love this part of the country (I have raved about Cerro Gordo and Brent’s book Ghost Town Living) and I went to school in Riverside, I figured I’d read it. It’s a strange but very interesting book. And then I was reminded of it again when someone was telling me the story of the controversy about the lesbian bar in Massachusetts. Yellow Pine does a good job of capturing, skewering but also humanizing the kind of wounded, earnest but also insane mindset of certain activist types…without letting go of the fact that the world they are reacting to is also insane (and not so earnest). I liked it…

​Those Who Are About to Die: A Day in the Life of a Roman Gladiator by Harry Sidebottom
​Ok for a book that could have been pretty mid and I still would have enjoyed (I mean, gladiators, c’mon), the level of detail in this book is incredible. It might be one of the best and most informative books about Rome I have ever read. It basically walks you through twenty-four hours in the life of a gladiator at the height of the gladiatorial games…and as a result, tells you everything about every level of Roman society. I was genuinely shocked at how much I learned reading this. Being able to place–and physically imagine–some of the stuff from this book was a benefit of our trip to Pompeii this summer. Like when you stand in the gladiator barracks or in a training ground you get it. It also strikes you how small they were (men were 5’5’’). It’s also fascinating that the Stoics hated the gladiators. “Nothing is so damaging to good character than wasting time at the Games,” Seneca said. In the very first pages of Meditations, Marcus thanks his lucky stars that he never got hooked on chariot racing. I wish they’d objected because the games were cruel but it was more because they thought they were mindless (I disagree–it seems, at least from this book, that these were elite athletes). But we just had a cage match on Capitol Hill (named for the Capitoline), so not much has changed!

​Tao Te Ching by Laozi
​I read a different translation of the Tao Te Ching (I believe on Rick Rubin’s recommendation) when I was writing Stillness Is The Key. When I saw that David Bentley Hart–who also did an eye-opening translation of the New Testament–had been studying the Tao for years and was now doing his version, I thought, well that’s a fascinating convergence. I have to read that. And I did. Funny enough, I read it while I was in Rome (again, the convergence). It’s not quite as lyrical as some of the others I’ve read, but I found the wisdom in it to be striking regardless. This is my favorite passage–it’s actually the one I quote at the beginning of Stillness:

So very circumspect, as though fording a winter stream,
so very vigilant, as if alert to perils on every side,
so very courteous, as if making a formal visit,
so very yielding, like ice beginning to thaw,
so unpretentious, like unfashioned wood,
so open, like a rolling valley,
so opaque, like a turbid pool.

It’s a nice short read you can flip through. Maybe put it by your bedside and read a few at a time.

​Situated: Find the People and Places That Bring Out Your Best by Angela Duckworth
​If you’ve read Grit, you should read this. If you think grit, or really any of the virtues are important, you should read this. Because it makes a very important point: virtue is situational. Someone who is courageous one day, might not be on another. Someone who is hard to discourage in one area of their life, might be a quitter in another. Why? Because it depends! It depends on so much! It depends on what you ate for breakfast. It depends on your identity. It depends on how much sleep you got. Look, I’ve met a lot of these academic thought leaders over the years–I’ve just always felt Angela Duckworth is different. I like her. I like her work. I liked this book. Especially the part where she talked about her marriage and the struggle she went through to turn it around. Just great stuff here. She came on The Daily Stoic Podcast a few years ago and we had a wonderful conversation, which you can listen to here. Her new episode should drop soon.

Misc
​Seth Wickersham–one of my favorite sportswriters–has a new book out with a title based on a song by one of my favorite musicians (Springsteen)...so yeah, I liked Be on That Hill. It’s a collection of his best essays profiling athletes like Tom Brady, Patrick Mahomes, Deion Sanders, Alex Honnold, Kobe Bryant and more. Plus, it’s got a foreword from one of my favorite writers, Wright Thompson (who wrote The Barn, a book I’ve raved about in this email many times). Also, I mentioned being in Rome over the summer. While I was there, I did one of the hardest things I have ever done: I ran 50K through the streets of the eternal city, checking out all the major Stoic sights as I did. You can watch the video we did about it here. I did a similar thing last summer where I did the original Marathon in Greece. Anyway, as always, Murakami’s What I Talk About When I Talk About Running was helpful with all that.
