​Go Gentle by Maria Semple
​Ok, maybe you picked up on why I was in Hawaii, last month? It was to do Oprah (which will come out soon). Why on earth did Oprah want to talk to me? It was because of Maria Semple’s absolutely lovely new book, Go Gentle, which is about a Stoic philosopher! Maria is funny and a great writer…and a practicing Stoic. She combined all these into a lovely new novel that I’m sure is going to be a huge hit. Go Gentle follows Adora Hazzard, a Stoic philosopher and single mother in New York City, as her well-ordered life is turned upside down. It’s funny, smart and full of plot twists that include black-market art deals and a bunch of Stoic themes. If you want more novels about Stoicism, then I must recommend (as I did to Oprah), Marguerite Yourcenar’s Memoirs of Hadrian as well as Tom Wolfe’s A Man in Full. I think we could also put Walker Percy’s The Moviegoer in as a Stoic novel, too. The main character in Maria’s novel published a popular adaptation of Epictetus that is clearly based on a real book called The Art of Living by Sharon Lebell. It’s a great and accessible book that I also recommend. You can listen to me doing a deep dive into Stoicism with Maria here…but go read the book. It’s great and might be a good pick for a fiction-loving spouse who doesn’t ‘get’ what you love about the philosophy. We have signed copies here.

​The Best and the Brightest by David Halberstam
​I first read–or read a big chunk of this book–many years ago. Now that I am writing about Vietnam, I knew I would have to re-read it. The funny thing is that in the intervening years, I’ve read many books by Halberstam, including The Children (amazing. MUST read), Breaks of the Game (a favorite), The Coldest Winter (haunting) and his book about Michael Jordan, too. But now I was finally ready to appreciate The Best and the Brightest as the absolute masterpiece that it was. I just don’t know how he does it–not just the reporting, but his ability to get to the essence of the people he writes about. It’s not journalism, honestly, it’s literature. Like if you told me The Best and the Brightest was a novel about a fictional war, I’d still be recommending it! (Both because it’s literature and because it would teach you about human folly). How did we blunder into this war? How did we mismanage it once we did? How did the personality flaws–but also the virtues of Kennedy, LBJ, Dean Rusk, McNamara, Maxwell Taylor, George Ball, John Kenneth Galbraith–make that not only possible, but in a way, make it a foregone conclusion? As always, the way to understand the present is by studying the past. I chose to read this book instead of watching the news and it’s made me a more informed citizen. It also gives me a pit in my stomach about our recent foreign interventionisms (General Mattis said the same thing at Stanford).

​What to Make of a Life: Cliffs, Fog, Fire and the Self-Knowledge Imperative by Jim Collins
​Speaking of Stoicism and Stanford…I also got to speak to Jim Collins this month, who famously popularized the “Stockdale Paradox” in Good to Great, which resulted from his time at Stanford with Admiral Stockdale (and is based on Stoicism). His new book is really good and classic Jim Collins. It reminded me a lot, in its set up, of Plutarch’s Parallel Lives in the way that it profiles two similar historical or pop culture or business figures and how they dealt with the problems and opportunities that life gave them. He studies the diverging paths taken when faced with similar, life-altering “cliff moments,” and uses those patterns to explore how we can most meaningfully answer the question of what to make of our lives. Jim sent me an early edition of the book back in January, in which he included a note that read: “Ryan, this is the best work of my life to date. It transformed me over 12 years. I’ll be curious how it changes how you look at life. Enjoy, Jim.” And it did. It changed how I look at life. It’s a lovely book. You can listen to my conversation with him on The Daily Stoic Podcast here, or watch it on YouTube.

​The Hunters, Cassada, Gods of Tin: The Flying Years, and Burning the Days by James Salter
​I don’t know what possessed me to decide to re-read Salter’s novels this month but as soon as I started, I read three of his books in three days and now I am knee-deep in his memoir, Burning the Days. I remember I first heard of him from his New York Times obituary in 2015 and decided to read one of his books. From the jump, I was hooked–especially by The Hunters, his autobiographical novel of an ambitious Air Force fighter pilot in Korea. His real life story is fascinating: He went to West Point, became a flyer, distinguished himself in Korea (where he flew 100 missions and shot down a MiG), then became a writer, a collaborator with Robert Redford and a friend of Hunter S. Thompson. His writing is sparse and clean and forceful (often compared, fittingly, with the planes he flew). I got so much out of re-reading them, things I just didn’t appreciate 10 years ago. One of the first things I did when I finished The Hunters was text Jack Carr and tell him that he should read Salter if he hadn’t yet (he hadn’t!). You should do the same! (If you have to pick, start with The Hunters). Cassada is about a fighter pilot who just doesn’t have the right stuff, Burning the Days is Salter’s memoir, and Gods of Tin is basically a greatest hits of his writing (fictional and otherwise) on flying.
