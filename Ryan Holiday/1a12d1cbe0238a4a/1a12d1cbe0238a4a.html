​They Don’t Want You To Read These 8 Banned Books (But I Think Everyone Should)​

—Today’s newsletter is sponsored by Shortform.

“We’re coming for the books.” I guess we can appreciate the Lieutenant Governor of Texas saying the quiet part out loud, but if you were one of the people who thought this whole ‘banned books’ thing was a bit hysterical or just a bit of marketing from the publishing industry, you can’t deny it anymore. In Texas, the state where I live, the second-most-powerful politician put together a list of 2,200 books he’s trying to remove from libraries and ban from schools–including books by Elie Wiesel, Anne Frank, Toni Morrison, Kurt Vonnegut and more. This shouldn't be a surprise to anyone, though, because back in 2021, he tried to cancel one of my favorite books (from three friends of mine) called Forget the Alamo that criticized some foundational myths and legends of Texas history.

So here we are.

Book banning is back. Not that it ever went away. It has always been with us.

So that’s the subject of this month’s email–some of my favorite books that have, over the years and all over the world, found themselves on various banned books lists. For instance, I am on tour in Australia right now–come see me!–and in the 20th century, it had some of the strictest federal censorship laws. Candide (published in 1759) was on that list. It’s an amazing book that everyone should read! I’ll be in Nashville next month (come see me!), and much more recently, a school district there banned Beloved. I’ll be in DC in December (come see me!) and well, you remember in 2024, the Department of Defense pulled 400 books out of the Naval Academy library, including I Know Why the Caged Bird Sings by Maya Angelou and then my speech was canceled for criticizing that insane decision (here’s my video about the whole thing).

Access to books and the free flow of ideas are things I feel very strongly about–and always have. In 2022, we gave away hundreds of banned books in front of The Painted Porch. ​​Fahrenheit 451 by Ray Bradbury
​The only book more ironic to ban than Fahrenheit 451 would be 1984 (which–surprise!–has also been banned in Florida and Colorado). But here we are. When I read Bradbury’s Fahrenheit 451 in high school, I took the book as a warning against totalitarian censorship by the government. It was only later, re-reading it as an adult, that I realized Bradbury—who had written the book on purchased time at a library typewriter—was depicting something much more insidious. This horrendous burning of books hadn’t been forced on them by a tyrant. Censorship was what the people wanted. They had chosen this. “Colored people don’t like Little Black Sambo,” he says, using terms that today would render the book politically incorrect, if not entirely canceled. “Burn it. White people don’t feel good about Uncle Tom’s Cabin. Burn it. Someone’s written a book on tobacco and cancer of the lungs? The cigarette people are weeping. Burn the book.” Bradbury’s message is a much more salient warning to modern Americans than many of us are ready to acknowledge upon first reading. America has always been in less danger of top-down Chinese or Soviet style suppression and much more vulnerable to short-sighted or even well-intentioned democratic censorship. I didn’t grasp this as a high schooler, but I can see it now.

​Anne Frank: The Diary of a Young Girl by Anne Frank
​Anne Frank’s diary is one of the books on the Lieutenant Governor of Texas’s list. The whole reason Anne Frank’s father allowed her diary to be published in the first place was so that something like the Holocaust would never happen again. If you want to keep your kids safe, you can’t hide history from them. If you want your kids to be good and decent and kind, show them the costs of a world that is not decent or kind or good. Don’t raise kids who are afraid of the world. Raise kids who know what the world is and choose to be good and decent and curious anyway.

​I Need a New Butt! by Dawn McMillan
​One of my kids’ favorite books is I Need a New Butt! (Whose author, I just learned, is from New Zealand…where I’m currently writing this as I get ready for The Daily Stoic Live tour.) I don’t want to spoil the plot of this book, but he needs a new butt because his has a crack in it. That’s funny, especially if you’re four or five or six or seven. My boys love the sequels too: I Broke My Butt! and My Butt Is So Noisy! Well, back in 2022, Toby Price, an assistant principal in Mississippi, read I Need a New Butt! to a group of second graders. Then—gasp—some of the kids started saying the word “butt” a lot. So Price was fired. Seriously. He sued for wrongful termination. He spent a fortune on legal fees. The school district spent a fortune on legal fees. Eventually, he won and was reinstated. Everyone lost, including the taxpayers and the kids. As Price put it, “If you limit the books kids have, you limit the person they become.” And this wasn’t some isolated bit of insanity. A little more than an hour from where we live in Texas, some crazy people in Llano County became fixated on getting the Butt books removed from the public library, calling them “grooming stuff.” Seriously, did they even read it? The campaign spread, more books disappeared, and a librarian who resisted what was happening was eventually fired. As I said, my kids love this book and think it's hilarious. Protect this classic at all costs!

​A Calendar of Wisdom: Daily Thoughts to Nourish the Soul, Written and Selected from the World's Sacred Texts by Leo Tolstoy
​Sometimes seeing things happen in other countries helps you see events in your own country more clearly. Tolstoy’s page-a-day masterpiece was originally published in 1904, but was banned by the Soviet Union after the Russian Revolution because of the book’s “spiritual orientation and its number of religious quotes.” It was basically lost to history until it was published again in 1995 and sold over 300,000 copies. Tolstoy said that this book–not his novels–was his most essential work. I’ve had this book for years and I still reach for it. I’ve given it to countless Daily Stoic Podcast guests and to my friends when they come to town. Clearly I’m a fan of the daily-read format, seeing that I’ve written two of them (The Daily Dad and The Daily Stoic, which we’re celebrating the tenth anniversary of this month with this special edition!), so if you enjoy those books, you’ll love this one. It’s got thought-provoking quotes from the Stoics, Schopenhauer, Socrates, Confucius and so much more. If you don’t have this one on your shelf, you should absolutely pick it up. And remember: if the totalitarians did something (book banning), it’s probably a bad idea to do it here!

​The 48 Laws of Power by Robert Greene
​Not only is this one of the most shoplifted books, it’s also the most requested and most banned book in the federal prison system. A friend of mine who works on death penalty cases told me that he has to warn defendants against reading it, as prosecutors will use possession of it against them in sentencing! The irony of banning this book is that it made it that much more controversial, which he talks about in Law 6: “Court attention at all costs,” and Law 7: “Get others to do the work for you.” As I’ve said before, people mistake this book as a how-to book when they should look at it, at the very least, from a defensive standpoint. Robert is not just a kind and generous person but he is a person of integrity and decency. He didn’t write this to say ‘this is how the world should work.’ He wrote it to say ‘this is how the world works.’ There’s a darkness to this book, but there’s a darkness to this world. You have to understand it to be able to defend against it.

​The Great Gatsby by F. Scott Fitzgerald
​Perhaps you read The Great Gatsby in high school, although maybe you didn’t if you grew up in South Carolina or Florida because they tried to ban it. Yes, Gatsby! One of the greatest novels ever written in English was challenged because they thought it was ‘vulgar’ and had ‘strong’ language in it. When I read about the attempts to ban Gatsby, I thought of that famous scene in Gatsby’s library, where Carraway is going through the books and notices that none of the pages had been cut. Gatsby only interacted with his idea of what books were, not what was actually in them. Which is fitting here, and unfortunately a bit ironic. Had these people ever actually opened a book? Did they not understand how powerful this book is, what it’s supposed to teach? When I was on Real Time with Bill Maher earlier this year, I told him Gatsby is the one book every American needs to read. If you haven’t read Gatsby in a while, I recommend picking it back up with this incredible 100th-anniversary annotated edition. It will give you the chance to not only reread the book from scratch, but also to learn more about it.

​Montaigne by Stefan Zweig
​Stefan Zweig is one of my favorite authors. In 1933, his work was banned in Germany and his books were publicly burned (let’s not wait for things to get that bad here). Most people know him for his book The World of Yesterday, which is about the Europe that was lost in the destruction of World War I, but his best work was written just before he committed suicide. Living in exile in Brazil, reeling from the censorship and closed-mindedness of his time, Zweig discovered a “dusty old edition” of the essays of Montaigne, who himself had lived through the religious persecution and violence of his time. Montaigne writes about the certainty it takes to sentence someone to death or to suppress ideas, about the intellectual hubris of it. That’s an important thing to keep in mind when we talk about book banning. We think we know what’s appropriate. We think we know what’s right. And that tends not to age well. Zweig’s biography of Montaigne itself wasn’t banned, but last year it almost went out of print. I bought up all 1,000 remaining copies, and thankfully the publisher agreed to another printing. We’re waiting for those copies to come in, but preorder it here because you need to read it.

​The Adventures of Huckleberry Finn by Mark Twain
​Banning The Adventures of Huckleberry Finn is a good example of political correctness as opposed to censorship. Huck Finn has been banned because of its harsh language and racial stereotypes–even though the book (originally published in 1885) is meant to push back against those stereotypes and against racism. Instead of banning these books, we need to contextualize them and refute them, which is exactly what Percival Everett did when he wrote James. In James, Everett retells the story of Huckleberry Finn from Jim’s perspective. His portrayal of Jim is incredible. He gives him a rich emotional and mental life that forces you to rethink the entire book.
