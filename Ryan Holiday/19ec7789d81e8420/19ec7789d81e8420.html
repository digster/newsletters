​12 Novels That Changed How I Think About The World​

—Today’s newsletter is sponsored by Shortform​

It was a long winding road trip this month, starting in Seattle and ending in Sacramento. In between, I did talks in Portland (visited Powells) and San Francisco (my parents came) and we covered a lot of ground. Cannon Beach. Redwood forests. Mendocino. Alcatraz. Swimming in Folsom Lake. Along the way, we listened to a good chunk of the audiobook of the Emily Wilson translation of The Odyssey (narrated by Claire Danes) because it’s the pick for the Quarterly Classics Book Club we are doing with The Painted Porch. I’ve read a bunch of translations, I’ve read chunks of it to my kids (they love the Polyphemus story best) and even took them to Greece to see some of the sights…but this was my first go-around of having it read to me. There is something about this story that gives you something new each time you pick it up, especially if you pick it up from a new angle–be it a new translation or at a different phase of your life. And despite being 3,000 years old (and make-believe), it not only feels current but also teaches timeless real-world lessons (hospitality, the perils of hubris, mercy, pragmatism, endurance). Also while I was in Greece, I filmed a video talking about the Stoic lessons in The Odyssey, which you can check out here. Anyway, I was inspired by that as I put together this month’s list. So here is some fiction–some new and some old–that taught me about life…that I think you should read and re-read. (Also we’d love to have you in the Quarterly Classics Book Club or join me on the rest of the Daily Stoic Live tour in Minneapolis, Chicago, and Detroit this summer, New Zealand and Australia in the fall, and other east coast dates this winter.)

​The Road by Cormac McCarthy
​A year or so ago, The New York Times asked me for my favorite books of the 21st Century and apparently I only picked one novel. But they followed up last week to ask if I would do a deep dive on The Road by Cormac McCarthy, the book I had chosen. I re-read the book (for at least the third time) in less than two days. It was as powerful and as moving as ever. After multiple reads, what I take away from it the most is the image of carrying the fire. That each of us have this kind of goodness, morality, earnestness innately within us. Our job as a parent and person surviving in a fallen, broken, evil world is to keep that fire alive, to carry it. This book asks questions that a lot of books in today’s literature fail to ask: Where are the heroic figures? Where do we go to learn virtue and hope and values? Everyone should read this one, but if you have kids you should especially read it. It’s one of my favorite parenting books in disguise.

​The Moviegoer by Walker Percy
​This remains not only one of my favorite novels, but a genuine treat to read. On my segment with Morning Joe I explain it as a The Catcher in the Rye, but for 30-year-olds. The main character, Binx Bolling, is a successful and seemingly well-adjusted young man in 1960s New Orleans. But he is also listless and empty, desperate for meaning and purpose. He hopes to get out of the “everydayness” of his life, and so he undertakes his “search,”…which is really just watching movies and dicking around. Stoicism doesn’t work. Neither does religion. So what will? What makes this book so incredible is that Walker Percy is writing about deep and profound philosophical ideas in an accessible and shockingly modern way. We’re following Binx’s spiritual journey, but really it’s a reflection of the spiritual journey we’re all on. There are so many observations woven into this book that leave you thinking, “wow, I get that. I’ve experienced that, too.” This one is especially important for young men to read today. I did an hour-long interview exclusively talking about this book for The Free Press, which you can check out here.

​Address Unknown by Kathrine Kressmann Taylor
​I first read this book in 2019 (it was originally published in 1938), but I’ve been talking about this book a lot recently and have returned to it several times. It’s a haunting series of letters between two men, both art dealers in business together–one Jewish, one not–during the rise of Hitler in Germany. One is slowly corrupted by the events happening around him, his heart closing to the people and ideas he once believed in, to the point that it eventually overrides the love and affection he once had for his close friend. As a novel, it serves as a perfect, indirect way of capturing the process we have all seen over the last few years, where people we thought we knew, thought we understood, become radicalized (or indifferent) and thus complicit in truly evil things. It reveals the stakes, the risks, the subtle ways that people betray their values and lose their compassion. This book could not be more relevant or more of a cautionary tale.

​Memoirs of Hadrian by Marguerite Yourcenar, Augustus by John Williams, and The Last of the Wine by Mary Renault
​This is one of my favorite novels of all time. It’s written from the perspective of Emperor Hadrian to his successor, Marcus Aurelius, at the end of his life. Hadrian was a complicated leader, as all people drawn to power are, but this novel reveals how he was able to identify and cultivate not just one but two heirs who were much better than he was. While this story isn’t strictly true, it’s a novel that gets at the truth in a way that only fiction can. As I mentioned last month, Memoirs of Hadrian would make a great gift for recent grads. Along similar lines, I was also bowled over by Augustus by John Williams and The Last of the Wine by Mary Renault. Augustus is basically the same premise as Memoirs of Hadrian, but about Augustus (who was quite frankly a much more interesting and impressive historical figure). The Last of the Wine is a coming-of-age novel of a young boy in Periclean Athens. Every few pages feature a delightful appearance of someone (Socrates, Plato, Euripides, Xenophon) or some place or battle you’ve heard of (Marathon, Sicily, Thrace, Battle of Munychia). These books are a reminder that the past was not a peaceful place, but as scary and uncertain and violent and cruel (as well as inspiring and creative and hopeful) as ours is today.

​The Oresteia: Agamemnon; The Libation Bearers; The Eumenides by Aeschylus and Medea and Other Plays by Euripides
​I was honestly pissed the first time I read The Oresteia (a series of three plays which cover the murder of Agamemnon by his wife Clytemnestra, the revenge killing of Clytemnestra by her son Orestes and then finally the famous trial of him for that murder) because I had just published my book Conspiracy, which was also about revenge and the legal system. You’re telling me there is a famous Greek play literally about "The Furies” and about the establishment of the legal system (and a 12-person jury) to moderate and end the cycle of retribution? How did I not know this? Anyway, these plays are some of the greatest writing ever done. Now what about Euripides? I had read Medea before, but that was before I had kids. It is a very different experience after…But I also read it now as effectively a play about a painful divorce and how easily one can go from loving to hating someone they were once so close to. But the play that struck me the hardest of all of these was Euripides' The Children of Hercules. Here we have, written in 430 BC, a play about the plight of the refugee, and how a society is judged by how it treats the weak and the vulnerable. The young children of Hercules are hounded to the Temple of Apollo (in Marathon, of course) by a bounty hunter from an angry king. He demands they be handed over to be punished. “They are suppliants and strangers,” the Athenians reply, “Who look to our city for help. / To reject them is to defy the gods.” But the angry king threatens war over it. In the play, Athens–like America, like Europe, now and forever–is torn. Sure, you say you care about people…but would you fight for them? What would you risk for them? This is incredibly moving given our current political climate and I wish I would have referenced this in my now-viral clip about U.S. immigration and deportations. If you need help reading either of these plays, I suggest Edith Hamilton’s The Greek Way as a good place to start for context and insight.

​The Annotated Great Gatsby: 100th Anniversary Deluxe Edition by F. Scott Fitzgerald​
When you first read Gatsby, you think, “this is a novel about The Jazz Age and the American Dream.” Then you get older and pick it up again and think, “oh, it’s so much more than that.” This is what great novels do. They grow with you. What you take out of them the first time is different from what you’d take out of them the second and the third and the fourth time. Aside from The Odyssey, no book has revealed this better to me than Gatsby (I wrote an entire essay about this, which you can check out here). There’s an incredible new annotated edition that came out last year with tons of fascinating asides that provide a ton of new context. If you haven’t read Gatsby, read this one. If you have, read it again with this new one. You’ll love it.

​James by Percival Everett
​It’s interesting to me which characters jump out at us at different points of our life. Obviously, Jim in Huckleberry Finn was presented a certain way by Mark Twain (and by centuries of racism). But what’s so brilliant about Everett’s novel–which retells the story but from Jim’s perspective…and actually gives him a rich emotional and mental life–is that it forces you to re-think the entire book. And somehow, despite being a much more tragic perspective, Everett still manages to make it funny. That alone is a task worthy of Mark Twain, which I talked about with Ron Chernow (who wrote Twain’s biography, which I also recommend) on an episode of the Daily Stoic Podcast. James reminds me of two other books I loved: Wicked River by Lee Sandlin (an absolutely incredible book about the history of the river) and Life on the Mississippi by Rinker Buck (about a guy who traveled the river on his own raft, not too dissimilar to the one Huck and Jim were on).

​The Plague and The Fall by Albert Camus
​I didn’t have the stomach to read The Plague during the pandemic, but it had been on my mental to-read list for a while. I finally got around to reading it about a year ago. I had already read The Fall, which partly inspired a talk I gave in the Netherlands for Daily Stoic Live (here’s a clip where I tell the main story from the book). It’s always amazing to me when fiction manages to capture something you’ve experienced in real life years before you’ve experienced it–the subtle observations and insights into how people deny, then accommodate themselves to something as insane as a plague. All of Camus’ novels are sort of philosophical allegories and both The Fall and The Plague are uniquely suited to the crises of our moment. Of course, the other plague book I have recommended a million times is The Great Influenza. If you’re thinking, “why should I read this, we’re past the pandemic?”, I assure you, at this rate, we’ll be experiencing one again soon enough. The past is prologue…
