It was a long winding road trip this month, starting in Seattle and ending in Sacramento. In between, I did talks in Portland (visited Powells) and San Francisco (my parents came) and we covered a lot of ground. Cannon Beach. Redwood forests. Mendocino. Alcatraz. Swimming in Folsom Lake. Along the way, we listened to a good chunk of the audiobook of the Emily Wilson translation of [The Odyssey](https://bc5923e4.click.convertkit-mail4.com/p9u97kol36u9h2w0qz4sqhpq2wp8rirhg8o6r/l2hehmhl9pkx24h6h0/aHR0cHM6Ly93d3cudGhlcGFpbnRlZHBvcmNoLmNvbS9wcm9kdWN0cy9vZHlzc2V5P19wb3M9MSZfc2lkPWJiODg5MWY1ZSZfc3M9cg==) (narrated by Claire Danes) because it’s the pick for the [Quarterly Classics Book Club](https://bc5923e4.click.convertkit-mail4.com/p9u97kol36u9h2w0qz4sqhpq2wp8rirhg8o6r/m2h7h5h3pxn450tmhq/aHR0cHM6Ly90aGUtcGFpbnRlZC1wb3JjaC1ib29rLWNsdWIubW4uY28vbGFuZGluZy8=) we are doing with The Painted Porch. I’ve read a bunch of translations, I’ve read chunks of it to my kids (they love the Polyphemus story best) and [even took them to Greece to see some of the sights](https://bc5923e4.click.convertkit-mail4.com/p9u97kol36u9h2w0qz4sqhpq2wp8rirhg8o6r/dpheh0hegx7p6mbmh4/aHR0cHM6Ly93d3cueW91dHViZS5jb20vd2F0Y2g_dj04dy0zYm9kRWhTVQ==)…but this was my first go-around of having it read to me. There is something about this story that gives you something new each time you pick it up, especially if you pick it up from a new angle–be it a new translation or at a different phase of your life. And despite being 3,000 years old (and make-believe), it not only feels current but also teaches timeless real-world lessons (hospitality, the perils of hubris, mercy, pragmatism, endurance). Also while I was in Greece, I filmed a video talking about the Stoic lessons in The Odyssey, [which you can check out here](https://bc5923e4.click.convertkit-mail4.com/p9u97kol36u9h2w0qz4sqhpq2wp8rirhg8o6r/e0hph7h7gznrvnt8h2/aHR0cHM6Ly93d3cueW91dHViZS5jb20vd2F0Y2g_dj1rRmk4NzBNcWxLTQ==). [The Road](https://bc5923e4.click.convertkit-mail4.com/p9u97kol36u9h2w0qz4sqhpq2wp8rirhg8o6r/x0hph6he6m7q23c5hl/aHR0cHM6Ly93d3cudGhlcGFpbnRlZHBvcmNoLmNvbS9wcm9kdWN0cy9yb2FkP19wb3M9MSZfc2lkPWZiNDBmNzU5MSZfc3M9cg==) by Cormac McCarthy
A year or so ago, The New York Times asked me for [my favorite books of the 21st Century](https://bc5923e4.click.convertkit-mail4.com/p9u97kol36u9h2w0qz4sqhpq2wp8rirhg8o6r/6qheh8hlg429r0hohk/aHR0cHM6Ly93d3cueW91dHViZS5jb20vd2F0Y2g_dj1BbENNQ1dfS0NnVQ==) and apparently I only picked one novel. But they followed up last week to ask if I would [do a deep dive](https://bc5923e4.click.convertkit-mail4.com/p9u97kol36u9h2w0qz4sqhpq2wp8rirhg8o6r/kkhmh6hnqp9xwzhlh7/aHR0cHM6Ly93d3cubnl0aW1lcy5jb20vMjAyNi8wNi8xMi9wb2RjYXN0cy8xMDAtYm9va3Mtcnlhbi1ob2xpZGF5Lmh0bWw=) on [The Road](https://bc5923e4.click.convertkit-mail4.com/p9u97kol36u9h2w0qz4sqhpq2wp8rirhg8o6r/x0hph6he6m7q23c5hl/aHR0cHM6Ly93d3cudGhlcGFpbnRlZHBvcmNoLmNvbS9wcm9kdWN0cy9yb2FkP19wb3M9MSZfc2lkPWZiNDBmNzU5MSZfc3M9cg==) by Cormac McCarthy, the book I had chosen. I re-read the book (for at least the third time) in less than two days. It was as powerful and as moving as ever. After multiple reads, what I take away from it the most is the image of carrying the fire. That each of us have this kind of goodness, morality, earnestness innately within us. Our job as a parent and person surviving in a fallen, broken, evil world is to keep that fire alive, to carry it. This book asks questions that a lot of books in today’s literature fail to ask: Where are the heroic figures? Where do we go to learn virtue and hope and values? [Everyone should read this one](https://bc5923e4.click.convertkit-mail4.com/p9u97kol36u9h2w0qz4sqhpq2wp8rirhg8o6r/x0hph6he6m7q23c5hl/aHR0cHM6Ly93d3cudGhlcGFpbnRlZHBvcmNoLmNvbS9wcm9kdWN0cy9yb2FkP19wb3M9MSZfc2lkPWZiNDBmNzU5MSZfc3M9cg==), but if you have kids you should especially read it. It’s one of my favorite parenting books in disguise.
[The Moviegoer](https://bc5923e4.click.convertkit-mail4.com/p9u97kol36u9h2w0qz4sqhpq2wp8rirhg8o6r/58hvh7hg8w96xqh6h4/aHR0cHM6Ly93d3cudGhlcGFpbnRlZHBvcmNoLmNvbS9wcm9kdWN0cy9tb3ZpZWdvZXI_X3Bvcz0xJl9zaWQ9OTZiMmY5NzI2Jl9zcz1y) by Walker Percy
This remains not only one of [my favorite novels](https://bc5923e4.click.convertkit-mail4.com/p9u97kol36u9h2w0qz4sqhpq2wp8rirhg8o6r/58hvh7hg8w96xqh6h4/aHR0cHM6Ly93d3cudGhlcGFpbnRlZHBvcmNoLmNvbS9wcm9kdWN0cy9tb3ZpZWdvZXI_X3Bvcz0xJl9zaWQ9OTZiMmY5NzI2Jl9zcz1y), but a genuine treat to read. On [my segment with Morning Joe](https://bc5923e4.click.convertkit-mail4.com/p9u97kol36u9h2w0qz4sqhpq2wp8rirhg8o6r/25h2hoh3zxom99s3h4/aHR0cHM6Ly93d3cueW91dHViZS5jb20vd2F0Y2g_dj0wUlZORjhPVDA1cw==) I explain it as a [The Catcher in the Rye](https://bc5923e4.click.convertkit-mail4.com/p9u97kol36u9h2w0qz4sqhpq2wp8rirhg8o6r/qvh8h7hd536kwpblhk/aHR0cHM6Ly93d3cudGhlcGFpbnRlZHBvcmNoLmNvbS9wcm9kdWN0cy9jYXRjaGVyLWluLXRoZS1yeWU_X3Bvcz0xJl9zaWQ9NDQ2NWFhYzQzJl9zcz1y), but for 30-year-olds. The main character, Binx Bolling, is a successful and seemingly well-adjusted young man in 1960s New Orleans. But he is also listless and empty, desperate for meaning and purpose. He hopes to get out of the “everydayness” of his life, and so he undertakes his “search,”…which is really just watching movies and dicking around. Stoicism doesn’t work. Neither does religion. So what will? What makes [this book](https://bc5923e4.click.convertkit-mail4.com/p9u97kol36u9h2w0qz4sqhpq2wp8rirhg8o6r/58hvh7hg8w96xqh6h4/aHR0cHM6Ly93d3cudGhlcGFpbnRlZHBvcmNoLmNvbS9wcm9kdWN0cy9tb3ZpZWdvZXI_X3Bvcz0xJl9zaWQ9OTZiMmY5NzI2Jl9zcz1y) so incredible is that Walker Percy is writing about deep and profound philosophical ideas in an accessible and shockingly modern way. We’re following Binx’s spiritual journey, but really it’s a reflection of the spiritual journey we’re all on. There are so many observations woven into this book that leave you thinking, “wow, I get that. I’ve experienced that, too.” This one is especially important for young men to read today. I did an hour-long interview exclusively talking about this book for The Free Press, [which you can check out here](https://bc5923e4.click.convertkit-mail4.com/p9u97kol36u9h2w0qz4sqhpq2wp8rirhg8o6r/g3hnh5hm52zq95brh9/aHR0cHM6Ly93d3cueW91dHViZS5jb20vd2F0Y2g_dj0yWG50cVptMWMtaw==).
[Address Unknown](https://bc5923e4.click.convertkit-mail4.com/p9u97kol36u9h2w0qz4sqhpq2wp8rirhg8o6r/3ohphkh3l8dpmeurhn/aHR0cHM6Ly93d3cudGhlcGFpbnRlZHBvcmNoLmNvbS9wcm9kdWN0cy9hZGRyZXNzLXVua25vd24_X3Bvcz0xJl9zaWQ9MjUxNzQ0MjAyJl9zcz1y) by Kathrine Kressmann Taylor
I first read [this book](https://bc5923e4.click.convertkit-mail4.com/p9u97kol36u9h2w0qz4sqhpq2wp8rirhg8o6r/3ohphkh3l8dpmeurhn/aHR0cHM6Ly93d3cudGhlcGFpbnRlZHBvcmNoLmNvbS9wcm9kdWN0cy9hZGRyZXNzLXVua25vd24_X3Bvcz0xJl9zaWQ9MjUxNzQ0MjAyJl9zcz1y) in 2019 (it was originally published in 1938), but I’ve been talking about this book a lot recently and have returned to it several times. It’s a haunting series of letters between two men, both art dealers in business together–one Jewish, one not–during the rise of Hitler in Germany. One is slowly corrupted by the events happening around him, his heart closing to the people and ideas he once believed in, to the point that it eventually overrides the love and affection he once had for his close friend. As a novel, it serves as a perfect, indirect way of capturing the process we have all seen over the last few years, where people we thought we knew, thought we understood, become radicalized (or indifferent) and thus complicit in truly evil things. It reveals the stakes, the risks, the subtle ways that people betray their values and lose their compassion. [This book could not be more relevant or more of a cautionary tale](https://bc5923e4.click.convertkit-mail4.com/p9u97kol36u9h2w0qz4sqhpq2wp8rirhg8o6r/n2hohvhvp4o7q7u6hg/aHR0cHM6Ly93d3cudGhlcGFpbnRlZHBvcmNoLmNvbS9wcm9kdWN0cy9hZGRyZXNzLXVua25vd24_X3Bvcz0xJl9zaWQ9ZjZiZDNhNGJmJl9zcz1y).
[Memoirs of Hadrian](https://bc5923e4.click.convertkit-mail4.com/p9u97kol36u9h2w0qz4sqhpq2wp8rirhg8o6r/wnh2hghqpdo07ds7hx/aHR0cHM6Ly93d3cudGhlcGFpbnRlZHBvcmNoLmNvbS9wcm9kdWN0cy9maWN0aW9uMjI_X3Bvcz0xJl9zaWQ9ZWI5ZDBhNTg5Jl9zcz1y) by Marguerite Yourcenar, [Augustus](https://bc5923e4.click.convertkit-mail4.com/p9u97kol36u9h2w0qz4sqhpq2wp8rirhg8o6r/reh8hohmrp56d8c2h6/aHR0cHM6Ly93d3cudGhlcGFpbnRlZHBvcmNoLmNvbS9wcm9kdWN0cy9hdWd1c3R1cy1uZXcteW9yay1yZXZpZXctYm9va3MtY2xhc3NpY3M_X3Bvcz0zJl9zaWQ9MjIwOTNjMGNmJl9zcz1y) by John Williams, and [The Last of the Wine](https://bc5923e4.click.convertkit-mail4.com/p9u97kol36u9h2w0qz4sqhpq2wp8rirhg8o6r/reh8hohmrp56d8c2h6/aHR0cHM6Ly93d3cudGhlcGFpbnRlZHBvcmNoLmNvbS9wcm9kdWN0cy9hdWd1c3R1cy1uZXcteW9yay1yZXZpZXctYm9va3MtY2xhc3NpY3M_X3Bvcz0zJl9zaWQ9MjIwOTNjMGNmJl9zcz1y) by Mary Renault
This is one of my favorite novels of all time. It’s written from the perspective of Emperor Hadrian to his successor, Marcus Aurelius, at the end of his life. Hadrian was a complicated leader, as all people drawn to power are, but this novel reveals how he was able to identify and cultivate not just one but two heirs who were much better than he was. While this story isn’t strictly true, it’s a novel that gets at the truth in a way that only fiction can. As I mentioned last month, [Memoirs of Hadrian](https://bc5923e4.click.convertkit-mail4.com/p9u97kol36u9h2w0qz4sqhpq2wp8rirhg8o6r/wnh2hghqpdo07ds7hx/aHR0cHM6Ly93d3cudGhlcGFpbnRlZHBvcmNoLmNvbS9wcm9kdWN0cy9maWN0aW9uMjI_X3Bvcz0xJl9zaWQ9ZWI5ZDBhNTg5Jl9zcz1y) would make a [great gift for recent grads](https://bc5923e4.click.convertkit-mail4.com/p9u97kol36u9h2w0qz4sqhpq2wp8rirhg8o6r/08hwh9h2547lr0ilh5/aHR0cHM6Ly9ja2FyY2hpdmUuY29tL2IvZ2t1bmg1aGw1bjQ2dml6b2RkOW9rdThvZTI0MGxibWgyZXZ3ZA==). Along similar lines, I was also bowled over by [Augustus](https://bc5923e4.click.convertkit-mail4.com/p9u97kol36u9h2w0qz4sqhpq2wp8rirhg8o6r/reh8hohmrp56d8c2h6/aHR0cHM6Ly93d3cudGhlcGFpbnRlZHBvcmNoLmNvbS9wcm9kdWN0cy9hdWd1c3R1cy1uZXcteW9yay1yZXZpZXctYm9va3MtY2xhc3NpY3M_X3Bvcz0zJl9zaWQ9MjIwOTNjMGNmJl9zcz1y) by John Williams and [The Last of the Wine](https://bc5923e4.click.convertkit-mail4.com/p9u97kol36u9h2w0qz4sqhpq2wp8rirhg8o6r/8ghqhohov85rn0akh9/aHR0cHM6Ly93d3cudGhlcGFpbnRlZHBvcmNoLmNvbS9wcm9kdWN0cy90aGUtbGFzdC1vZi10aGUtd2luZT9fcG9zPTEmX3NpZD1hMjMyZGYyOTUmX3NzPXI=) by Mary Renault. [Augustus](https://bc5923e4.click.convertkit-mail4.com/p9u97kol36u9h2w0qz4sqhpq2wp8rirhg8o6r/reh8hohmrp56d8c2h6/aHR0cHM6Ly93d3cudGhlcGFpbnRlZHBvcmNoLmNvbS9wcm9kdWN0cy9hdWd1c3R1cy1uZXcteW9yay1yZXZpZXctYm9va3MtY2xhc3NpY3M_X3Bvcz0zJl9zaWQ9MjIwOTNjMGNmJl9zcz1y) is basically the same premise as [Memoirs of Hadrian](https://bc5923e4.click.convertkit-mail4.com/p9u97kol36u9h2w0qz4sqhpq2wp8rirhg8o6r/vqh3hrho76kwrvbghl/aHR0cHM6Ly93d3cudGhlcGFpbnRlZHBvcmNoLmNvbS9wcm9kdWN0cy9maWN0aW9uMjI_X3Bvcz0xJl9zaWQ9NGY5NmU4Mjg5Jl9zcz1y), but about Augustus (who was quite frankly a much more interesting and impressive historical figure). [The Last of the Wine](https://bc5923e4.click.convertkit-mail4.com/p9u97kol36u9h2w0qz4sqhpq2wp8rirhg8o6r/8ghqhohov85rn0akh9/aHR0cHM6Ly93d3cudGhlcGFpbnRlZHBvcmNoLmNvbS9wcm9kdWN0cy90aGUtbGFzdC1vZi10aGUtd2luZT9fcG9zPTEmX3NpZD1hMjMyZGYyOTUmX3NzPXI=) is a coming-of-age novel of a young boy in Periclean Athens. Every few pages feature a delightful appearance of someone (Socrates, Plato, Euripides, Xenophon) or some place or battle you’ve heard of (Marathon, Sicily, Thrace, Battle of Munychia). These books are a reminder that the past was not a peaceful place, but as scary and uncertain and violent and cruel (as well as inspiring and creative and hopeful) as ours is today.
[The Oresteia: Agamemnon; The Libation Bearers; The Eumenides](https://bc5923e4.click.convertkit-mail4.com/p9u97kol36u9h2w0qz4sqhpq2wp8rirhg8o6r/l2hehmhl9pkx74s6h0/aHR0cHM6Ly93d3cudGhlcGFpbnRlZHBvcmNoLmNvbS9wcm9kdWN0cy90aGUtb3Jlc3RlaWEtYWdhbWVtbm9uLXRoZS1saWJhdGlvbi1iZWFyZXJzLXRoZS1ldW1lbmlkZXM_X3Bvcz0xJl9zaWQ9ODYxZDc5NDVmJl9zcz1y) by Aeschylus and [Medea and Other Plays](https://bc5923e4.click.convertkit-mail4.com/p9u97kol36u9h2w0qz4sqhpq2wp8rirhg8o6r/m2h7h5h3pxn4z0umhq/aHR0cHM6Ly93d3cudGhlcGFpbnRlZHBvcmNoLmNvbS9wcm9kdWN0cy9tZWRlYS1hbmQtb3RoZXItcGxheXMtcGVuZ3Vpbi1jbGFzc2ljcz9fcG9zPTEmX3NpZD01MDNlMTIxMDUmX3NzPXI=) by Euripides
I was honestly pissed the first time I read [The Oresteia](https://bc5923e4.click.convertkit-mail4.com/p9u97kol36u9h2w0qz4sqhpq2wp8rirhg8o6r/l2hehmhl9pkx74s6h0/aHR0cHM6Ly93d3cudGhlcGFpbnRlZHBvcmNoLmNvbS9wcm9kdWN0cy90aGUtb3Jlc3RlaWEtYWdhbWVtbm9uLXRoZS1saWJhdGlvbi1iZWFyZXJzLXRoZS1ldW1lbmlkZXM_X3Bvcz0xJl9zaWQ9ODYxZDc5NDVmJl9zcz1y) (a series of three plays which cover the murder of Agamemnon by his wife Clytemnestra, the revenge killing of Clytemnestra by her son Orestes and then finally the famous trial of him for that murder) because I had just published my book [Conspiracy](https://bc5923e4.click.convertkit-mail4.com/p9u97kol36u9h2w0qz4sqhpq2wp8rirhg8o6r/dpheh0hegx7pnmcmh4/aHR0cHM6Ly93d3cudGhlcGFpbnRlZHBvcmNoLmNvbS9wcm9kdWN0cy9yeWFuMTA_X3Bvcz0xJl9zaWQ9YTczNmE3ZTc2Jl9zcz1yJnZhcmlhbnQ9NDAzMDg0NjcwNDA0MTA=), which was also about revenge and the legal system. You’re telling me there is a famous Greek play literally about "The Furies” and about the establishment of the legal system (and a 12-person jury) to moderate and end the cycle of retribution? How did I not know this? Anyway, these plays are some of the greatest writing ever done. Now what about Euripides? I had read [Medea](https://bc5923e4.click.convertkit-mail4.com/p9u97kol36u9h2w0qz4sqhpq2wp8rirhg8o6r/m2h7h5h3pxn4z0umhq/aHR0cHM6Ly93d3cudGhlcGFpbnRlZHBvcmNoLmNvbS9wcm9kdWN0cy9tZWRlYS1hbmQtb3RoZXItcGxheXMtcGVuZ3Vpbi1jbGFzc2ljcz9fcG9zPTEmX3NpZD01MDNlMTIxMDUmX3NzPXI=) before, but that was before I had kids. It is a very different experience after…But I also read it now as effectively a play about a painful divorce and how easily one can go from loving to hating someone they were once so close to. But the play that struck me the hardest of all of these was Euripides' The Children of Hercules. Here we have, written in 430 BC, a play about the plight of the refugee, and how a society is judged by how it treats the weak and the vulnerable. The young children of Hercules are hounded to the Temple of Apollo (in Marathon, of course) by a bounty hunter from an angry king. He demands they be handed over to be punished. “They are suppliants and strangers,” the Athenians reply, “Who look to our city for help. / To reject them is to defy the gods.” But the angry king threatens war over it. In the play, Athens–like America, like Europe, now and forever–is torn. Sure, you say you care about people…but would you fight for them? What would you risk for them? This is incredibly moving given our current political climate and I wish I would have referenced this in my [now-viral clip about U.S. immigration and deportations](https://bc5923e4.click.convertkit-mail4.com/p9u97kol36u9h2w0qz4sqhpq2wp8rirhg8o6r/e0hph7h7gznrons8h2/aHR0cHM6Ly93d3cuaW5zdGFncmFtLmNvbS9yZWVsL0RGTE1udzB4Z1ktLw==). It’s interesting to me which characters jump out at us at different points of our life. Obviously, Jim in [Huckleberry Finn](https://bc5923e4.click.convertkit-mail4.com/p9u97kol36u9h2w0qz4sqhpq2wp8rirhg8o6r/x0hph6he6m7q03i5hl/aHR0cHM6Ly93d3cudGhlcGFpbnRlZHBvcmNoLmNvbS9wcm9kdWN0cy90aGUtYWR2ZW50dXJlcy1vZi1odWNrbGViZXJyeS1maW5uP19wb3M9MSZfc2lkPTE5MzY4OWYxNiZfc3M9cg==) was presented a certain way by Mark Twain (and by centuries of racism).
