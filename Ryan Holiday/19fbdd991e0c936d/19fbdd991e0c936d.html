Not much has happened since we chatted last…except that we opened a Banned Book Library next to The Painted Porch. We had this extra space in the building next door and several months ago when I heard a librarian in Texas was fired for reading one of my kids’ favorite books, I Need a New Butt (here’s the backstory), I decided to put it to good use. Some of the comments have been absolutely insane to me. Apparently if a book is available anywhere, it’s not actually banned? Tell that to the librarian who got fired?! It’s a nonsensical argument. In the antebellum south, Uncle Tom’s Cabin was banned and criminalized. The fact that you could still buy it in Boston doesn’t cancel that out! And it’s still happening! In 2024, the ACLU had to file a lawsuit to restore access to 1984 in a Colorado school district! It would be funny if it wasn’t true. Just last year, the U.S. Naval Academy removed 400 books from its library for being “too woke.” My talk on wisdom was cancelled because I was going to say that that was stupid (I wrote a whole NYT article about it). So if you’re passing along any of these tropes or rationalizations, just know you are complicit. If you’d like to be part of the solution, send a book to the library (912 Main St., Bastrop, TX) or just come say hello. See you there…(or in Minneapolis, Chicago, Detroit, New Zealand, Australia, Boston, Philadelphia, Nashville, and Washington, D.C. on my speaking tour starting next month).

​Close to Shore: The Terrifying Shark Attacks of 1916 by Michael Capuzzo
​I think I finally did it–found a book that I can rave about like I have with The Tiger and Night of the Grizzlies! And this one could not have come from more absurd circumstances. My youngest son picked out a book about shark attacks at a bookstore (it was one of what we call the “big head” books in our family, titled What Were the 1916 Shark Attacks?). My wife read it to him and then–and I mean this in the most loving way possible–she just would not shut up about it. She was telling everyone she talked to about this series of shark attacks in 1916 as if she’d read a real book about it and not something meant for children! Thankfully somebody pointed us to Capuzzo’s book which is not just for adults but features some absolutely incredible writing (I do not make the comparisons to The Tiger or Night of the Grizzlies lightly but it’s that good). The reason Samantha was hooked even from a kid’s book version is that these shark attacks were insane (a rogue Great White killed four people on the coast of the Jersey shore over a 12-day span, the first known attacks on swimmers in the U.S.)–and the characters are unreal (some millionaire friend of Theodore Roosevelt would jump off his yacht and swim with sharks, convinced they were harmless). The original subtitle of the book was “A True Story of Terror in an Age of Innocence” which, I think, better gets to the essence of what makes this book so great. At the turn of the century, people were just coming out of the Victorian era and rediscovering the beach and swimming. They simply did not believe that sharks killed people…just as they could not conceive of the horrors of the First World War which was just then beginning. This book gives you not just an incredible story but really puts you inside a world that no longer exists. If you liked Tunnel 29 or Shadow Divers or any of the narrative nonfiction books I’ve raved about, trust me on this one.

​Under the Jaguar Sun by Italo Calvino
​It’s unreal how good this little book is and that’s why I am recommending it again despite talking about it just last week. It was originally designed to be a series of essays/explorations of the five senses. The first one–on sight–is probably the greatest travel piece you will ever, ever read. The second essay, about the perspective of a king, is one of the most moving meditations of power and fame that I think has ever been produced. I could imagine Marcus Aurelius reading it and weeping. I had that exact thought as I walked down “the emperor’s shortcut,” a hidden, 300-yard passageway of zigzagging ramps that carried the emperor from the palace on the Palatine down to the Forum, safely separated from the streets of Rome. The last time we were in Rome–back in 2014–this wasn’t open to the public and now it is. But because of what I’d read–both from Calvino and from Meditations–I was able to see this lonely corridor with eyes that I don’t think the rest of the tourists could. Just an incredible book–and one of the only times I’ve read something and immediately needed to reread it to make sure I got it all.

​The Time of Cherries by Montserrat Roig
​In one of Admiral Stavridis’ books–I forget which one but I liked Sailing True North and To Risk it All–he talked about how as the Supreme Allied Commander of NATO, he tried to read one novel from each of the 28 countries in the alliance at the time. I’m not exactly trying to do the same thing but it did spur me to try to read more international fiction. I read You Be Mother from Meg Mason in Australia a few months ago. I’ve been reading Claire Keegan (see below). I loved Italo Calvino’s book (see above). When I heard Anne Applebaum recommend The Time of Cherries (set at the end of Franco’s reign), I thought it sounded like the right book for me because it checked not only my international fiction box but my “The best way to understand the present is to look at an analogous moment in the past.” How does a society accommodate itself to fascism? (rationalization, busyness) What does it do to your soul? (degrades) How does ordinary, daily life change (not as much as you think). The author, Montserrat Roig, was a Catalan writer, activist and journalist who grew up under Franco's dictatorship and spent her career exploring the moral compromises and injustices of the era.

​The Forester's Daughter by Claire Keegan
​Is Claire Keegan capable of writing a bad story? The answer is obviously no. Perhaps more up for debate is whether she can write a happy story (the fact that Small Things Like These is probably the happiest says a lot!) You’ll notice the link above does not go to The Forester’s Daughter, but to her collection Walk the Blue Fields. We found a lovely little edition of only The Forester’s Daughter when we were in Dublin, but it’s not available in America. You’ll only be able to find it in this collection of other stories but that’s a good thing, right? Because it means more Claire Keegan for you to read! I also love So Late in the Day and Foster. Like I said before, nothing gets you out of a reading rut like reading something short.

​My Reading Life by Pat Conroy
​When I flew from Atlanta to San Francisco in March to do my chat with General Mattis on Firing Line (you can listen here), a lady was reading this a couple of seats over. I’m not saying that’s how I find out about a lot of the books I read but it’s been more than a few. I emailed the manager of The Painted Porch and it was waiting for me when I got home. The book reminded me of another very similar book, Louis L’Amour’s Education of a Wandering Man. (It also reminded me to reread The Great Santini) The stories are lovely and inspire you to read more. Pat Conroy’s reading life covered everything from Milton to Tolkien, Philip Roth to Thucydides. He read poetry, he read history, he read philosophy. It’s a perfect reminder that reading is more than just something to do for pleasure. Every time you pick up a book, you take something from it. A lesson on how to live, how to learn, how to understand the past and prepare for the future, how to be a better person.

Misc
​I’m seeing The Odyssey movie this afternoon, which I’ve been eagerly anticipating. Watching some of the right-wing criticism about it has been fascinating because Seneca predicted it 2,000 years ago as I explained in this video (actually we also did a deep dive into a bunch of lessons from The Odyssey here and we are finishing up our Quarterly Classics Book Club on the Emily Wilson translation if you want to join us). I have always liked Eugen Herrigel's Zen in the Art of Archery (as well as Herrigel’s wife’s book Zen in the Art of Flower Arranging) even though I knew he was problematic (a German studying in Japan in 1929…) Someone sent me Arthur Koestler’s very critical essay about Zen in the Art of Archery, which I read with great enthusiasm because I loved Koestler's Darkness at Noon. He is basically convinced that Herrigel's book is complete nonsense and I have to say, he makes some good points. I still like the book but it gave me a lot to think about. (Which is how reading is supposed to work. It’s good to read from people you disagree with or who disagree with you!)

Kids
​My son was assigned to read (or for us to read as a family) Judy Blume’s Tale of a Fourth Grade Nothing. We’ve been making our way through it and I have to say when we got to the end–spoilers!–and Fudge ate the turtle, it was mayhem. And it hit me particularly hard because I remember being 8 or 9 and having the same reaction as him when I got to that point. Then yesterday at a birthday party for some of the kids at school, I asked if any of the kids had finished the book yet. They went crazy! I can’t believe Fudge ate the turtle! The girls all took Sheila’s side in the dispute about the group project. The kids all thought that Dad was a mess. It was just two lovely, lovely moments. That’s what books do. My seven-year-old read and loved Noodles Conquers Comfy Mountain by Jonathan Graziano. He also liked Call Me Moby by Lars Kenseth. And then we did some rereading of Floof and Shmoof by Heidi McKinnon, which are family favorites.
