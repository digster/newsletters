​The Reading List: August 2026​

It’s been said that books are “uniquely portable magic” but, believe it or not, I think that understates it. I had an incredible experience this month that just reminded me of all the reasons I love to read and how reading enriches a life in almost unimaginable and inexpressible ways. I had just landed in Minneapolis for the first of three talks in my little tour (come see me in Boston, Philly, Nashville, D.C., Australia and New Zealand here) and I walked by an indigenous restaurant called Indígena at the Guthrie Theater right next to my hotel. It sounded interesting so I walked in and ordered and pulled the book I had started on the plane out of my bag: Death Comes for the Archbishop by Willa Cather. I’ve found that when you read old famous novels you find one of two things: they are either totally unreadable, or they are so readable you can’t stand it. This book is incredible and sitting there reading it while I ate bison bone marrow was a nearly transcendent experience. It was like traveling through time and world. Written in 1927 about a bishop in 1848 dispatched to the American Southwest to oversee an enormous diocese of tens of thousands of square miles after the Mexican-American War, the book is slow–there are no huge action scenes, but every page is somehow riveting and beautiful. It’s got buffalo and corrupt priests. It’s got cathedrals and canyons. It’s got primordial underground rivers and Kit Carson. And the huge sweep of history and territory is breathtaking–it starts in Rome with these cardinals deciding which missionary to send, even to get there was incredible. The bishop is starting in Ohio and well, just look at this paragraph: “No one in Cincinnati could tell him how to get to New Mexico no one had ever been there. Since young Father Latour's arrival in America, a railroad had been built through from New York to Cincinnati; but there it ended. New Mexico lay in the middle of a dark continent. The Ohio merchants knew of two routes only. One was the Santa Fe trail from St. Louis, but at that time it was very dangerous because of Comanche Indian raids. His friends advised Father Latour to go down the river to New Orleans, thence by boat to Galveston, across Texas to San Antonio, and to wind up into New Mexico along the Rio Grande valley. This he had done, but with what misadventures!” Anyway, one of my favorite parts of reading this book was the mention of some church in New Mexico which I looked up and found was built in the 1600s. In 1976 it was added to the National History Register…one year before my house, which, I assure you, is of slightly less historic significance and slightly newer. Life is weird. Reading makes it wonderfully weirder. In any case, come see me on tour this fall and check out some of these books because they are incredible.

​History of the Peloponnesian War by Thucydides
​Samantha and I chose this for our Quarterly Classics Book Club and it’s been a form of time travel for me. Not just because Thucydides is a great writer who transports you to the three-decade-long war between Athens and Sparta but because I first read this book roughly 20 years ago and I still have my copy. It is crazy to look at notes I wrote at that age…We never step in the same river twice (although I am amazed at how consistently the same passages still strike me). But of course, the craziest thing about Thucydides is this sentence from the introduction: “My work is not a piece of writing designed to meet the taste of an immediate public, but was done to last forever.” And here we are, talking about his book 2500 years later. You did it dude! The book could not be more relevant either. Two enormous powers, one established, one rising, stumbling into war, not so much deliberately but as a result of a number of tertiary conflicts…how does that happen? Well, we should probably figure it out because we’re on the verge of doing it again! In any case, one of the thoughts I have now, reading the book after our trip all over Greece is What were they fighting over?! The population of Greece today is roughly the same as it was then. It seems like there is plenty of room for everybody! But again, I guess that’s the stupidity of war.

​Yesteryear by Caro Claire Burke
​Ok, I have to ask, was the author rage-baiting us with this book, like her character did with social media posts? Because if so, bravo! I’ve talked about this before but one of my theories on a lot of the popular modern fiction these days is that the authors are very skilled at plot devices and characters (fans sometimes refer to this as ‘tropes’ but in a non-negative way) but generally pretty mid at writing. That’s what I thought about this. Clearly it’s a very clever plot (it’s about a trad-wife with millions of social media followers who wakes up one day in 1855 and is forced to live the life she’s been selling online) and it nailed the moment or it would not have gotten all the attention it did. Was there one memorable sentence in this entire book? Did the author do like one minute of research? As someone who has large social media followings, lives on a ranch, knows some political families and has in fact met some of the people (and people like them) that the book is based on, I would say “You couldn’t tell.” In fact, it mostly feels like the opposite. Yet, I can’t stop talking/debating the book with people. Samantha read it first and was ranting to me about it and then decided to stop because I needed to read it myself so we could do it together. So…mission accomplished.

​Magnifica Humanitas: On Safeguarding the Human Person in the Time of Artificial Intelligence by Pope Leo XIV
We were checking out of a bookstore in Rome and they had copies of this at the counter. Earlier this year, Pope Leo released his first encyclical—the traditional papal letter to the whole Church–and he chose to write it about artificial intelligence. Obviously I’d read some of the coverage about it but I wasn’t planning on reading the whole thing. But seeing it as a little book, I grabbed it and you know what, Bob from Chicago can write! Here, he is more eloquent and philosophical about technology than any of the reporters or pundits I’ve read over the last several years. I think his conceit is a good one: When it comes to AI, are we building a new Tower of Babel–an act of pride that ends in ruin? Or are we building Nehemiah’s Wall–a collaborative, human project that serves and protects us all? The Pope is honest about the fact that this technology is neither evil nor neutral. It takes on the character of whoever builds it, funds it, and uses it. Which is all of us. Also I put together a video of books I think are extra relevant in this new world of AI that you might like.

​All the Pretty Horses by Cormac McCarthy
​I must be due for a Southwest road trip or something, I’m not sure why I was so drawn to books on this part of the country recently. Maybe it was after I re-read The Road that I decided to re-read All the Pretty Horses? It turns out my father-in-law and brother-in-law were also reading it at the same time. There’s just something immensely soothing to me about this book, even though some of the subject matter is horrifying. It must be–like in Death Comes for the Archbishop–the rhythm of the sentences. Maybe it’s the old-fashioned values too? My favorite passage, however, is the one I quoted in Courage Is Calling: “The world wants to know if you have cojones. If you are brave?”

​Madhouse at the End of the Earth: The Belgica's Journey into the Dark Antarctic Night by Julian Sancton
As I’ve said before, I don’t like boats but I love shipwreck books. It’s hard to beat some of my favorites like Endurance, Dead Wake and A Night to Remember. Oh and Robert Kurson who wrote Shadow Divers and Pirate Hunters came out to my talk in Chicago. So yeah, this one didn’t match those. But it was still pretty good! It’s about a Belgian expedition to Antarctica in the 1890s that gets trapped in the ice, forcing the crew to spend months in total darkness through the Antarctic winter. Once you exhaust the other shipwreck books I’ve recommended, or even The Wager (which I liked, but didn’t love) give this book a look.

​Train Dreams by Denis Johnson
​Nothing like a short book to cleanse your palate or restore your momentum as a reader. As I’ve said, Claire Keegan’s books are perfect for this–Small Things Like These, Foster, So Late in the Day, Walk the Blue Fields. I don’t know how I had never heard of this one, but I loved it and read through it in a few sittings. It’s got a Cormac McCarthy vibe to it, I guess, but somehow a little sweeter and more earnest? It follows Robert Grainier, a laborer working in the railroad and logging industries in the Pacific Northwest, as he navigates personal grief and the rapidly changing landscape of early twentieth-century America.

Misc
​On Bill Maher earlier this month, he asked me what book I think every American should read. My answer? The Great Gatsby. Why? There are so many layers to this book. The first time you read it, you understand it’s about the American dream. But then you read it again and you realize it’s also about the careless behavior and indifference of the rich. With every read (as I explain here), you walk away with something different yet equally profound. I especially loved this 100th-anniversary edition that came out recently. I may go read it again. I mentioned road trips earlier, I also read and liked Peter Santenello’s book Your Fellow Americans. He was on The Daily Stoic podcast and I thought the episode was great too (listen to that here). Also at my talk in San Francisco back in June, I told a story that connected to another one of my favorite novels, Tom Wolfe’s A Man in Full. If you remember the “saddlebags” scene with the pit stains? Well, that happened to me at my first (and worst) ever appearance in SF. And BTW the restaurant I went to in Minneapolis–Indígena–was so good I went back for dinner that same night with Joey Coleman and his family. His book Never Lose a Customer Again is a great read as well.

Kids
​After reading Tales of a 4th Grade Nothing, my oldest and I moved on to Double Fudge. I am trying to get him to read Summer of the Monkeys next, but we’ll see. He’s currently hooked on the Pluto Rocket series of graphic novels and he just finished all the Pocket Peaches books, too. I know some parents are against graphic novels but that’s stupid. Reading is reading. Get them what they are excited about and interested in. My youngest is liking what we’ve been calling the “Big Head” series, especially the one about Atlantis. And they are both very excited to hear the news that there will be a National Treasure 3 movie coming in the next few years. I wish someone would turn that into a series of novels for kids (or adults). But honestly, our main family time this month has been spent playing Goldeneye on Nintendo 64. We bought a vintage console (yes, the thing I saved up my money to buy when I was their age is not an antique) and have had so much fun together on it. The graphics are so bad–especially when stretched out on a modern TV–that I don’t even know if it counts as violent. If you’re looking for something fun to do with your kids, try this. I’m serious.
