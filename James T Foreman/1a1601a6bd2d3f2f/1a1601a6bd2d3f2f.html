A Dialogue on Mystical Experiences

weaving through the woo-woo

James Taylor Foreman and A. A. Kostas
Oct 21
 
READ IN APP
 

I’ve had many experiences in my life that can only be described as ‘mystical’ or ‘supernatural’. But it can be difficult to talk about these experiences without sounding insane or woo-woo or terribly self-involved.

Fortunately, I’m not stuck trying to figure this out on my own. I’ve asked the ever-insightful James Taylor Foreman to help me untangle the nebulous knot of mystical experiences. Below is our dialogue, a format I much prefer to the ‘podcast’.

AAK
Okay, James. We’ve both had what we would call ‘mystical experiences’, and while it can feel tricky to write about them to a wider audience, my guess is they aren’t as unusual as we think. If we broaden the definition of ‘mystic’ just a bit, we find that many people have had an experience they cannot quite explain, that doesn’t quite align with rational materialism (i.e. seeing ghosts, hearing voices, prophetic dreams, etc.). To my mind, there are two ways this happens:

  1. You’re in a heightened state of emotion or altered brain chemistry which makes you more perceptive of non-rational occurrences.

  1. You are intensely focused and in the flow of an activity or meditation or contemplation so that your mind is very quiet, and you are caught off guard by something you’ve never seen before.

What do you think? Are mystical experiences actually commonplace and we just filter them out because we don’t have a good way of understanding them? Or are there different categories of mystical experience and some are more meaningful than others? And how do these personal experiences all fit into more mainstream religion?

JTF
I’ve also had a few of those ‘unexplainable’ experiences, which I’ll overview briefly. But before I do, I want to say that I liked that you were careful about keeping it to the facts and not leaping right into blind fanaticism about supernatural experiences. This sort of discussion so easily falls into that, which I find unbearable. Even though I don’t think the empirical framework is the ultimate framework, I do think it’s the most conducive to keeping things on the rails, and to allowing us to find the limits of what seems reasonable. That is exactly what we find so interesting about mystical experiences in the first place, anyway.

The first ‘mystical’ experience I can remember was a half-brain seizure when I was about twelve. The left hemisphere of my brain, I later deduced, shut off. I lost all linguistic and symbolic capacity and was reduced to a pre-linguistic observer, which is difficult to even imagine. It scared the hell out of me.

The second big mystical experience was chemically induced, right after college. I got convinced by the Tim Ferriss and Sam Harris podcast people. At the time there was a new psychedelic movement and it all seemed very legitimate and scientific to me, with research being done by Roland Griffiths and his sort. So, with some friends, I took mushrooms in an Airbnb in Austin. Sadly, I had a mostly terrifying experience that also scared the hell out of me. It didn’t really lead to any interesting insights or positive changes. The reason for this, I think, has less to do with the fact that an artificially induced mystical experience isn’t pragmatic, generally.

To illustrate what I mean, I want to define ‘mystical experience’. For me, the best definition is this: a mystical experience is the experience of certainty of belief. Under this umbrella, all first-person human experience is technically mystical experience. The greater the certainty of belief, the greater the experience of what people call ‘the face of God’ or ‘unity’ or ‘flow’. It takes on different character and timbre depending on the suddenness or gradualness of onset and the level of the certainty.

What do you think of that?

AAK
Wow okay. That definitely broadens the definition of ‘mystical experience’ beyond how I usually think of it. I’ve experienced unity with a group of people or with nature, have been in flow states when I’m writing or snowboarding or playing sports, and have had many other spiritual experiences which fit more squarely in a religious category such as visions and prophetic dreams and so on (which we can discuss later).

My concern with your working definition is that if everything is mystical then nothing is. Doesn’t this render the terminology of ‘mystical’ or ‘spiritual’ or ‘supernatural’ essentially useless?

JTF
I agree that practically it makes sense to have a divide between ‘normal’ states of consciousness and ‘mystical’ ones. But, ultimately, that divide blurs. Understanding why, I think, can help us get a better grip on what exactly is going on.

For example, a flow state is a low-level mystical experience. But it’s one where your actual behaviors are driving you toward a greater sense of belief that your current orientation will take you where you want to go. I like these mystical experiences a lot, because they demand real-life pragmatism. The feedback of the actual environment needs to tell you, in real time, that what you’re doing is working. You could suppose that a perfect flow experience would be a feeling of heaven on earth, where all of your beliefs take you, without fail, exactly to your intended outcome with the right amount of effort. That’s probably never going to happen 100%, but you can approach it more and more closely.

What makes normal, everyday life feel mundane (i.e. not like what you’d ordinarily define as a ‘mystical experience’) is that our consciousness is usually crowded with doubt. Much of it is correct doubt: the recognition that you believe things that don’t take you where you want to go. This is happening at many levels, across many people, all the time. In fact, most of what you ‘see’ in normal consciousness is just the memory of what you expect to see, based on what you believe. This has been shown in some detail by researchers. The fact that you ‘see’ strange images, geometry, and distortions of reality during a very extreme mystical experience shouldn’t confuse us on the practical definition, either. You are always ‘seeing’ what isn’t exactly there. Mystical experiences just give you a glimpse under your own hood, so to speak.

In normal life, if you learn to pay attention to your latent doubts, and suffer them, you can become the sort of person who can not only withstand doubt but also hold a kind of open resting awareness that may find opportunities for higher beliefs.

AAK
Right, so the gap between the mystical and the mundane is how much doubt we hold? That kind of makes sense to me. The sense of peace or thrill that comes from a mystical experience is how unified everything feels, how certain you are about the reality of the entire cosmos.

And I also agree with what you seem to be saying, which is that learning to accept and integrate doubt into our lives is a necessary ingredient for maturity. So how do these things go together? I don’t think either of us advocate for expunging ourselves of doubt and giving into pure fanaticism…

JTF
Yes, exactly. That’s my problem with psychedelics.

Another way of looking at doubt and certainty: doubt is like entropy and belief is like order. That might sound like I’m saying all doubt, like entropy, is bad, and all belief, like order, is good. But that kind of statement is itself a belief that doesn’t work. Sometimes you can have a false belief, such as a delusion or denial of reality. It feels good, but it doesn’t take you where you want to go. And sometimes you can have a true doubt: a right realization that things are more chaotic than you’d like them to be, and that you don’t yet see how it all might resolve. This is the very broad sketch of what it feels like to be a mortal being.

Psychedelics mechanically induce feelings of extreme certainty that have little or nothing to do with actual circumstances. So when I came out of that heightened state, I almost couldn’t deal with the legions of unexamined doubt, manifesting as all the various mundane problems in my life.

AAK
Yeah, I’ve never taken any kind of psychedelics and they don’t appeal to me. It does seem like a false kind of ‘leap-frogging’ whereby you hijack yourself to experience things you may not yet be ready for. Also, psychedelic experiences seem extremely isolating in how individualised they are, down to the fact that you are the one inducing it by taking the drug. Whereas every mystical experience I’ve ever had has been a relational experience: I’ve been communing with an Other, I’ve been invited to glimpse something more because it’s the right time and place for me to do so.

I’m not sure if a scientist would agree with me, given that all a psychedelic does is adjust certain chemicals in your brain, but that’s been my personal experience thus far. What do you think?

JTF
Ultimately, you have to figure out what all scientific findings have to do with you, in particular. What science is, in essence, is one particular scientist finding predictable patterns, at various levels of analysis, and reporting them to the rest of us. What it doesn’t include is a moral framework, which is just, very plainly: what does this have to do with me? That, you have to figure out entirely on your own. To me, this is why the question of whether a mystical experience is “real” becomes meaningless at the deepest levels. The deepest level is always this: across the landscape of all possible frameworks of information, every single person has to figure out for themselves what to do about them. We each have to make for ourselves a pattern of true beliefs, attitudes for assessing beliefs, and ways to manage doubt, which never goes away.

AAK
I like that a lot. This is what I mean when I say things like ‘everything is moral’, because we all act according to our personal morals. And this is how I wish everyone would engage with the seeming ‘divide’ between the material world and non-material spirituality. It’s an intensely personal question, which you keep grappling with the longer you live. Even if you entirely accept the tenets of a certain religion, you still have to decide how to live your life and what religious beliefs you should apply in your daily decisions.

I’d like to apply this framework to something more specific. If, as you say, we each have to “figure out for ourselves what to do” with our experiences, beliefs, and received wisdom (including the wisdom of the scientists), then how do you personally deal with the question of things like ghosts or magic or the occult? These seem to be a major focus by many people, both inside and outside formal religions.

JTF
This is an ongoing question for me. I feel I can almost explain it in a few ways: It seems like every person has a pattern of intention that interacts with many other people’s lives. Even when they die, that pattern doesn’t dissipate. What they wanted can still exert some will on you. Beyond that, the choices they made in their lives changed the outlooks of other people, altered the physical environment with traces and hints and subconscious cues. Ghosts almost certainly aren’t corporeal because you can’t measure one like the width of a two-by-four. But you can still infer how they change behavior. I’ve never personally been susceptible to seeing ghosts, but I know people who have experienced things like demonic possession and exorcism, and I trust those people’s judgment. So as far as that goes, I say: who knows?

As for manifestation and magic and the occult: it seems clear to me that your intention can subtly change the behaviors of everybody who could possibly interact with you. Just like when you focus on getting a basketball into a hoop, all of your hormones and chemicals and neurons and organs cooperate with that pattern to achieve that end. Even in basketball, that’s true at levels much deeper and more subtle than we can really know about. For larger networks like culture and religions, the knock-on effects are innumerable. As far as calling that magic, I’m ambivalent. You can call it magic if you want, because you can’t go in and identify every single causal interaction that participates in the pattern without killing the pattern. You might as well give the pattern itself a name—magic, if you like, or manifestation, or prayer. Some of these practices help you find true beliefs better than others. That’s a story for another day.

AAK
Yeah I’ve never seen ghosts, though I have had a few experiences with darker spiritual forces. I won’t go into too much detail, but one experience happened while I was at a religious retreat and had a night of strange visions and dreams. One of those episodes involved a demon stalking the grounds of the retreat centre and repeatedly throwing itself against my bedroom window and then being dragged away, like a mad dog on a leash. Another similar experience was when I came down with a viral fever while travelling and I had a vision of a small green snake sitting on my shoulder, whispering horrible things into my ear. In both experiences, these dark forces eventually withdrew after I verbally stated some simple phrase of rebuke and focused instead on the light of God (during my viral fever, my brother and friends came across me lying on a deck chair repeating the phrase “piss off Satan, piss off Satan”).

Re your definition of ‘magic’ or ‘manifestation’, that does basically make sense to me. People and networks of people are susceptible to manipulation, for good and for ill. It reminds me of all the talk about ‘being agentic’ in the sense that it really is possible to bend the world to your will, but that doesn’t necessarily mean it will be good for the world (or for you).

How about positive spiritual experiences? Have you had these?

JTF
What you say rings true. Most of the people I know who have had mystical experiences have had very negative ones. I’ve seen visions of snakes and swastikas and demons myself. I think those forces tend to produce the most lucid experiences of supernatural power. Maybe because they are interested in convincing people that they are ‘real’. But I find this often backfires: I know a lot of religious people who have seen visions of true evil and it made them realize that if that is real, then God must also be real.

I mostly have positive mystical experiences when I’m reading a good book or in a movie theater or having a good conversation or walking outside. They are very delicate and go away with the slightest whiff of wanting more of it. They don’t need or want you to believe in them as ‘more real’ than reality because God is really Being itself and so our mundane struggles with doubt and belief are where all the good stuff lies. Which is also why I am very suspicious of psychedelics.

What about your positive experiences? Anything as vivid as the snake?

AAK
Oh yeah I like this recognition that darker/negative spiritual experiences seem to insist upon themselves, whereas lighter/positive experiences are more effervescent and disappear when we grasp for them. And that psychedelics aren’t to be trusted because they are a way of forcefully holding onto and constructing what may feel like a ‘positive’ spiritual experience.

I’ve had lots of wonderful experiences of rapturous worship or praise and feeling ‘caught up’ with God, which are vivid with a sense of God’s awesomeness or His overpowering love. But the most tangible experiences are strange occurrences which could be chalked up to coincidence: clear signs, mysterious help, the sense of being led to a certain place or person. Which I think is that ‘sweet spot’ you’re talking about regarding mundaneity and doubt, because you can always find some way to explain them away if you wanted to.

For example: a few years ago my wife and I were on an island in a foreign country with no money for the ferry back to our hotel on the mainland (my fault, I hadn’t brought enough cash that day). The ferry terminal only took cash, and none of the ATMs on the island were working. We had tried everything we could think of, and were sitting by the side of the road in despair when a Muslim man and his wife came out of nowhere and gave us some cash and then walked away into the crowd. We weren’t obviously begging for money and we couldn’t speak this man’s language, but somehow they knew we needed money and they gave it to us as an act of charity. It felt impossible, it still sounds impossible, and yet it really did happen. And we knew were being cared for by the hands of God.

Is there anything else you think needs to be said about mystical experiences? Do you find any tension with fitting these intensely personal experiences into a prescribed religious framework?

JTF
Great story. I like how you pointed out that the possibility of doubting it as a miracle is part of how these things always tend to manifest. It’s like they don’t want to be seen out in the open, if you know what I mean. Maybe that’s why explaining dreams or synchronicities to people always falls flat. It only makes sense in first-person.

As far as putting these ideas, hazy as they sometimes are, into my larger religious framework, no, I don’t find any tension. I find that it fits well into Catholic sensibilities. Better than most, really. The Catholics seem unique in their ability to absorb and orient pagan myths and value structures into the monomyth, to use Campbell’s term. Cajuns in Louisiana have faith-healing “traiteurs” (my great uncle was one), and of course the South American churches are full of miracles, Marian apparitions, and images of their old gods. There is a lot of room for play in the Church. Reminds me of my priest’s favorite bit from Chesterton:

“We might fancy some children playing on the flat grassy top of some tall island in the sea. So long as there was a wall round the cliff’s edge they could fling themselves into every frantic game and make the place the noisiest of nurseries. But the walls were knocked down, leaving the naked peril of the precipice. They did not fall over; but when their friends returned to them they were all huddled in terror in the centre of the island; and their song had ceased.”

AAK
Yeah that’s a great quote which shows the necessity of some kind of framework to understand all the weird and wonderful and sometimes terrifying ways the world really works. It needs to be open enough to the unexplainable, but not wholly lacking in boundaries or we are in danger of flying off the edge of the cliff.

I’ve really enjoyed this dialogue, and if I had to sum up what I’m taking away it would be:

  1. Much of our lives can qualify as ‘mystical’, as a mystical experience is the experience of certainty of belief.

  1. The gap between the mystical and the mundane is how much doubt we hold, but the best way to live isn’t erasing all doubt, but instead learning how to discern and integrate doubt into our lived experiences.

  1. How you integrate your own experience of the inexplicable with the material facts of the world is an intensely personal question, even if you accept the structures of an organised religion, and it’s vital that you seek an answer to that question.

  1. ‘Negative’ mystical experiences tend to insist upon their own importance, whereas ‘positive’ mystical experiences tend to be experienced as an effervescent gift, difficult to pin down.

  1. If God is Being itself, then our doubts and what we do with those doubts in everyday life are more important any ‘special’ mystical experience.

JTF
Agreed. Thanks for the great conversation. Lots to think about here.

Do our experiences and thinking align with your own ‘spiritual’ or ‘mystical’ or ‘unexplainable’ experiences? Let us know in the comments, as this is something we’re both interested in understanding beyond our own perspectives…
