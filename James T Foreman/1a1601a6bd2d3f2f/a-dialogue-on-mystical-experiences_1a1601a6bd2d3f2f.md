I’ve had many experiences in my life that can only be described as ‘mystical’ or ‘supernatural’. But it can be difficult to talk about these experiences without sounding insane or woo-woo or terribly self-involved. Fortunately, I’m not stuck trying to figure this out on my own. I’ve asked the ever-insightful AAK You’re in a heightened state of emotion or altered brain chemistry which makes you more perceptive of non-rational occurrences.
You are intensely focused and in the flow of an activity or meditation or contemplation so that your mind is very quiet, and you are caught off guard by something you’ve never seen before.
What do you think? Are mystical experiences actually commonplace and we just filter them out because we don’t have a good way of understanding them? Or are there different categories of mystical experience and some are more meaningful than others? And how do these personal experiences all fit into more mainstream religion? ***note: the rest of this article is ‘free-walled’, which means the dialogue will appear below after you take out a free or paid subscription to Waymarkers.*** JTF The first ‘mystical’ experience I can remember was a half-brain seizure when I was about twelve. The left hemisphere of my brain, I later deduced, shut off. I lost all linguistic and symbolic capacity and was reduced to a pre-linguistic observer, which is difficult to even imagine. It scared the hell out of me. The second big mystical experience was chemically induced, right after college. I got convinced by the Tim Ferriss and Sam Harris podcast people. At the time there was a new psychedelic movement and it all seemed very legitimate and scientific to me, with research being done by Roland Griffiths and his sort. So, with some friends, I took mushrooms in an Airbnb in Austin. Sadly, I had a mostly terrifying experience that also scared the hell out of me. It didn’t really lead to any interesting insights or positive changes. The reason for this, I think, has less to do with the fact that an artificially induced mystical experience isn’t pragmatic, generally. To illustrate what I mean, I want to define ‘mystical experience’. For me, the best definition is this: a mystical experience is the experience of certainty of belief. Under this umbrella, all first-person human experience is technically mystical experience. The greater the certainty of belief, the greater the experience of what people call ‘the face of God’ or ‘unity’ or ‘flow’. It takes on different character and timbre depending on the suddenness or gradualness of onset and the level of the certainty. What do you think of that? AAK My concern with your working definition is that if everything is mystical then nothing is. Doesn’t this render the terminology of ‘mystical’ or ‘spiritual’ or ‘supernatural’ essentially useless? JTF For example, a flow state is a low-level mystical experience. But it’s one where your actual behaviors are driving you toward a greater sense of belief that your current orientation will take you where you want to go. I like these mystical experiences a lot, because they demand real-life pragmatism. The feedback of the actual environment needs to tell you, in real time, that what you’re doing is working. You could suppose that a perfect flow experience would be a feeling of heaven on earth, where all of your beliefs take you, without fail, exactly to your intended outcome with the right amount of effort. That’s probably never going to happen 100%, but you can approach it more and more closely. What makes normal, everyday life feel mundane (i.e. not like what you’d ordinarily define as a ‘mystical experience’) is that our consciousness is usually crowded with doubt. Much of it is correct doubt: the recognition that you believe things that don’t take you where you want to go. This is happening at many levels, across many people, all the time. In fact, most of what you ‘see’ in normal consciousness is just the memory of what you expect to see, based on what you believe. This has been shown in some detail by researchers. The fact that you ‘see’ strange images, geometry, and distortions of reality during a very extreme mystical experience shouldn’t confuse us on the practical definition, either. You are always ‘seeing’ what isn’t exactly there. Mystical experiences just give you a glimpse under your own hood, so to speak. In normal life, if you learn to pay attention to your latent doubts, and suffer them, you can become the sort of person who can not only withstand doubt but also hold a kind of open resting awareness that may find opportunities for higher beliefs. AAK And I also agree with what you seem to be saying, which is that learning to accept and integrate doubt into our lives is a necessary ingredient for maturity. So how do these things go together? I don’t think either of us advocate for expunging ourselves of doubt and giving into pure fanaticism… JTF Another way of looking at doubt and certainty: doubt is like entropy and belief is like order. That might sound like I’m saying all doubt, like entropy, is bad, and all belief, like order, is good. But that kind of statement is itself a belief that doesn’t work. Sometimes you can have a false belief, such as a delusion or denial of reality. It feels good, but it doesn’t take you where you want to go. And sometimes you can have a true doubt: a right realization that things are more chaotic than you’d like them to be, and that you don’t yet see how it all might resolve. This is the very broad sketch of what it feels like to be a mortal being. Psychedelics mechanically induce feelings of extreme certainty that have little or nothing to do with actual circumstances. So when I came out of that heightened state, I almost couldn’t deal with the legions of unexamined doubt, manifesting as all the various mundane problems in my life. AAK I’m not sure if a scientist would agree with me, given that all a psychedelic does is adjust certain chemicals in your brain, but that’s been my personal experience thus far. What do you think? JTF AAK I’d like to apply this framework to something more specific. If, as you say, we each have to “figure out for ourselves what to do” with our experiences, beliefs, and received wisdom (including the wisdom of the scientists), then how do you personally deal with the question of things like ghosts or magic or the occult? These seem to be a major focus by many people, both inside and outside formal religions. JTF As for manifestation and magic and the occult: it seems clear to me that your intention can subtly change the behaviors of everybody who could possibly interact with you. Just like when you focus on getting a basketball into a hoop, all of your hormones and chemicals and neurons and organs cooperate with that pattern to achieve that end. Even in basketball, that’s true at levels much deeper and more subtle than we can really know about. For larger networks like culture and religions, the knock-on effects are innumerable. As far as calling that magic, I’m ambivalent. You can call it magic if you want, because you can’t go in and identify every single causal interaction that participates in the pattern without killing the pattern. You might as well give the pattern itself a name—magic, if you like, or manifestation, or prayer. Some of these practices help you find true beliefs better than others. That’s a story for another day. AAK Re your definition of ‘magic’ or ‘manifestation’, that does basically make sense to me. People and networks of people are susceptible to manipulation, for good and for ill. It reminds me of all the talk about ‘being agentic’ in the sense that it really is possible to bend the world to your will, but that doesn’t necessarily mean it will be good for the world (or for you). How about positive spiritual experiences? Have you had these? JTF I mostly have positive mystical experiences when I’m reading a good book or in a movie theater or having a good conversation or walking outside. They are very delicate and go away with the slightest whiff of wanting more of it. They don’t need or want you to believe in them as ‘more real’ than reality because God is really Being itself and so our mundane struggles with doubt and belief are where all the good stuff lies. Which is also why I am very suspicious of psychedelics. What about your positive experiences? Anything as vivid as the snake? AAK I’ve had lots of wonderful experiences of rapturous worship or praise and feeling ‘caught up’ with God, which are vivid with a sense of God’s awesomeness or His overpowering love. But the most tangible experiences are strange occurrences which could be chalked up to coincidence: clear signs, mysterious help, the sense of being led to a certain place or person. Which I think is that ‘sweet spot’ you’re talking about regarding mundaneity and doubt, because you can always find some way to explain them away if you wanted to. For example: a few years ago my wife and I were on an island in a foreign country with no money for the ferry back to our hotel on the mainland (my fault, I hadn’t brought enough cash that day). The ferry terminal only took cash, and none of the ATMs on the island were working. We had tried everything we could think of, and were sitting by the side of the road in despair when a Muslim man and his wife came out of nowhere and gave us some cash and then walked away into the crowd. We weren’t obviously begging for money and we couldn’t speak this man’s language, but somehow they knew we needed money and they gave it to us as an act of charity. It felt impossible, it still sounds impossible, and yet it really did happen. And we knew were being cared for by the hands of God. AAK I’ve really enjoyed this dialogue, and if I had to sum up what I’m taking away it would be: Much of our lives can qualify as ‘mystical’, as a mystical experience is the experience of certainty of belief.
The gap between the mystical and the mundane is how much doubt we hold, but the best way to live isn’t erasing all doubt, but instead learning how to discern and integrate doubt into our lived experiences.
How you integrate your own experience of the inexplicable with the material facts of the world is an intensely personal question, even if you accept the structures of an organised religion, and it’s vital that you seek an answer to that question.
‘Negative’ mystical experiences tend to insist upon their own importance, whereas ‘positive’ mystical experiences tend to be experienced as an effervescent gift, difficult to pin down.
If God is Being itself, then our doubts and what we do with those doubts in everyday life are more important any ‘special’ mystical experience.
