The Supply Chain of Intelligence: And the Coherence Problem at Its Heart

On the physical world that intelligence runs on, the believers who built it first, the craft knowledge neither can escape, and the hidden bottleneck.

“During a gold rush, sell shovels.” — attributed to everyone, understood by almost no one.

Brannan’s Lesson

In January 1848, James Marshall found flakes of gold in the American River at Sutter’s Mill. Within a year, 300,000 people had flooded into California. By 1855, they had extracted roughly $2 billion in gold at today’s prices.

It financially ruined most of them.

The average forty-niner earned something close to the prevailing daily wage he could have earned at home, after accounting for travel costs, equipment costs, food, and the real probability of finding nothing at all. The Gold Rush produced enormous aggregate wealth. It distributed almost none of it to the people most directly participating in the technology — the extraction of gold from rock.

The people who got rich were selling shovels. Levi Strauss made his fortune not by mining but by selling pants that wouldn’t disintegrate in the mud. Samuel Brannan, California’s first millionaire, made his money by buying every shovel and pan in San Francisco before publicly announcing the Gold Rush. He understood the production function precisely: you need gold to sell gold, but you need a shovel whether or not you find any.

Brannan’s genius was not just identifying the constraint. It was acting on that identification before the constraint was legible to anyone else. He did not wait for confirmation. He committed to his model of the future while the world still believed gold was a rumor. By the time 300,000 forty-niners arrived needing shovels, Brannan owned the shovels. The return was not a reward for risk tolerance. It was a rent on having seen clearly, first.

This is a structural description of how value accumulates in a technology transition — toward the production constraints, away from the technology itself, and specifically toward the people who understood those constraints before the market did. It recurs, with remarkable consistency, at the beginning of every major cycle.

The AI infrastructure race is being won the same way. Not by whoever builds fastest, but by whoever believed earliest.

Every five-year GPU contract signed at $1.90/hour is now generating margin against a market where the same capacity clears at $2.40 or above. The margin isn’t from operational excellence. It’s from temporal arbitrage, correctly pricing future scarcity before it arrived. The company that locked up TSMC‘s most advanced fab capacity in 2022, the cloud provider that signed GPU contracts at prices that looked irrational at the time, the AI lab that reserved training clusters before it had revenue to justify them, each committed earliest, each is capturing the most margin today. And the commitment required, in most cases, was not capital. It was epistemic. You had to actually believe the future was arriving before anyone else would sign a contract with you.

What follows is a map of the physical supply chain that intelligence runs on — nine layers of constraint that sit beneath the software, invisible to most investors, systematically mispriced by a market whose analytical categories were built for a different era. And threaded through the map, at the layer where the components must finally be made to talk to each other: the coherence problem, the most important and least discussed constraint in the entire AI infrastructure buildout.

It is, necessarily, a long map. But maps are most useful at the beginning of a journey, when you don’t yet know where you’ll get lost.

The Production Function and the Compulsion Machine

Every technology transition has a production function — a set of inputs that must exist before the technology can exist. Some inputs are created by the transition. Most aren’t.

The inputs created by the transition compete intensely with each other, because the transition produces them in abundance. New models, new frameworks, new applications, these are the things everyone is building, and because everyone is building them, they are the things that compete hardest on price, talent, and margin. The inputs that preexist the transition, or that become scarce because of it, face a different dynamic entirely. They don’t need a better product. They need capacity. And capacity, in physical systems, responds to market signals on timelines measured in years, not quarters.

The pattern holds across every major transition. The 1870s railroad boom made fortunes in steel, in land along rail corridors, and in the banking structures that reorganized bankrupt lines, and destroyed most of the railroad operators themselves. Of the hundreds of railroad companies that existed in 1880, fewer than twenty survived to 1920 as independent concerns. The smartphone era made fortunes in display glass, in processor architecture, in cell tower infrastructure, and in NAND flash memory, and destroyed hundreds of handset manufacturers. The device market collapsed to a duopoly. The infrastructure layer compounds.

What makes the AI moment structurally different from prior transitions is not the pattern itself… the pattern is the same, but the force driving demand.

The dominant logic of AI infrastructure spending is loss avoidance, not investment return. This distinction changes everything.

The five largest AI infrastructure companies plan to spend $685 billion on capital expenditure in 2026 alone. Oracle spent 86 cents of every revenue dollar on capital expenditure in 2025. The capital intensity ratios across the hyperscaler group — 45% to 60% of revenue, for companies that a decade ago operated at 15% to 20% — have converged on levels that resemble aluminum smelters or semiconductor fabs.

This is not rational capital allocation under any traditional ROI framework. But it is not irrational either. The AI systems that emerged between 2022 and 2025 demonstrated something that changed the competitive calculus permanently: capability is a function of compute. Each generation trained on more compute produces measurably better capabilities, and each improvement creates products that generate revenue, attract users, and produce data that feeds the next training run. The deficit compounds. A company that underinvested by $10 billion in 2024 does not need to spend $10 billion more in 2025 to catch up, it needs to spend enough to close a gap that has been growing while it waited.

They are not making investment decisions. They are making loss avoidance decisions. And when loss avoidance is the dominant logic, the answer to “how much?” is always: whatever it takes.

This compulsion is what is pulling $685 billion of hyperscaler capex into the physical world in 2026, filling the order books of gas turbine manufacturers, transformer producers, specialist piping fabricators, and optical transceiver suppliers with demand they cannot fulfill quickly enough.

Understanding why those constraints matter, and which among them is the most structurally underpriced, requires two more frames before the map.

The Negentropy Tax and the Knowledge That Lives Below the Neck

The second law of thermodynamics states that entropy — disorder — increases in any closed system over time. Order is unstable. It requires continuous energy input to maintain itself. Everything humans call progress is, at the physical level, the local imposition of order against this universal tendency.

Training a frontier AI model is one of the most negentropy-intensive operations humans have ever performed: taking the universe’s tendency toward disorder and, through the application of enormous energy over time, crystallizing it into a precise configuration of weights inside a neural network. A trillion parameters, each set to a specific value, each encoding a learned relationship between concepts. This arrangement is so improbable that it could not exist without weeks of continuous energy expenditure by tens of thousands of specialized processors.

The negentropy tax is what you pay to get there. Not in abstract financial terms — in watts, joules, and the physical infrastructure required to move and remove heat from both. The chips must run. The power must flow. The heat must be removed. The transformer must be wound. The circuit must be printed. The mirror must be polished to atomic flatness. None of this is dissolved by a better algorithm or made optional by a more elegant architecture.

And here is where Polanyi enters, because the negentropy tax is not only paid in energy. It is paid in craft. And craft is a form of knowledge that does not behave like other inputs.

Michael Polanyi, the philosopher-chemist, said something precise and unsettling: “We can know more than we can tell.”

He was describing a genuine epistemic category — knowledge that has no linguistic address because it exists not in propositions but in the body itself. The master glassblower who can feel the moment the gather is ready to be shaped. The transformer winder whose calibrated grip knows, without conscious calculation, when another half-turn will compromise the insulation. The HRSG piping fabricator whose hands understand the resistance of P91 chromium-molybdenum steel at operating temperature in a way that no training manual can reproduce.

Ask any of them to explain precisely what they are doing, and they will fail you. Not from unwillingness. From genuine impossibility. The knowledge lives below the neck.

Tacit knowledge is not undigitized knowledge. It is not knowledge awaiting the right data collection infrastructure to extract and encode it. It is knowledge whose medium of storage is the body itself. This distinction has three structural implications for the AI economy.

The first: tacit physical knowledge cannot be written down, which means the craftsmen who hold it are more economically durable than the market has priced. The ageing copper winder at a transformer OEM is not a vacancy to be filled. He is a decade of accumulated calibration that cannot be replicated by hiring his replacement.

The second: tacit physical knowledge cannot be synthesized by AI models, which means the physical world is the most irreplaceable training data in existence. A model trained on synthetic data learns to predict what a model would say rather than what reality looks like. The circularity compounds with each generation — outputs become more internally consistent and less grounded in physical truth.

The third: tacit knowledge can only be acquired through embodied experience. A robot cannot acquire the knowledge required to catch an egg by training on descriptions of catching eggs. It needs a body that has dropped eggs, a history of almost-catching-and-not, thousands of repetitions that wear the pathway smooth from conscious deliberation to something below thought. This is not a metaphor for why robotics is hard. It is the literal technical constraint that explains why foundation models trained on internet video cannot simply be pointed at a factory floor and expected to function.

Three taxes denominated in the physical world. The negentropy tax — watts and welders and grain-oriented steel required to build and run the infrastructure. The ground truth tax — physical-world knowledge required to train the models that run on that infrastructure. The embodiment tax — years of real-world deployment experience required for physical AI systems to acquire the tacit knowledge that makes them genuinely capable. All three are irreducible. All three grow larger the more powerful the AI systems become. All three are paid in human craft, or its machine equivalent.

With that frame in place, the map becomes legible.

The Supply Chain: Ten Layers From Physics to Pipe

The supply chain of intelligence has Ten layers, ordered by depth. The deeper the layer, the more physical the constraint, the more tacit the knowledge required, and, as a general rule, the more durable the competitive position of the companies that occupy it.

Layer One: The Machine at the Bottom of Everything

In Veldhoven, Netherlands, ASML manufactures the only machines capable of printing the circuits that make frontier AI compute possible. A single extreme ultraviolet lithography machine costs approximately €380 million, requires three years of lead time to build, contains roughly 100,000 custom-engineered components, and cannot be replicated by any other company on earth. The EUV process works by firing a high-power laser at a tin droplet 50,000 times per second inside a near-perfect vacuum, generating extreme ultraviolet light focused through mirrors coated with alternating layers of silicon and molybdenum — each layer precisely 6.7 nanometers thick — to project patterns onto silicon wafers. Carl Zeiss SMT manufactures the mirrors inside every EUV machine, the only company capable of polishing a reflective surface to the atomic-level flatness that EUV requires. ASML owns a 24.9% stake in Carl Zeiss AG specifically to protect this supply chain.

There is no frontier AI without ASML. No H100s. No B200s. No custom TPUs. Every chip, every model, every token prediction traces its lineage back to a machine that only one company makes.

By 2028 or 2029, the bottleneck in the entire AI compute expansion cascade will fall here. TSMC‘s projected $100 billion in annual capex means nothing if the EUV machines required to execute that capex cannot be produced faster than they currently are. The absolute ceiling on how much AI compute humanity can produce in any given year is ultimately set by a factory in the Netherlands. This is not a bottleneck that will be resolved by throwing capital at it. It is a constraint embedded in the accumulated craft of one institution over decades.

Layer Two: The Blueprint Before the Chip

No chip gets designed without Electronic Design Automation tools. Synopsys and Cadence Design Systems together control approximately 80 to 90% of the global EDA market — the software through which circuit design is expressed, simulated, verified, and prepared for manufacturing. EDA software is embedded in the workflows of engineers who have spent years learning specific tools. Switching isn’t a software migration. It’s a multi-year retraining of a scarce and expensive workforce. Every chip designed for AI compute passes through Synopsys or Cadence tools. They are the blueprints. The chip is downstream.

Layer Three: The Inspection Layer Nobody Mentions

At 3nm node geometries, a single misplaced atom can render a circuit non-functional. KLA Corporation is the world’s dominant provider of semiconductor process control equipment — the machines that inspect wafers at each stage of manufacturing, detect defects, and allow engineers to diagnose and correct process errors before they propagate. KLA’s tools are used in every advanced semiconductor fab on earth. Teradyne and Advantest test every finished chip before it ships; between them, roughly 85% of the global ATE market. Applied Materials and Lam Research make the deposition and etch equipment that executes the manufacturing steps themselves. Every new fab that TSMC or Samsung builds requires a full suite of their equipment. The AI infrastructure buildout is, mechanically, a sustained revenue cycle for all of them.

Layer Four: The Constraint Inside the Chip

The H100 GPU is not compute-constrained. It is memory-bandwidth constrained. The bandwidth of the channel between compute units and memory is the actual limiting factor on training speed, which is why every H100 ships with 80 gigabytes of High Bandwidth Memory at throughput above 3 terabytes per second. HBM is DRAM chips stacked vertically — four to twelve dies tall, connected through thousands of microscopic copper pillars called through-silicon vias, mounted directly on the same package as the GPU. The manufacturing process is sufficiently complex that only three companies produce it at scale: SK Hynix, Samsung, and Micron.

SK Hynix holds roughly 50% of the HBM market and is NVIDIA‘s primary supplier. For every H100 or B200 shipped, SK Hynix supplies the memory that makes it function.

The investment paradox: SK Hynix trades at multiples reflecting its history as a commodity DRAM manufacturer — cyclical, prone to oversupply, margin-compressed. That history is accurate for conventional DRAM. It is structurally wrong for HBM, a custom, complex product with 12 to 18 month lead times, deep customer qualification requirements, and essentially no competitive overhang from Chinese manufacturers who remain years behind on the technology. The market is pricing the old business model against the new one. The gap between those two models is the thesis.

Layer Five: Light at the Speed of Data

At some point in every data pipeline, information moves as light. The transition from copper to optical interconnects inside data centres is one of the most capital-intensive infrastructure shifts underway, invisible to most AI observers because it happens inside the building, between racks, at wavelengths the human eye cannot see.

The structural argument is this: as GPU cluster density increases, the optical layer becomes the chokepoint. Coherent Corp manufactures the transceivers that make that layer function, converting electrical signals to light for transmission over fibre between racks. Marvell Technology makes the digital signal processors that translate between the optical and compute domains; without Marvell’s DSPs, the light is uninterpretable.

In India, the global fibre supply tightening has created an opening the market has not fully priced. Standard single-mode fibre rose over 75% in a single month in late 2025. The core upstream constraint is preform, the high-purity glass rod from which fibre is drawn, with all major manufacturers at or near full utilisation and new capacity taking 18 to 24 months to come online. HFCL secured the largest contract in its history: a $1.1 billion five-year agreement to supply high-fibre-count optical cable to an undisclosed global multinational. Sterlite Technologies launched India’s first Hollow Core Fibre cable — light travelling through an air-filled core approximately 46% faster than traditional glass fibre, specifically designed for AI data centres. Both companies are covered by telecom analysts. Neither is covered by AI infrastructure analysts. The arbitrage between those two analytical domains is the point.

Layer Six: The Grid is the Binding Constraint

In most US markets, the queue for new grid interconnection exceeds five years. A 100-megawatt data centre requires utility-scale grid capacity to function, capacity that must be permitted, studied, approved, and physically constructed. GPUs are sitting in warehouses. Buildings have been constructed. The power is not there.

This is not a chip shortage. It is electrons.

Constellation Energy owns 21 nuclear power plants in the United States — the largest nuclear generation fleet in the country, producing baseload power that runs continuously, predictably, and at near-zero carbon emissions. Microsoft has signed a 20-year power purchase agreement to restart Three Mile Island and dedicate its output to data centre power. Constellation earns power at roughly $25 to $35 per megawatt-hour from its nuclear fleet and sells it at market rates that have roughly doubled as AI demand materialises. The spread is the thesis.

Eaton Corporation makes the power distribution infrastructure inside the data centre — switchgear, uninterruptible power supplies, power quality equipment, circuit breakers rated for megawatt-scale loads. Their electrical business has been growing at 15 to 20% annually as data centre construction accelerates. It is priced as an industrial conglomerate with slow-growth characteristics.

Bloom Energy represents a more structural opportunity: not a backup power solution but a grid bypass solution. A Bloom Energy server is a solid oxide fuel cell — an electrochemical device that converts natural gas directly into electricity at 60%+ efficiency, near-zero noise, and without requiring grid interconnection. A data centre operator who installs Bloom servers can achieve operational status years before a comparable facility dependent on the five-year grid queue. Microsoft, Google, and SK Telecom have all signed significant Bloom contracts. MTAR Technologies, in Hyderabad, manufactures the hot box components — the core structural and thermal enclosures of the solid oxide fuel cell — at 60 to 70% wallet share with Bloom. The defensibility is structural: qualifying a new supplier for components operating in extreme thermal environments takes years. When Bloom’s order book grows, MTAR’s production schedule grows with it.

Layer Seven: Power at the Point of Failure

A data centre without backup power is not a data centre. A momentary grid interruption, a few milliseconds, causes in-flight computation to fail, in-memory state to corrupt, and potentially physical damage to hardware that was not cleanly shut down. The cost of an unprotected outage to a facility running continuous AI training is measured in millions of dollars per hour.

Caterpillar is the dominant manufacturer of large-format diesel and gas generators for data centre applications. AI-era data centres draw 2 to 5 times more power per square foot than legacy data centres, so the generator fleet inside every new facility is commensurately larger. In India, both Caterpillar and Cummins are winning data centre orders that were negligible to their businesses three years ago.

Layer Eight: Thermal Physics as Investment Thesis

The NVIDIA H100 GPU dissipates approximately 700 watts of heat. Rubin, arriving in 2026, reaches 1,800 watts per chip. Feynman, already named, is projected at 4,500 watts. A dense AI GPU rack reaches 120 kilowatts. The power density per rack has increased roughly 50 times in a decade.

Beyond roughly 30 to 40 kilowatts per rack, air cooling physically cannot carry the heat away fast enough. At 120 kilowatts per rack, it is not merely inefficient.

It is impossible.

The transition to liquid cooling is therefore not a product decision. It is a thermodynamic mandate.

Vertiv manufactures thermal management systems for data centres — cooling distribution units and direct liquid cooling systems that pump chilled water to heat exchangers attached to GPU servers. Their competitive position is built on installation relationships: a cooling system that is already qualified, installed, and maintained by Vertiv service teams is not replaced casually. Alfa Laval manufactures the heat exchangers at which every liquid cooling system ultimately terminates. Their data centre segment is growing at double-digit rates from a small base.

Aeroflex Industries in India holds an exclusive five-year agreement to supply liquid cooling skids to Vertiv for their domestic operations. The defensibility is structural: the exclusivity means competitors are not in the supply chain, and the product is subject to qualification regimes where switching introduces risk no OEM has incentive to take. KRN Heat Exchanger and Refrigeration supplies heat exchangers to the OEMs assembling finished cooling products — data centre contribution growing from 7% to 15% of revenue in a single year.

Layer Nine: The Craft at the Foundation

In a fabrication shop outside Hyderabad, a man is bending P91 chromium-molybdenum steel pipe — an exotic alloy engineered to survive temperatures above 600 degrees Celsius without distorting. The pipe will become part of a heat recovery steam generator, downstream of a combined-cycle gas turbine, generating electricity for a data centre, training an AI model. But right now, none of that exists. There is only the pipe, the man’s hands, and decades of accumulated knowledge about exactly how much force to apply at what angle without compromising the molecular structure of the alloy. His certification took years to earn. The global pool of people who can do this work correctly is in the low hundreds.

The future is being constrained by the medieval.

Dee Development Engineers fabricates these piping systems for heat recovery steam generators. Fewer than ten qualified fabricators globally can do this to the required standard. This is Polanyi’s tacit knowledge expressed as a balance sheet constraint in the global AI supply chain. The moat is not a patent or a network effect. It is the accumulated knowledge in the hands of certified welders — knowledge that took years to build, that died with fabricators who closed during the last power sector downturn, and cannot be reconstituted quickly.

Azad Engineering produces turbine blades, vanes, and hot section components from monocrystalline nickel superalloys at temperatures exceeding 1,200 degrees Celsius — for Siemens, GE Vernova, and Mitsubishi, who together hold 70% of the global CCGT market. Components operate under rotational stress so extreme that they require materials grown as single crystals to prevent failure at the grain boundaries. Qualifying a new supplier takes years. The knowledge that produces these components is not architectural. It is experiential, accrued through thousands of attempts at the failure boundary of what is metallurgically possible.

CG Power and Industrial Solutions manufactures power transformers — the single most acute global supply bottleneck in the AI buildout. A large power transformer is not manufactured. It is assembled, by hand. The magnetic core requires grain-oriented electrical steel with global production concentrated in a handful of mills. The copper windings must be precisely wound and insulated. A single large transformer can take 12 to 18 months to manufacture and costs $2 to $5 million. The workforce that can do this work is ageing. OEMs are reluctant to expand capacity because they cannot staff the facilities they already operate.

The most sophisticated technology companies in human history are being rate-limited by the unavailability of skilled craftsmen who know how to wind copper around grain-oriented steel.

The Coherence Problem: The Tenth Layer No One Is Mapping

Nine physical layers. Each one a production constraint. Each one paid, at its foundation, in craft knowledge that cannot be written down, taught from a manual, or compressed by additional capital.

And then there is the tenth layer — one that is not in the supply chain charts, not covered by the infrastructure analysts, not yet legible as a category. It is not a physical constraint in the same way the first nine are. It is an architectural constraint, and it sits precisely at the interface where the physical supply chain hands off to the computational system it has built.

It is the coherence problem.

There is a moment in every infrastructure transition when the obvious bet stops being the right one, because the value has migrated somewhere less visible, to the layer where the different pieces of the infrastructure must be made to talk to each other.

The railroad age had this moment. The obvious bet was steel and rolling stock. The less obvious bet was the telegraph running alongside every rail line, not because telegraphs were more important than trains, but because the train created the need for the telegraph to exist. The telegraph was, in a precise technical sense, the coherence layer of the railroad system: the thing that made the parts behave as a unified whole rather than as isolated segments of track.

We are in the equivalent moment. The most interesting value migration is happening in the coherence layer. And the coherence layer, in 2026, is the CPU.

The Hidden Dependency

For three years, the mental model of an AI data center was architecturally simple: a GPU cluster, fed with data, producing outputs. The CPU was the support act, necessary but undifferentiated, like the loading dock of a factory. You needed it. You didn’t think about it.

This model was approximately correct for the first generation of AI workloads. Large language model pretraining is, at its core, matrix multiplication at scale. Tokens in, gradients out. The operation is massively parallelizable, structurally simple, and almost entirely resident on the GPU. The CPU’s job was logistics: shuffle data to the GPU, manage I/O, handle orchestration. One CPU to eight GPUs, roughly, and the CPU was never the bottleneck.

Then reinforcement learning arrived. Not as a research curiosity — as the dominant paradigm for making frontier models actually useful.

The RL training loop has a structure fundamentally different from pretraining. The model generates a candidate output — a piece of code, a mathematical proof, a reasoning chain. Then something must evaluate that output. Check whether the code compiles, runs, and produces the right answer. Verify the mathematical proof step by step. This evaluation is not a GPU workload. It is branchy, sequential, conditional logic — precisely the kind of work GPUs are terrible at and CPUs were designed to do.

The RL environment is a CPU farm. A serious RL training cluster requires CPU compute roughly equivalent in scale to the GPU compute it supports — not in raw operations, but in the wall-clock time budget available before GPU idle time becomes unacceptable. The GPU generates a sample in milliseconds. The CPU must evaluate it before the next sample is generated. Fall behind, and the most expensive hardware in the cluster sits idle, waiting.

This is the hidden dependency. The GPU buildout created a CPU demand that wasn’t modeled by anyone, because the previous paradigm — pretraining — didn’t require it. The demand emerged from a paradigm shift inside the AI methodology stack, not from a change in the hardware roadmap. Nobody issued a press release. The CPUs just started disappearing from inventory.

Intel‘s Q4 2025 earnings contained an anomaly: unexpected CPU demand strength from data center customers, supply constraints appearing faster than the sales team had predicted, capex guidance being revised upward to add capacity. The explanation wasn’t Intel executing better. It was that the workload changed underneath them, and their product happened to be in the path of the new demand.

The Coherence Problem, Precisely Stated

The CPU renaissance is real. But understanding it as a simple demand recovery… more CPU units sold, higher ASPs, growing TAM, misses the structurally more important thing happening in parallel.

In the current generation of AI infrastructure, CPU and GPU exist in separate memory spaces. The CPU has its DRAM. The GPU has its HBM. Moving data between them requires explicit memory copy operations across the PCIe bus — a high-latency, bandwidth-limited operation that is, in many agentic and RL workloads, the actual bottleneck. The GPU finishes its computation. The result is copied to CPU memory. The CPU processes it, generates the next input. That input is copied back to GPU memory. The GPU proceeds.

This copy-intensive architecture is a historical accident. CPUs and GPUs were designed separately, for separate workloads, by separate teams, at separate companies. They were never designed to share a memory space because for most of computing history they never needed to.

Agentic AI broke this assumption. An agent doesn’t complete a task and hand it off. It operates in a continuous loop — generate, evaluate, retrieve, generate again… where GPU inference and CPU reasoning are tightly interleaved, happening in near-real-time, with each step dependent on the result of the previous step. The latency of crossing the PCIe bus, repeated hundreds of times per second across thousands of concurrent agents, is not a rounding error. It is a system-level bottleneck that determines how many agents a given infrastructure can run efficiently.

CPU and GPU cannot address each other’s memory directly. They cannot share cache coherency. They are, in a precise technical sense, two separate computers installed in the same physical machine, connected by a bus designed for a different era.

The solution to the coherence problem will determine the architecture of AI infrastructure for the next decade.

Three Positions Around One Problem

AMD‘s position is the cleanest: they have the best product at the right moment. Turin — the EPYC 9005 generation — offers up to 192 cores per socket, socket consolidation ratios of 7:1 over aging Intel Cascade Lake deployments, and competitive performance per watt. The RL environment needs many, dense, power-efficient cores — structurally the same workload profile as cloud-native computing, where AMD spent four years winning with Zen architecture’s core density advantage. AMD didn’t need to pivot. The workload came to them. The investment thesis is a core-count-demand thesis: a structural argument that RL environments will scale by adding more CPU cores in roughly linear proportion to GPU compute additions, and that AMD wins the marginal core-count dollar where cost-per-core-per-watt is the dominant procurement metric.

ARM Holdings’ position is categorically different. ARM doesn’t sell CPUs. ARM sells the architectural license and per-unit royalty on which most of the world’s CPUs — and increasingly its accelerators — are built. AWS Graviton 5, Google Axion, Microsoft Cobalt, NVIDIA‘s Grace and Vera: three of the four major hyperscalers have committed to ARM-based custom silicon, and NVIDIA‘s coherence solution (NVLink-C2C, a high-bandwidth coherent interconnect between Grace CPU and GPU) is built on an ARM CPU core. ARM collects a royalty on the CPU side of the equation in virtually every plausible coherence architecture scenario. The compounding arithmetic is favorable. The deeper thesis is the architectural migration itself: hyperscalers writing custom silicon for specific workloads are not bound by x86 legacy software compatibility, and in that degree-of-freedom competition, ARM wins on power efficiency for the workloads that matter.

Intel‘s position requires intellectual honesty. The problem is structural and not reversible: the hyperscalers that built custom ARM silicon are not coming back to x86. Intel‘s TAM is smaller than it was in 2019. The tailwind is real but temporary: the RL demand surge hit before AMD could fully absorb it, and existing customers needing more of what they already owned benefited Intel‘s Xeon inventory for 18 to 24 months. The more interesting Intel argument is in the architectural possibility: Intel has EMIB — Embedded Multi-die Interconnect Bridge — packaging technology that allows heterogeneous dies to communicate at high bandwidth with low latency. A CPU manufactured in the same facility as its coherent accelerator, connected via EMIB, with shared memory addressing — this is architecturally elegant and technically achievable. Whether Intel can execute on this architecture is the question its recent history makes genuinely uncertain. The honest Intel thesis is probabilistic: an option on a recovery, not a recovery.

The Value Chain Behind the Visible Narrative

The CPU renaissance story, told as AMD-versus-Intel-versus-ARM, is the surface layer. Underneath it is a value chain whose structure determines where the durable returns live.

The RL environment is a CPU farm, but not only a CPU farm. The full value chain includes verification infrastructure — code compilation, test execution, formal verification tools, mathematical proof checkers, primarily software workloads running on CPU, but at RL scale requiring specialized deployment patterns. The companies building this execution and verification layer are not semiconductor companies; they are infrastructure software companies underpriced because they are narratively illegible: they don’t sound like AI companies, but they are structurally as important to frontier AI development as the GPU clusters they serve.

Memory architecture is the structural bottleneck underneath the coherence problem itself. The RL loop’s coherence problem is fundamentally a memory problem — CPU must read GPU outputs, GPU must read CPU evaluations, both must access shared storage at low latency. The memory hierarchy between GPU HBM, CPU DRAM, and persistent storage is being redesigned in real time. NVIDIA‘s Bluefield DPU line is one approach: offload memory management to a dedicated processor, create a new tier in the memory hierarchy that routes around the PCIe constraint.

And then there is the co-location requirement that nobody discusses: the RL environment and the GPU training cluster cannot be in different buildings. The round-trip latency of sending an evaluation request over a WAN connection is fatal to training efficiency. This is driving data center floor plan redesigns — Microsoft‘s Fairwater cluster makes it explicit: a 48MW CPU and storage building supports a 295MW GPU cluster in adjacent structures. The companies providing high-bandwidth, low-latency intra-cluster networking that connects CPU RL environments to GPU training clusters are structural beneficiaries of RL scaling in a way not yet reflected in their analyst coverage.

The Coherence Problem as a General Principle

The CPU-GPU coherence problem is not a semiconductor-specific phenomenon. It is an instance of a general principle that appears in every infrastructure transition: the period of maximum value creation is not when the components of the infrastructure are being built, but when the components must be made to work together as a unified system.

The railroad network’s coherence problem was time zones. When trains ran on local time in each city, scheduling and coordination were impossible — the network couldn’t function as a network, only as a collection of local lines. The resolution (standardized time zones, adopted in the US in 1883) was not a railroad technology problem. It was a coordination problem. Its solution was institutional, not engineering. And the economic value it unlocked was enormous.

The internet’s coherence problem was the protocol layer. Individual computers connected by physical links created no value without a shared language for communication. TCP/IP was the coherence layer of the internet — not the most glamorous piece of infrastructure, not the part that attracted the most venture capital in 1995, but the part that made everything else possible.

The AI infrastructure’s coherence problem is the CPU-GPU interface. The individual components — GPU clusters, CPU farms, memory hierarchies, networking — are being built with extraordinary capital and urgency. The question of how they function as a unified computational fabric is receiving proportionally less attention, because it is architecturally less obvious and commercially less legible.

This is the pattern. The coherence layer always underprices relative to the component layers during the buildout phase, because the coherence layer’s value is conditional — it only matters once the components exist. Once the components exist, the coherence layer’s value becomes obvious retroactively, and the window for asymmetric positioning has closed.

We are in the window.

The Exhaust Economy

Models cannot synthesize the training data required to improve themselves without degrading. A model trained on synthetic data learns to predict what a model would say rather than what reality looks like. The models need ground truth. Ground truth exists in the physical world. The physical world cannot be reproduced by the models trained on it.

The data most resistant to synthesis is the data that lives where tacit knowledge lives — in the physical domains where humans do things that are too complex, too embodied, too outcome-dependent to be reproduced by a system that has never tried and failed: the surgeon’s hand movements during a procedure, annotated with outcomes across ten thousand operations; the factory worker’s assembly decisions, labeled with quality outcomes at production scale; the radiologist’s micro-decisions across a million scans, correlated with diagnosis accuracy and downstream patient outcomes; the welder’s calibrated grip at operating temperature.

This data has three properties that make it structurally unlike anything else in the AI stack. It is generated continuously as a byproduct of other activities — the surgeon is not producing training data, the surgeon is operating; the training data is exhaust from the primary activity, accruing without additional cost per unit. It cannot be synthesized at any price, you cannot generate a dataset of surgery outcomes without knowing what the actual outcomes were; the loop is closed. And the collection infrastructure is genuinely hard to replicate, requiring operational integration into the physical world at the point of generation, years of relationship development and trust accumulation that capital alone cannot compress.

The unit economics: collect the data once, license it many times. Marginal cost of the thousandth license approaching zero. No inference cost. No compute bill. Gross margins approaching software-as-a-service — not because the business is software, but because the product, once produced, is infinitely reproducible at near-zero cost.

The companies building this infrastructure are invisible. They appear in vendor agreements as line items. Their invisibility is structural: the value of the asset depends on exclusivity. Watch for one specific signal — companies releasing unusually high-quality domain-specific datasets from domains where synthetic alternatives don’t exist. The open-source release is not generosity. It is the demonstration of asset quality to buyers who can triangulate, from a fraction of the data, what the full corpus is worth. The open-source release is the floor. The signed contract is the ceiling. And the distance between floor and ceiling, in the right domain, is very large.

The circular economy above the physical layer will eventually find its external anchor here, in the physical-world data pipelines that the models need in order to move beyond the current capability plateau. The hyperscalers are building data centres and renting compute to AI model companies. The AI model companies use that compute to train models they sell back to enterprises, some of whom are the hyperscalers themselves. The hyperscalers invest directly in the AI model companies to ensure their cloud revenues persist. The AI model companies use that investment capital to buy more compute. Round and round. The enterprise market buying products outside this loop generates roughly $115 billion in annual revenue against $685 billion in annual infrastructure spend. The ratio is not improving fast enough to close. The exhaust economy is where the external anchor will eventually be found — the physical-world data the models need that the loop cannot self-generate.

The railroads made the commodity traders rich. The telephone made the advertising industry possible. The internet made the data giants inevitable. The AI economy will make the exhaust economy valuable.

What the Body Knows

The physical AI systems now being deployed in factories, warehouses, and agricultural operations face the same constraint the welder faces: you cannot acquire physical competence without physical experience.

The moat in physical AI is not architectural. It is the irreversible accumulation of on-policy, task-specific, failure-boundary data — data that only exists because physical systems were deployed early enough and broadly enough to generate it. A competitor starting today doesn’t just need to build the robots, train the models, and acquire the customers. They need to generate the years of deployment experience that have been accumulating since the first systems went into the field. The replication cost rises with every passing month. Not because anyone designed it that way. Because time cannot be purchased.

In the long arc, what physical AI offers is the possibility of capturing embodied knowledge before it disappears. Writing down the things we could not previously write down. Not the explicit knowledge that fills textbooks and specifications, that was already written. The embodied knowledge. The knowledge below the neck. The master glassblowers are retiring. The HRSG piping fabricators who trained a generation are mostly gone. The machinists who could hear a lathe’s pitch change and know what it meant, there are fewer of them every year.

The AI narrative has been, in its dominant form, a story about displacement. But it contains a hidden recursion that the narrative has not yet caught up to.

The creation of AI at scale requires the physical world, not as a substrate to be eventually transcended, but as an active participant in the construction of every model that has ever been trained or ever will be. The electrons that move through a GPU had to be generated somewhere. The heat those electrons produce had to be removed by coolant flowing through pipes that had to be welded by a certified welder. The power had to flow through transformers wound by a craftsman who learned the skill over a decade. The circuits on the chips had to be printed by an EUV machine whose mirrors were polished in Oberkochen, by people who have spent careers developing a precision that no specification document fully captures.

And then, this is what the AI narrative has not yet absorbed, the intelligence that emerges from that process will need to keep improving. Improving requires training data from domains where intelligence is not yet competent. And those domains are the physical world: the operating rooms and factory floors and fabrication shops. The places where the welder bends pipe, where the surgeon makes micro-adjustments, where the radiologist’s eye picks up a shadow that no current model can reliably name.

The Thermodynamic Moat

The strongest moats in this transition are not built on software platforms or network effects. They are embedded in physical reality — in the optics of light, in the thermodynamics of heat, in the electrochemistry of power, in the tacit knowledge of the craftsman whose skill cannot be downloaded.

The production constraints identified here are most durable precisely because they are physical. Constraints that dissolve require either that the architecture changes in a way that routes around them, or that someone solves a physics problem that has resisted solution for decades. What makes EUV unnecessary? A chip architecture that doesn’t require sub-5nm circuits. What makes HBM unnecessary? A training paradigm that doesn’t move data in the pattern that transformer architectures require. What resolves the coherence problem? A unified memory architecture that makes the CPU-GPU boundary irrelevant. Each route around is technically conceivable. Each requires teams of physicists who mostly fail, over years, at tremendous expense.

That is what makes them worth owning.

The believer who understood this in 2022 was not predicting a supply shortage. They were reading the thermodynamic structure of a system whose constraints were already visible, already physical, already inexorable. Brannan bought the shovels before the rush. The believers of this era signed the contracts before the demand arrived. In both cases, the return was the same rent, paid again, to the same quality of attention.

The Supply Chain Ends in a Human Hand

Return, at the end, to the welder.

The supply chain of intelligence runs from the EUV mirror polisher in Oberkochen to the circuit designer in San Jose to the wafer inspector in TSMC‘s fabs to the HBM stacker in Icheon to the fibre drawer in Hyderabad to the transformer winder in Bhopal to the fuel cell fabricator in Secunderabad to the cooling system engineer in Mumbai to the CPU farm evaluating the RL loop’s outputs — and then, through the coherence layer where CPU and GPU must finally be made to speak to each other, to the model that emerges from all of it.

At every point in that chain, value accumulates in the constraint.

The CPU renaissance is a demand story. The coherence problem is an architecture story. They are the same story told at different resolutions. At the demand resolution: more CPUs are being bought, AMD is winning share, ARM is collecting royalties on the transition, Intel is a distressed option on its own recovery. At the architecture resolution: the AI infrastructure buildout will hit a coherence bottleneck — a point where the inefficiency of moving data between CPU and GPU memory spaces becomes the dominant constraint on system performance. The entity that solves this bottleneck cleanly, at scale, at competitive cost, will define the architecture of AI infrastructure for the next decade.

But the question worth sitting with is not which company wins the bottleneck competition. It is structural: what becomes possible once the coherence bottleneck is resolved?

The RL environment, in its current form, is constrained by the coherence problem. Agents that reason in tight loops are constrained by the coherence problem. Physical AI systems that must process sensor data and generate motor commands in real time are constrained by the coherence problem. Every application that requires tight interleaving of symbolic reasoning and pattern recognition is constrained by the coherence problem.

Remove the constraint, and applications become thinkable that currently aren’t.

The roads are being built. The interchanges are being designed. The destinations, the things that only become possible once the interchange is complete, are still, for most investors, unimaginable.

That is not a reason to avoid them.

That is the reason to pay attention.

The supply chain of intelligence ends in a human hand. And that, it turns out, is the market’s largest unpriced insight. The coherence problem is not the exception to this rule. It is the newest expression of it, the latest constraint in a chain that runs from atomic precision in the Netherlands to a CPU farm evaluating code in a datacenter in Virginia, all of it built on physics that cannot be accelerated by capital alone, and craft knowledge that cannot be written down. The arbitrage is between what the market sees and what is actually happening. What the market sees is a demand recovery. What is actually happening is that the physical world is asserting itself… in watts and welders and silicon and steel, against every model that assumed intelligence could be built without it.
