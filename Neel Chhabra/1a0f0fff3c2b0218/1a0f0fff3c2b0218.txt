The argument: The secretary who corrected his master
“Knowledge itself is power.”
— Francis Bacon, Meditationes Sacrae, 1597
Bacon’s line became the motto of the scientific age. Less remembered is that in the 1620s Thomas Hobbes worked for Bacon, taking down his thoughts and helping translate his essays into Latin. Decades later, in Leviathan, the former secretary quietly amended his old master.
“The Sciences, are small Power; because not eminent.”
— Thomas Hobbes, Leviathan, 1651
Hobbes was not saying knowledge is useless. He was saying something sharper. Knowledge becomes power only when it is eminent: rare, unmatched, and recognised by others. Knowledge that many people share, or that nobody else can see you hold, confers little. Power belongs to whoever controls terms that others must accept.
Bacon describes how an advantage is built. Hobbes describes when it can be charged for. Most arguments about moats are really arguments between these two men, and nowhere is the argument more live than in the software that sits between Indian brands and their buyers.
Two bodies: A system with two centres of gravity
“To every Action there is always opposed an equal Reaction.”
— Isaac Newton, Principia, Law III (Motte translation, 1729)
Newton’s third law has a consequence that surprises people. When two bodies orbit each other, neither sits still at the centre. The Earth does not circle a fixed Sun; both circle a shared point called the barycentre, which lies closer to the heavier body. Put a second mass into a system and the centre of gravity moves.
In February, an earlier essay, Unicommerce: The Schwerpunkt of Indian Commerce [ https://substack.com/redirect/685195c4-03d5-4caa-a186-ffebcab2b144?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ], borrowed Clausewitz’s word for the centre of gravity: the point from which all other strength flows. It argued that the coordination problem of multi-channel commerce was that point, and that Unicommerce had been quietly occupying it since 2012.
On 19 August 2026, a second body entered the system. Shiprocket listed at ₹131, 35% above its issue price, and five weeks later carried a market value of about ₹8,750 crore, roughly nine times Unicommerce’s. The centre of gravity of Indian e-commerce enablement did not disappear. It began to orbit.
The substrate: Entropy only rises
“What an organism feeds upon is negative entropy.”
— Erwin Schrödinger, What Is Life?, 1944
The Schwerpunkt essay rested on one directional claim: Indian e-commerce will not simplify. It will complexify. [ https://substack.com/redirect/685195c4-03d5-4caa-a186-ffebcab2b144?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] Every force acting on the system adds another thing a brand must connect to. More marketplaces. More couriers. More payment methods. Quick-commerce apps with their own rules. New compliance. None of these removes a system; all of them add one.
Schrödinger’s point was that living things survive by paying continuously against disorder. So do brands. The payment shows up as oversold stock, unreconciled marketplace payouts, parcels refused at the door, and operations teams that spend their days copying numbers between screens. The cost does not grow in a straight line with the business. It grows faster.
Management has since supplied a number that sharpens the claim. Unicommerce’s MD and CEO, Kapil Makhija, told that the company handles about 25–30% of India’s dropship e-commerce volumes, the orders a brand ships from its own warehouse rather than the marketplace’s.
“We have effectively become an index to e-commerce market.”
— Kapil Makhija
He added that dropship is about half the market today and expected to reach roughly 65% within a few years, because brands want control of the customer experience and platforms prefer a model that needs less of their own capital. That matters, because the biggest risk named in the earlier essay was the opposite drift: brands handing fulfilment back to Amazon and Flipkart, and shrinking the stream of events Unicommerce sees.
The market is also younger than it looks.
“It’s only been 5 years of this market growing. A lot of brands, early-stage brands, still use Excel to manage their e-commerce operations.”
— Kapil Makhija
Every brand still running on spreadsheets is disorder that has not yet been priced. It becomes demand for a ledger the day its second channel goes live.
And most of that disorder has not yet arrived. Online shopping was about 8% of Indian retail in 2025, against about 17% in the US and 35% in China, and is expected to reach 14–15% by 2030. Some 56–61 million small retailers make up about two-thirds of the retail market, and about 45% of online spending already comes from tier 2 and smaller cities. Sales through brands’ own websites and apps are only about 11% of e-commerce, a narrower pool than Makhija’s dropship, which also counts marketplace orders that sellers ship themselves.
Read the picture from the top. The largest pool of unserved disorder is not on websites at all. It sits with small exporters shipping abroad and with shops that sell from a counter, and the fastest growth is expected in offline and social selling, each compounding at 40% or more. The Schwerpunkt essay argued that entropy follows every new channel [ https://substack.com/redirect/685195c4-03d5-4caa-a186-ffebcab2b144?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ]. The next channels are already visible: offline, and across borders.
The first body. The ledger: knowledge of the present
“A map is not the territory it represents.”
— Alfred Korzybski, Science and Sanity, 1933
Korzybski warned against confusing the map with the land. Commerce adds a twist. Once a business runs on a map, the map becomes load-bearing. Uniware is that map: the single record of what stock exists, where, which channel sold it, what was invoiced and whether the marketplace paid in full. The earlier essay called it an ERP that dares not speak its name [ https://substack.com/redirect/685195c4-03d5-4caa-a186-ffebcab2b144?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ].
Its defining property is exclusivity inside each client.
“A brand never uses multiple solutions for managing their e-commerce operations.”
— Kapil Makhija
So clients rarely leave for a rival. Makhija says more than 80% of churn comes from three causes: the brand shuts down, stops selling online, or hands fulfilment to a marketplace. None of those is a competitor winning.
“It is among the finest inventions of the human mind.”
— Goethe, on double-entry bookkeeping, Wilhelm Meister’s Apprenticeship (1795–96)
Werner, the merchant’s son in Goethe’s novel, praises double entry because it lets anyone see at a glance what is true about a business. Five centuries after Pacioli codified double entry, the most valuable property of a record is still that others believe it. Makhija offered an unusual piece of evidence.
“Many brands tell us that today, auditors ask for Unicommerce data to audit the books of a brand.”
When an auditor reaches for your system to check a client’s books, the record has stopped being a mere tool. It has become part of how the brand proves what it sold.
Since February the story has moved in the direction the essay expected, with one deliberate pause. FY26 revenue reached ₹204.3 crore. In Q1 FY27 revenue grew 14.3% to ₹51.4 crore while adjusted EBITDA fell 14.5% to ₹8.1 crore, because spending on AI, people and sales was pulled into the first half. Uniware’s enterprise clients rose from 979 to 1,228 in a year, and the top ten’s share of its revenue fell from 15.2% to 11.9%. Cash stands at ₹92.6 crore.
The longer arc is more striking. Over five years, revenue grew more than fivefold, from ₹40 crore in FY21 to ₹204 crore in FY26. FY26’s adjusted EBITDA alone, ₹43.9 crore, was larger than the company’s entire revenue five years earlier.
In Hobbes’s terms, the ledger’s knowledge is of the present. It knows what is true right now, with no error to learn from once it is recorded correctly. That makes it hard to displace and hard to reprice. It is power over the client’s switching decision, not yet eminence over the market.
“The growth levers for Uniware are threefold. One is the market growth on which we have limited control.”
— Kapil Makhija
The other two, he said, are new customers and selling new modules to existing enterprise clients. Both are ways of turning a record that clients cannot leave into a record they pay more for.
The modules are not random additions. Makhija describes them as arriving in the order a brand needs them.
“Typically, when a brand starts their journey, they start with the OMS.”
The ledger learns to act: Can you vibe-code a system of record?
Every software company now faces the same question from analysts: if large language models can write code, what stops a brand from writing its own? The Schwerpunkt essay [ https://substack.com/redirect/685195c4-03d5-4caa-a186-ffebcab2b144?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] took this seriously, putting the strong version of AI disruption, where integrations become nearly free to build and maintain, at a 10–15% probability. On the Q1 FY27 call an analyst asked Makhija almost exactly that.
“AI typically disrupts shallow SaaS, which is generic or easy to replicate tools.”
— Kapil Makhija
His argument had three parts. Decade-long relationships with marketplaces and couriers cannot be generated by a model. Switching costs for a brand’s operational backbone are extreme. And trust is the scarce input: a mission-critical record needs years of use before anyone, least of all an auditor, relies on it. Code has become cheap. Credibility has not.
The company’s own plan is to move the ledger up a staircase, from recording what happened to acting on what is about to happen.
Here the ledger's weakness turns into a strength. A system of record cannot learn much from its records, because a correct count has no error to correct. But an agent that acts needs exactly what the ledger holds: a trusted, complete, real-time picture of the business. Models are becoming a commodity; clean, credible data about a brand's operations is not. If agents are the next interface to commerce, the record they read from becomes more valuable, not less. Hobbes would call that the path from knowledge to eminence.
The second body: The flow: knowledge of what happens next
“Everything in war is very simple, but the simplest thing is difficult.”
— Carl von Clausewitz, On War, 1832
Clausewitz called it friction: the thousand small failures between the plan and the ground. In commerce, friction lives at the doorstep. The buyer is out. The address is fake. The courier’s hub clogs. The cash-on-delivery buyer changes their mind and the parcel travels home at the seller’s cost.
Shiprocket lives in that friction. It pools about 2.2 lakh merchants, routes their parcels across 42 couriers, and then sells checkout, marketing tools, cross-border shipping and small loans around the same orders. Its CEO insists on the distinction that matters.
“We do this without owning any of the assets because that’s the business model.”
— Saahil Goel
Shiprocket is not a logistics company. It books the courier’s charge as its own revenue, but capital spending was 0.9% of revenue last year.
It did not begin at the parcel. Saahil Goel, Gautam Kapoor and Vishesh Khurana started the business in 2012 as Kartrocket, software for building online storefronts. Five years later they abandoned the storefront and rebuilt the company around the merchant’s sharpest pain, shipping. Everything since has been a walk back up the order.
There is a Clausewitzian logic to the route. A storefront was one tool among many, easy to replace. Shipping was the one problem every seller had to solve on every order. By retreating to that point and holding it, Shiprocket found its own centre of gravity, and it has advanced outward from there ever since.
Where the ledger knows the present, the flow is forced to predict the future. Will this parcel arrive? Will this buyer pay? Which courier is failing in this pincode this week? Those questions have answers that arrive a few days later as clean labels: delivered, late, refused. A prediction problem with fast labels is a learning machine.
The mirror: Two price cuts, one pattern
The Schwerpunkt essay spent a section on what it called “the realization per transaction paradox” [ https://substack.com/redirect/685195c4-03d5-4caa-a186-ffebcab2b144?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ]. Unicommerce’s revenue per transaction had slipped from about ₹1.30 to about ₹1.08. The bear reading was pricing pressure. The essay’s reading was a deliberate trade: lower minimums to land brands while they are small, and a mix shift toward high-volume quick-commerce and B2B work.
Shiprocket’s first earnings call revealed the same shape in a different body. In its core business, transactions grew 31% in a year while revenue grew 22%.
Shiprocket’s CFO gave the mechanical explanation.
“There is an input cost compression that we are passing on.”
— Tanmay Kumar
Part of the gap is courier savings handed to merchants rather than kept. Part is that larger merchants, who pay less per parcel, are growing faster. Its CEO set out the broader stance on the same call: for now the company would rather keep adding transactions than “expand every drop of margin,” because “the transaction is far more valuable.”
Here is the connection neither company states. A price cut is how a learning system buys labels. Every extra order routed is another outcome observed, another pincode scored, another buyer remembered. Both companies are trading short-term yield for a larger sample. The question Hobbes would ask is whether the sample ever becomes eminent: large enough, and exclusive enough, that others must pay for what it teaches.
Knowledge and power: Where each one sits between Bacon and Hobbes
The Schwerpunkt essay drew a line that now does most of the work. Unicommerce, it said, has knowledge power but limited structural power [ https://substack.com/redirect/685195c4-03d5-4caa-a186-ffebcab2b144?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ]. It controls its own workflow and data. It does not control marketplace policy, courier rate cards, consumer demand or whether brands keep their own warehouses. Structural changes can bypass the knowledge layer entirely.
Add the second body and the map fills in.
Neither enabler holds much structural power. Marketplaces set the rules. Couriers own the trucks, and none of Shiprocket’s courier arrangements are exclusive. So both must win Bacon’s way, through knowledge, and then hope it becomes Hobbes’s kind, through eminence.
The industry sorts itself into four models. Large marketplaces own the whole chain. Service providers such as Delhivery, Gati and Shadowfax move the parcels. Point software such as ClickPost, EasyEcom and Increff solves one job each. Horizontal platforms try to stitch the whole journey together, and that is where Shiprocket plants its flag. Look for listed peers and the list is short: Shopify, Global-E and BigCommerce abroad, and in India only Unicommerce. Meanwhile, nothing stops the couriers from building self-serve platforms of their own. The owners of the trucks can learn too.
Shiprocket’s case for eminence rests on one asymmetry. An ad platform knows who clicked. The layer that carries the parcel knows who paid.
“A large part of India is still cash on delivery.”
Shiprocket says about 93% of checkouts on its platform fill the buyer's address automatically, and that it scores return risk from buyer and address history built on more than 15 crore consumers. In principle, a buyer who refused a parcel from one merchant can be flagged for the next. No single brand can build that memory. It is knowledge that only exists across the network, which is the closest thing in this sector to Hobbes's eminence.
There is evidence the loop is more than a drawing: three years of costs falling as volume grows. The newer businesses lost 86.5 paise of adjusted EBITDA for every rupee of revenue in FY24, 46 paise in FY25 and 31 paise in FY26; in Q1 FY27 the figure was 24 paise. In the core, acquiring a merchant cost ₹4,101 in FY24 and ₹2,829 in FY26, though it rose to ₹3,560 in Q1 FY27. Across the company, revenue per product and technology employee rose from ₹3.3 crore to ₹6.1 crore. That is what a learning curve looks like, bumps included.
The sensor: An instrument is only as good as its sample
The earlier essay made a claim about Shipway, the courier aggregator Unicommerce began buying in late 2024: about ₹68 crore in cash for a first stake of roughly 43%, with the rest acquired through a share swap in August 2025. It argued that the acquisition was data closure, not margin expansion [ https://substack.com/redirect/685195c4-03d5-4caa-a186-ffebcab2b144?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ]. Before Shipway, Uniware went blind the moment a parcel left the warehouse. After it, the loop from order to doorstep was closed. Shipway was more an instrument than an asset.
That reading still holds. The listing of Shiprocket adds a second fact: instruments differ in sensitivity, and in statistics sensitivity comes from sample size.
Makhija is candid about the gap.
“We are a relatively small player in a large market.”
Shipway’s advantage is placement. It sells to enterprise brands whose stock already lives in Uniware, and it gained better courier rates by joining the group’s contracts. The share of Uniware clients also using Shipway or Convertway rose from under 5% to over 10%. Shiprocket’s advantage is volume: it routes each parcel using more than 70 crore past shipments. One sensor sits in the best spot. The other has the biggest lens. Which matters more is the empirical question of the next two years.
Makhija argues that placement wins, and his reasoning is about switching costs running in one direction.
“Given that we have a sticky OMS system already, which none of the other players in the courier aggregator industry has, gives us a unique advantage.”
His logic is an asymmetry in switching costs. Courier aggregators, he noted on the same call, are not very sticky, and brands often use more than one to spread risk. An order system, by contrast, runs the whole operation and is rarely replaced. So an OMS vendor can sell shipping to its base more easily than a courier aggregator can sell an OMS to its own. Shiprocket’s counter is structural: it already offers order and warehouse management through Omuni and its fulfilment business, and its CEO argues that data scale is what drives outcomes. The Schwerpunkt essay’s framing applies to both: the one that closes the loop from order to doorstep with the most data wins the next round.
The culminating point: How far each advance can go
Clausewitz, the source of the Schwerpunkt, had a second idea that matters here. Every attack, he observed, carries its own decay. The further an army advances, the longer its supply lines and the weaker its striking power, until it reaches what he called the culminating point. Pressing beyond it invites the counterstroke.
Both enablers are now advancing into the other’s ground.
“We want to enable merchant orders and then be able to power more and more margin pools on the same order.”
— Saahil Goel
Shiprocket’s culminating point is in marketing. Its delivered-sales signal has to pass through Meta’s and Google’s auctions, which it does not control. If those platforms learn to ingest cash-on-delivery outcomes directly, the advance stalls. Unicommerce’s culminating point is in logistics, where it fights a data-scale war with a thinner lens and accepts lower gross margins to do so; its consolidated gross margin slipped from 54.2% to 51.9% in a year as Shipway grew. Neither has reached its point yet. Both are closer than their press releases suggest.
The clock: Quick commerce turns parcels into pallets
“Remember that time is money.”
— Benjamin Franklin, Advice to a Young Tradesman, 1748
Blinkit, Zepto and Swiggy Instamart moved Indian buyers from days to minutes. For a brand, feeding a dark store is a different job from shipping a parcel: pallets instead of boxes, booked time slots instead of any time today.
Complexity is the raw fuel of infrastructure software, and quick commerce is adding it fast. Uniware's quick-commerce volumes grew from around 20 million order items across FY25 to a run-rate of more than 90 million a year by Q4 FY26. Shiprocket's cargo app now books dark-store delivery slots automatically when a purchase order arrives. The Schwerpunkt essay's directional claim is being confirmed by the newest channel in the system: every simplification for the buyer becomes a complication for the brand.
The setup: The telescope and the quarter
In 1610, Galileo pointed a new instrument at Jupiter and found moons circling it. Some of the most learned professors of his day refused even to look through the telescope. They were not stupid. They were measuring the universe with the instruments their careers had been built on.
“Worldly wisdom teaches that it is better for reputation to fail conventionally than to succeed unconventionally.”
— John Maynard Keynes, The General Theory, 1936
Keynes explained why that refusal repeats in markets. An analyst judged every quarter has every reason to look through the instrument everyone else is using. Right now that instrument is pointed at Unicommerce’s Q1 FY27.
Through the short lens, the quarter looked weak. Adjusted EBITDA fell 14.5% and the margin dropped from 21.1% to 15.8%. Through the long lens, the same quarter was a deliberate choice: investment in AI, leadership and sales pulled into the first half, with profitability guided to improve from the second. Cash still rose 72% year on year, to ₹92.6 crore.
“Remain technology-first, invest with discipline and build patiently.”
Unicommerce operating philosophy, FY26 annual report
The same report calls FY27 a foundation year for the company’s “next 5x journey.” It is worth noticing how each body pays for its long lens. When Unicommerce listed in 2024, it raised no new money; the IPO was entirely an offer for sale by existing shareholders, so this investment comes out of its own cash flow. Shiprocket’s IPO included ₹885.5 crore of fresh capital. One funds its telescope from operations, the other from the public market.
Some of the best investment setups are exactly this: management investing with a telescopic view, front-loading the build of capabilities, while analysts evaluate through a myopic lens. Such setups often surprise on the upside in ways no model anticipated. But to harvest the unimaginable, you must be capable of tolerating it. Tolerant of management’s transient missteps. Tolerant of your peers’ judgment. Willing to look stupid for a while, and immune to the noise.
The market lends Shiprocket a telescope and hands Unicommerce a microscope.
The asymmetry between the two bodies is striking. Shiprocket’s emerging businesses lost ₹169 crore last year, and the market capitalises them generously as investment in the future. Unicommerce gave up ₹1.5 crore of one quarter’s EBITDA to fund its own future, and its stock has drifted lower. The same act is read as vision in one company and weakness in the other. That gap is where patient capital earns its return, if the telescope turns out to be pointed at something real.
The exam: What the price is asking you to believe
“In the short run, the market is a voting machine.”
— Benjamin Graham, as retold by Warren Buffett
A share price is a forecast that someone has already made. The market does not pay a company for doing well. It pays for doing better than the price assumed, and it quietly punishes the company that merely meets the assumption. So the useful question is not what these two companies will earn. It is what their prices already expect, and where that expectation might be wrong.
In February the Schwerpunkt essay [ https://substack.com/redirect/685195c4-03d5-4caa-a186-ffebcab2b144?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] put Unicommerce at about ₹1,100 crore, roughly 21 times forward adjusted EBITDA, and concluded that the multiple was not obviously cheap but that the market value was small relative to the opportunity. Seven months on, its market value has slipped to about ₹930 crore, adjusted EBITDA has dipped by design, and a second, far larger enabler now trades beside it.
On sales, the two bodies look similar. On proven profit, the market pays twice as much for Shiprocket’s core. Prices carry moods as well as models. A fresh listing carries the glow of its debut; a company that has just posted a weak quarter carries the shadow of it. Both are biases, and both are in the price.
Run the two prices backwards. Ask what each company must earn by FY31 for a buyer today to make 13% a year, if the business is then valued at 20 times EBITDA.
Unicommerce needs about ₹77 crore of adjusted EBITDA, against ₹44 crore in FY26: growth of about 12% a year. Revenue growing 15% at a 19% margin gets there, and the margin in FY26 was already 21.5%. The price assumes the ledger keeps doing what it already does, and nothing more.
Shiprocket needs about ₹690 crore, against ₹18 crore of group adjusted EBITDA in FY26. If revenue compounds at 25%, that means about ₹6,200 crore of revenue at an 11% margin: the whole company earning roughly what its core earns today, while the newer businesses still lose 24 paise on every rupee of revenue. The price assumes the learning system finishes learning.
The highest returns rarely come from companies that meet their forecasts. They come from companies that stretch their S-curve further than anyone priced: by widening the market they serve, by earning more on each unit, or by finding a new curve to climb. Unicommerce’s price leaves all three unpaid for: Shipway’s courier business, the AI “system of action”, and the growth of quick-commerce and B2B orders. Shiprocket’s price has already paid for most of its three.
One of those levers can be sized. By industry estimates, a full-suite enablement platform can earn up to 20% of the GMV it enables, once merchants buy the supporting services (payments, financing, marketing, customer service) and not only shipping. Shiprocket’s FY26 revenue was about 6% of the ₹32,777 crore of goods on its platform. That GMV is only about 3% of the ₹10–11 lakh crore market sketched earlier, but it already equals 35–45% of all sales on brands’ own websites and apps, where most of it originates. The headroom lies mostly in the other rows: exports, counters and social selling.
The distance between 6% and 20% is the option the market is paying for. One caution before paying for it: much of Shiprocket’s revenue is freight passed on to couriers, which is why the cost of merchant solutions was 74% of revenue in FY26. A higher take rate is not the same as a higher margin. What matters is how much of the extra comes from software, data and credit rather than more freight.
Rough scenarios to FY30, discounted back at 13%, bracket the bet. For Shiprocket, revenue compounding near 25% a year with group margins of 4%, 8% or 12% gives a probability-weighted value around ₹8,000 crore, a little below today’s value. For Unicommerce, revenue compounding near 15% with margins of 18% to 20% gives roughly ₹1,200 crore, about a third above today’s value. Neither gap is dramatic. The difference is in the shape of the tails: one price is set near the floor, the other near the ceiling. The skill is not predicting which company grows faster. It is noticing which one the market has stopped expecting anything from, and seeing the inflection before it becomes consensus.
Inversion: How each body could lose its orbit
“Invert, always invert.”
— Charlie Munger, borrowing from Carl Jacobi
The Schwerpunkt essay closed its bear case with probabilities. They are worth carrying forward, updated, and set beside Shiprocket’s.
Read the two lists together and the tails differ. Unicommerce's risks are slow and visible: they show up in client additions and module adoption quarter by quarter, with little that could break it suddenly. Shiprocket's risks cluster around its most valuable asset. The cross-merchant buyer memory is what makes its knowledge eminent, and it is exactly what a competitor with more data, a platform with more reach, or a regulator with new consent rules could erode. The market pays for Shiprocket's upside and is less careful about how its left tail is built.
The open question: When knowledge becomes eminent
Hobbes once took down Bacon’s words. Decades later he corrected them, and his correction is still the best test for this pair. Knowledge is small power until it is eminent: rare enough, and recognised enough, that others must deal with whoever holds it. Both enablers are piling up knowledge at scale. Neither has yet shown it can set terms with it.
The Schwerpunkt essay closed on the observation that the empire nobody sees is often the empire that lasts [ https://substack.com/redirect/685195c4-03d5-4caa-a186-ffebcab2b144?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ]. Seven months later that still describes the ledger: quiet, sticky, compounding at the speed of its clients. What has changed is that the system now has two centres of gravity, tunnelling toward the same point on the order. One knows what is true now. The other is learning what will happen next.
The exam paper is already set. It has four questions, and the answers arrive every quarter:
Does Shiprocket’s core revenue per transaction stop falling? That is the day its knowledge turns into terms.
Does its emerging-business margin keep climbing? It was −31% in FY26 and −24% in Q1 FY27.
Does Uniware’s reported growth clear 15% from Q4 FY27, as guided? Underlying growth, excluding one lost client, already does.
Do more Uniware clients buy Shipway or Convertway? The share is about 10%. It measures whether the smaller body can borrow the larger one’s gravity.
Bacon explained how an advantage is built. Hobbes explained when it can be charged for. Somewhere beneath the Indian order, two tunnels are about to meet, and the market will mark, quarter by quarter, which company learned the difference first.
