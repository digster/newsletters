There’s a peculiar thing that happens when a new technology or behavior achieves genuine cultural saturation… it stops being analyzed and starts being assumed. Quick commerce in India has reached this point. The 10-minute delivery rider is now as unremarkable as the auto-rickshaw. Blinkit, Instamart, Zepto, these aren’t startups anymore in the public imagination. They’re utilities, as taken-for-granted as tap water in the neighborhoods they serve.

And this saturation has created a blind spot.

Everyone is watching the 10-minute race. The dark store density war. The non-grocery category expansion into fashion, electronics, pet care. The path to profitability. The question of whether any of it makes money when venture funding eventually insists on returns. These are genuine and important questions. But they’re questions about a model that has already been proven, at least in terms of consumer behavior, if not yet in terms of sustainable unit economics.

The more interesting question, and the structurally underexamined one, is what happens between the 10-minute world and the two-day world. Because right now, that gap is enormous. And enormous gaps in markets that are moving fast don’t stay empty for long.

The Speed Stack and Its Missing Layer

Start with the fundamental observation that India’s e-commerce fulfillment landscape is, right now, a stack with two very different layers and almost nothing connecting them.

At the fast end: quick commerce, operating at 10 to 30 minutes, dense dark store networks in urban cores, purpose-built for high-frequency, low-deliberation purchases. Grocery. Medicines. Snacks. The things you need immediately because you’ve already decided you need them and any delay feels like deprivation.

At the slow end: traditional e-commerce, operating at two to three days, warehouse-to-doorstep logistics optimized for cost efficiency over speed, appropriate for the long tail of planned purchases where delivery time is not a purchase decision factor.

Between them: almost nothing viable at scale.

This is not an accident. It reflects the investment logic that shaped the last five years of Indian commerce. Quick commerce attracted enormous capital because the consumer behavior thesis was clear… people will pay for immediacy, especially for daily essentials, and the infrastructure model was legible: dark stores, riders, density. Traditional e-commerce had already been built and was expanding. The four-to-six-hour middle wasn’t built because nobody had a clear model for it.

What’s changing is not consumer behavior, that was already shifting, but the economics of serving that behavior in the middle window. And the specific mechanism enabling those economics is worth understanding precisely.

Why India Is Structurally Built for This

Before getting to the model, the structural context matters, because it explains why India can do something that failed expensively elsewhere.

Europe attempted fast commerce and retreated. The unit economics were brutal: picking, packing, and delivery costs consumed over 30% of order value. Labor costs were high, employment regulations were comprehensive, population density outside major metros was too low to generate the order density needed to make hyper-local fulfillment financially sustainable. Dark store real estate in expensive urban markets ate margins before a single order was delivered. The regulatory environment then moved against the model… gig worker reclassification, dark store location restrictions, adding cost burden on top of already-stressed economics.

In India, the same cost structure looks categorically different. Picking, packing, and delivery together cost less than 12% of order value. Rent and utilities consume roughly 1% of order value, versus approximately 7% in the UK, a sevenfold structural advantage. Labor costs for last-mile delivery are a fraction of European equivalents. And India’s population density in urban and semi-urban markets creates the order concentration that makes hyper-local fulfillment viable without needing European-scale average order values to compensate.

This is not a marginal advantage. It’s a different physics. The model that broke in Europe under its own cost structure has room to breathe, and room to be profitable, in India, in ways that weren’t available in the markets where it was first attempted.

China proved the demand thesis at scale. Its quick commerce market exceeds $150 billion in GMV, with the dominant model leveraging existing retail inventory rather than proprietary dark stores, enabling sub-30-minute delivery across 30 to 40 cities. But China’s model evolved on the back of already-digitized retail infrastructure, supermarket chains and large retail networks with digital inventory management that could be plugged into quick commerce platforms. India’s retail base is predominantly unorganized, fragmented, and offline. This is simultaneously a challenge and an opportunity, because it means the market is wide open in ways that China’s wasn’t by the time platforms arrived.

India’s faster commerce segment, quick and rapid delivery combined, is growing at roughly 45% annually, more than twice the 20% CAGR projected for China’s quick commerce market in its current phase. The consumer behavior is there. The structural cost advantage is there. The infrastructure investment is arriving. What’s emerging now is the question of which model captures the middle velocity.

The Goldilocks Thesis

The specific claim about rapid commerce… four to six hour delivery, is worth stating carefully, because it’s easy to dismiss it as a consolation prize for brands that can’t achieve quick commerce speeds. That framing is wrong, and understanding why it’s wrong is the key to understanding the opportunity.

Quick commerce is not better rapid commerce. It’s a different product for a different need.

The 10-minute delivery model is purpose-built for a specific consumer psychology: the need that feels urgent, where any delay causes measurable distress or inconvenience. You’ve run out of milk at 9 PM. Your headphones died before a flight. Your child has a fever and you need paracetamol. These needs have a specific emotional character.. they’re unplanned, stressful in their small way, and resolved by immediacy. The consumer in this moment values speed over almost everything else. Assortment breadth matters less. Price premium is tolerable. The job to be done is relief.

But this emotional character describes a minority of the purchases that could benefit from same-day delivery. Consider the much larger category of purchases where the need is real and time-sensitive, but not acutely stressful: the skincare product you’ve been meaning to reorder and realize you’re running low on. The phone charger your colleague needs today because hers broke. The protein powder you want before tomorrow’s workout. The gift you need for an event this evening. The automotive accessory required to complete a repair you’re doing this afternoon.

These purchases share a common structure: the consumer has a preference for receiving them today, would not be distressed by a four-to-six-hour wait, and would not pay the operational premium required for 10-minute delivery if they understood the cost. They’re planned enough to tolerate a brief window, but time-sensitive enough that two-to-three-day e-commerce delivery doesn’t serve the need.

This is a massive category. Non-grocery items have already grown from 20% to 30% of quick commerce GMV in just six months… a signal that consumers are bringing non-urgent-but-time-sensitive purchases into fast commerce channels even though those channels are over-engineered for the need. They’re using 10-minute delivery for four-hour needs because four-hour delivery doesn’t widely exist yet. That’s not consumer preference for speed. It’s consumer adaptation to available options.

The moment a four-to-six-hour delivery option exists at appropriate price points, those purchases flow to the economically rational channel. This is the market creation mechanism: not converting traditional e-commerce customers to faster delivery, but capturing existing faster-delivery demand that is currently being served by an over-built and over-priced model.

Why the Arithmetic Works Differently

The unit economics of rapid commerce are not just marginally better than quick commerce. They’re structurally different in ways that enable a completely different set of market participants.

A 2,000 square-foot dedicated dark store requires approximately 700 daily orders to achieve operational profitability. A 500 square-foot dark store requires 150 to 200. These thresholds are achievable for platforms with significant consumer penetration and brand recognition, the Blinkits and Instamart of the world, where consumer habit has been installed through years of use and significant marketing investment.

For a D2C brand selling skincare or supplements or fashion, these thresholds are not achievable from a dedicated single-brand dark store. The order density isn’t there. The capital to build proprietary fulfillment infrastructure isn’t justified by the economics. And the operational complexity of running a dark store… real estate, inventory management, rider coordination, SKU optimization, is a completely different competency from what a brand is actually good at, which is product development and consumer relationship.

The shared dark store model, operated by a third-party logistics provider and stocked by multiple brands, converts this calculus. Fixed infrastructure costs are distributed across multiple brands, each contributing order volume. The shared facility achieves the density threshold that no individual brand could reach alone. The brand pays per order or per service… variable cost, not capex. The logistics provider earns by efficiently pooling demand from multiple clients across a shared network.

This is, structurally, what cloud computing did to enterprise IT infrastructure: converted a capital-intensive, proprietary-build requirement into a shared, variable-cost service. The brands that previously couldn’t justify rapid delivery infrastructure can now access it through the same mechanism they access warehousing, payments, or cloud compute… as a service, at variable cost, scaling with demand.

And the delivery cost math improves further through batching. A point-to-point delivery… one rider, one order, straight to the customer, is the right model for 10-minute delivery because any stop along the way breaks the time promise. But for four-to-six-hour delivery, batching becomes viable: a single rider carries five to ten orders from a shared hub, delivering them along an optimized route. Last-mile costs drop by 20 to 30% compared to point-to-point delivery at similar scales. And when the hub serves multiple brands’ orders simultaneously, the batching efficiency compounds, a single rider might deliver orders from three different brands to the same apartment complex in one trip, instead of three separate riders from three separate facilities.

The shared-hub-plus-batched-delivery combination is the operational core of viable rapid commerce. Neither element works as well alone. Together, they enable economics that make the middle-velocity delivery window accessible to a much broader set of brands and categories than either the quick commerce or traditional e-commerce models can reach.

The Category Map and Its Implications

Not every product belongs in the rapid commerce window. Understanding the category fit matters enormously, both for building the model and for understanding which D2C brands have the most to gain.

Quick commerce owns the categories where urgency is genuine and the purchase is essentially non-deliberative: groceries, fresh produce, medicines, baby essentials, immediate cravings. The consumer in these categories needs delivery in minutes, not hours. Any longer and the need is either met another way or the consumer has absorbed the deprivation. These categories justify the operational intensity of the dark store density model.

Traditional e-commerce owns the categories where delivery time is genuinely irrelevant to the purchase: furniture, large appliances, specialty electronics, anything the consumer is willing to plan for and wait a week on. The logistical complexity of rapid delivery for a sofa is not justified when the consumer has already decided to wait.

Rapid commerce occupies the productive middle… categories where urgency is real but not acute, where same-day arrival meaningfully changes the consumer’s day but four-to-six hours is more than sufficient:

Cosmetics and skincare reorders. The consumer knows they’re running low and wants to avoid a gap in routine. They don’t need it in 15 minutes. They want it today.

Nutritional supplements. High-frequency replenishment, predictable cycle, modest weight, easy to pick and pack. The consumer who runs out Wednesday and wants to resume Thursday is a natural rapid commerce customer.

Consumer electronics accessories… chargers, earbuds, cables, cases. High urgency when needed (a dead charger can disrupt a work day), but completely compatible with a few hours of wait.

Fashion for occasions. Someone attending an evening event who discovers a wardrobe gap in the morning. Quick commerce can’t cost-effectively deliver apparel at scale; traditional e-commerce takes too long. Rapid commerce is the natural fit.

Gifting and seasonal goods. The gift that needs to arrive today. The item needed for a dinner party or celebration. Time-sensitive but not instant.

Auto accessories and parts for same-day repairs. The car owner who discovers a problem in the morning and needs a part to complete the fix before evening. This use case barely exists in current fulfillment models but is a natural rapid commerce category.

The common thread across these categories: higher average order values than grocery quick commerce, lower perishability, more predictable replenishment cycles, and consumer value propositions that are completely satisfied by same-day delivery without requiring sub-hour precision. These are also, not coincidentally, the categories where D2C brands have proliferated most aggressively over the last five years and where the brands have the strongest interest in controlling the consumer relationship rather than surrendering it to marketplace intermediaries.

The Tier 2 Opportunity That Quick Commerce Can’t Reach

Here is the structural limitation of the quick commerce model that rapid commerce resolves: it is geographically constrained in ways that fundamentally limit its total addressable market.

Quick commerce requires order density within a two-to-three-kilometer radius to make the 10-minute promise economically viable. That density exists in metro cores and Tier 1 cities. It does not exist in most Tier 2 and Tier 3 cities, where population is more dispersed, demand is more heterogeneous, and the critical mass required to justify a proprietary dark store at the neighborhood level simply isn’t there.

The data on this is clear: Tier 2, 3, and 4 cities now account for over 40% of Indian online retail spending, and that share is growing faster than metros. The number of online shoppers in smaller cities is growing nearly twice as fast as in Tier 1 markets. These are not marginal consumers, they’re the growth frontier of Indian e-commerce, and their delivery expectations are converging toward the speed norms being set by the metro experience.

But quick commerce’s infrastructure model cannot serve them economically. The density required for a 10-minute dark store model doesn’t exist in Nashik or Coimbatore or Rajkot. Even with creative models… kirana integration, micro-fulfillment nodes, the 10-minute promise breaks down outside of dense urban clusters.

Rapid commerce with a four-to-six-hour window is inherently more geography-agnostic. Batching enables a single hub to serve a wider delivery radius. Longer promise windows allow for order accumulation before dispatch. Third-party logistics providers with existing nationwide networks… branch offices, rider fleets, and service infrastructure built over decades of B2B and B2C logistics, can activate rapid delivery capability in smaller cities without building proprietary infrastructure from scratch.

The specific advantage of established 3PLs is frequently underappreciated in this context. A logistics provider with existing operations in 400 cities has something enormously valuable for rapid commerce scale-out: the local presence, regulatory familiarity, and operational infrastructure that any new entrant would spend years and significant capital building. The rapid commerce model gives those existing networks a new high-value use case, moving from two-to-three-day delivery to same-day delivery for a subset of SKUs, using shared dark stores as forward inventory staging points that convert existing logistics infrastructure into faster-commerce capability.

This is how rapid commerce becomes a Bharat story and not just a metro story. The quick commerce model is structurally limited to dense urban markets. The rapid commerce model, built on shared infrastructure and third-party logistics networks, can reach the Tier 2 consumer who is increasingly expecting same-day options but cannot be served by the metro-centric quick commerce architecture.

What This Means for Brands: The Consumer Relationship Question

There is a strategic dimension to rapid commerce that goes beyond fulfillment speed, and it matters more to D2C brands than the logistics economics.

When a brand sells through a quick commerce platform — Blinkit, Zepto, Instamart, it gains access to fast delivery infrastructure but surrenders something in return: the consumer relationship. The platform knows who bought what, when, at what frequency, with what basket composition. The brand receives an order and ships it. The consumer’s loyalty, if it exists, is to the platform’s convenience, not necessarily to the brand’s product.

This is the same dynamic that made Amazon’s private label strategy so threatening to brands that built significant volume on Amazon marketplaces. The platform accumulates the data. The platform controls the consumer touchpoint. The platform can use that data to identify high-margin categories and introduce competitive products, or simply to extract progressively higher fees from brands that have become dependent on the channel.

Rapid commerce through a brand’s own digital channel — website, app, combined with third-party fulfillment infrastructure changes this configuration. The brand controls the consumer relationship. The brand owns the transaction data. The brand sees the purchase frequency, the replenishment cycle, the basket composition, the price sensitivity. The 3PL provides the physical delivery capability; the brand retains the consumer intelligence.

This is not a minor distinction. Consumer data, accumulated at the individual customer level over multiple purchase cycles, is the asset that enables everything else: personalized marketing, subscription offers, product development informed by actual usage patterns, loyalty programs that create genuine switching costs. A brand that builds rapid commerce through its own channel builds that asset. A brand that cedes fulfillment to platform quick commerce builds someone else’s asset.

The growth of retail media… brands spending 25 to 30% of sales on platform advertising to maintain visibility, is the tax that brands pay for not owning their consumer relationships. It’s an enormous cost that compounds as competition intensifies and platform algorithms optimize for their own margin rather than the brand’s growth. Rapid commerce through owned channels is, among other things, a strategy for reducing that tax by building direct purchase frequency that doesn’t require constant paid amplification to maintain.

The Speed Tier Strategic Architecture

One practical implication of all this deserves more attention than it typically receives: the intelligent multi-speed fulfillment strategy.

Not everything should be delivered at the same speed. This sounds obvious, but most consumer businesses currently make one of two mistakes: either they offer only traditional two-to-three-day delivery because that’s what their existing logistics can handle, or they pursue maximum speed uniformly without asking whether the delivery cost is justified by the margin and urgency profile of the product.

The sophisticated approach segments the SKU catalog by urgency, margin, and consumer need:

High-urgency, high-frequency items that consumers need immediately… medicines, fresh food, daily essentials, belong in quick commerce channels at 15 to 30 minutes, typically through platform partnerships because the order density requirement makes proprietary infrastructure for these SKUs difficult to justify for most brands.

Mid-urgency items where same-day arrival matters but precise timing doesn’t, the skincare reorder, the supplement, the fashion piece for this evening, belong in the rapid commerce window, ideally through owned channels with third-party fulfillment to preserve the consumer data asset.

Low-urgency, planned purchases… anything the consumer is comfortable waiting a day or two for, belong in traditional e-commerce with optimized cost structure.

This three-tier approach is not just operationally rational. It’s strategically powerful because it aligns delivery cost with the actual value of that delivery speed to the consumer. A low-urgency item sent via 10-minute delivery costs more to fulfill than it adds to consumer satisfaction or conversion. A high-urgency item stuck in three-day delivery loses the sale entirely. The alignment of speed tier to urgency profile maximizes both consumer satisfaction and contribution margin simultaneously.

The brands that understand this architecture, and build fulfillment relationships that enable multi-tier delivery, will have structurally better unit economics than competitors who either under-invest in speed (leaving conversion on the table) or over-invest in uniform speed (destroying margin on every order).

The Operational Realities Nobody Talks About Enough

The case for rapid commerce is compelling. The operational complexity required to deliver on it is genuinely hard, and worth naming precisely rather than glossing over.

Dark store setup is not fast. Navigating the regulatory layers required to launch a new dark store — GST registration as an Additional Place of Business, local shop and establishment compliance, zoning approvals, takes 25 to 50 days at minimum. For a brand or logistics provider trying to rapidly expand into new geographies, that timeline is a meaningful constraint. Regulatory streamlining, single-window clearances, unified registration frameworks, standardized dark store licensing across states, would materially accelerate the buildout. This is a legitimate policy lever that hasn’t been fully engaged.

Order density is the profitability gatekeeper. A dark store that serves too small a catchment, or that carries inventory for brands with insufficient order frequency in that location, doesn’t reach the volume required for operational viability. The shared model addresses this by pooling demand across brands, but the fundamental requirement, enough orders from the right catchment to justify the infrastructure, doesn’t disappear. It gets managed through scale and smart placement rather than being eliminated.

SKU and assortment optimization is genuinely complex in a multi-brand environment. What gets stocked where, in what quantities, with what replenishment frequency? For a single brand’s dedicated dark store, this is a manageable optimization problem. For a shared facility serving 10 or 15 brands across hundreds of SKUs, the coordination and system requirements are substantially more complex. Warehouse management systems, real-time inventory visibility, demand forecasting at the SKU-location level, these capabilities require real technology investment and operational sophistication.

Real estate in high-density urban markets is increasingly scarce and expensive for dark store purposes. Regulatory restrictions on dark store locations in residential areas, driven by legitimate community concerns about rider congestion, noise, and traffic, constrain optimal placement and add friction to expansion. The brands and 3PLs that establish good-neighbor relationships with local communities and navigate regulatory relationships proactively will have location advantages that compound over time.

None of these challenges is fatal to the model. They’re execution problems, and execution problems are solvable. But they explain why rapid commerce has been slower to emerge than the consumer demand signal would predict: the operational complexity is real, and the market hasn’t yet produced the specialist infrastructure players and the playbooks required to solve it efficiently at scale.

The Prize and Who Captures It

India’s rapid commerce segment… four-to-six-hour delivery, is projected to become a $20+ billion GMV market by 2030, with a corresponding $2+ billion opportunity in logistics infrastructure, fleet capacity, and technology investment required to support it.

These are large numbers, but the more interesting question is who captures the value in this configuration, and how the gains distribute across the ecosystem.

For D2C brands, the opportunity is direct and strategically significant: same-day delivery capability through owned channels, at economics that make the capability accessible without requiring dedicated proprietary infrastructure. Improved conversion rates, same-day delivery meaningfully reduces cart abandonment in the time-sensitive categories where rapid commerce is relevant. And critically, consumer data ownership that builds the retention and lifetime value metrics that separate the compounders from the treadmillers in Indian consumer commerce.

For third-party logistics providers with existing nationwide networks, the opportunity is the ability to monetize existing infrastructure… branches, rider fleets, operational footprint in hundreds of cities, in a higher-value, higher-margin service configuration than traditional parcel delivery. The provider that builds credibility as a reliable rapid commerce partner, with real-time visibility, consistent SLA performance, and fair cost structures for multi-brand shared hubs, captures a defensible position in the infrastructure layer that will be difficult to dislodge once established.

For consumers, particularly in Tier 2 and Tier 3 markets currently underserved by both quick commerce and convenient next-day delivery, the arrival of viable rapid commerce means access to a delivery experience that metro consumers have been able to take for granted. The geographic equity dimension of rapid commerce is often understated… it’s not just a metro luxury being extended to more affluent cities, but a structural opening for the mass market that has been waiting for fulfillment infrastructure to catch up to their rising expectations.

The Bigger Frame

Zoom out and what you’re watching in Indian commerce right now is the assembly, in real time, of a complete speed stack, one that matches every meaningful consumer need with an operationally viable fulfillment model.

The 10-minute model exists and is scaling. The two-to-three-day model has existed for over a decade and is mature. The four-to-six-hour middle layer is now coming into existence, built on shared infrastructure, batched delivery economics, and third-party logistics networks repurposed for faster fulfillment.

When all three layers are operational and accessible, the Indian consumer has a complete set of options calibrated to urgency. The brand has a complete strategic toolkit… choose the channel and speed tier appropriate to the product and consumer relationship goals. The logistics ecosystem has a complete stack of service offerings at differentiated price points and margin profiles.

This is what a mature commerce infrastructure looks like. India is building it faster than any comparable economy has built it before, because it started with the behavioral infrastructure… the UPI payment habit, the smartphone-first consumer orientation, the rising income base, the willingness to pay for convenience, already in place. The physical and operational infrastructure is catching up to behavior rather than the other way around.

The specific category of rapid commerce… the four-to-six-hour window that is currently a gap, currently an opportunity, and currently underinvested, is, in this frame, not a niche. It’s the missing middle of the stack. And missing middles in fast-moving markets tend not to stay missing for long.

The question is not whether this layer gets built. It’s who builds it, who controls the consumer relationship within it, and which brands use the window to establish direct purchase relationships that compound over time rather than surrendering those relationships to platform intermediaries who will monetize them upward indefinitely.

That question is answerable right now, in the choices that brands and logistics providers are making about infrastructure investment, partnership models, and channel strategy. The choices being made in the next two years will look obvious in retrospect. They are not yet obvious in the present, which is precisely what makes them worth paying attention to.

Speed is not the story. Who captures the consumer relationship at each velocity, that’s the story.
