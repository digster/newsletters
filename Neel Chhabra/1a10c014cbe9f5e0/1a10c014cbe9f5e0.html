Growth loves to hide

Real growth happens out of sight. Markets price the surface. Right now, the largest crust in the market may be India.

Neel Chhabra
Oct 5
 
READ IN APP
 

“Physis kryptesthai philei.” — Heraclitus

Three words, surviving only because later writers quoted them from a book that is now lost. They are usually translated as “Nature loves to hide.”

The translation undersells it. The Greek word physis comes from phyein, to grow. It meant nature, but it also meant the process of becoming: the sprouting, the unfolding, the way a thing turns into what it is. Read that way, Heraclitus is making a much stranger claim. Growth itself prefers to work where it can’t be seen.

The idea keeps reappearing, in traditions separated by centuries and continents. The Chinese told it as a warning. The Indians told it as a cosmic myth. Modern medicine drew it in textbooks. And markets, which are built to read only the surface, pay to relearn it every cycle, usually at the moment a company, or a country, looks its worst.

The man who helped

Mencius, writing in China about a century and a half after Heraclitus, told a story about a farmer from the state of Song. The farmer worried that his seedlings were growing too slowly. So one day he went into the field and tugged them upward, one by one. He came home exhausted and told his family, “I’m worn out today. I’ve been helping the sprouts grow.” His son ran out to the field. Every seedling had withered.

Mencius wasn’t writing about crops. He was writing about character, which he thought grew the way plants do: slowly, out of sight, and badly if forced. The farmer could only see the part above the soil, so he worked on that part, and tore out the part that mattered.

The physician’s crust

More than two thousand years later, a German physician named Giulia Enders found the same idea in a medical book.

When her grandmother died, she didn’t cry. She had expected grief to look the way it does in films: the black dress, the tears, crying into the soup. Instead she felt numb, and thought: I shouldn’t feel this way. Then she came across an illustration of a healing wound. On top sits a crust: dry, still, apparently idle. Look at it two days later and nothing has changed. Underneath is a construction site. Cells are being recruited. New blood vessels are growing in. New tissue is going down where the old tissue tore.

She recognised herself in the picture. Then she cried.

Enders noticed something else, too. Soon she found herself wanting to go for a walk again, wanting to eat or drink particular things. A healing wound doesn’t just sit there. It attracts what it needs: cells, nutrients, blood supply. That detail matters more than it looks, and it’s worth holding on to.

What the body already knows

Biology keeps returning to Heraclitus. The benefits of exercise (a stronger heart, better lungs, sharper brain chemistry) aren’t built during the workout. They’re built in the recovery afterwards. The workout is damage; the rest is when the body decides what to rebuild. Sports scientists call the result supercompensation: after the dip, the body overshoots its old baseline. Athletes who skip the recovery get weaker over the following weeks, not stronger. They are the farmer of Song, tugging at their own muscles.

Go inside a chrysalis and it becomes almost unsettling. The caterpillar doesn’t simply sprout wings. It releases enzymes that break down much of its own body, and small clusters of cells called imaginal discs build the butterfly out of what’s left. For a while, the thing in the chrysalis is neither one creature nor the other. Judged as a caterpillar, it’s a catastrophe.

The ocean is churned

Indian mythology tells the deepest version of all of this. The gods and demons, wanting the nectar of immortality, wrapped a serpent around a mountain and used it to churn the cosmic ocean. In the best-known telling, the first thing to rise was not nectar. It was halahala, a poison strong enough to end the world. The churning could only continue because Shiva drank the poison and held it in his throat, which turned blue. That is why he is called Neelkantha (also, the orgin of my name). The nectar came later, and only because someone held the poison long enough. The Gita (2.14) gives the holder his instruction: heat and cold, pleasure and pain “come and go; they are impermanent. Endure them.”

Heraclitus, Mencius, the churning, the crust. Four traditions, separated by centuries and continents, all describing the same shape: a surface that looks worse or looks idle, a reorganisation underneath, and a test of whether anyone can wait for it.

The surface the market reads

Benjamin Graham, as Warren Buffett tells it, put it in a sentence: “In the short run, the market is a voting machine … but in the long run, the market is a weighing machine.” The voting machine counts what’s visible. And what’s visible about a company is its reported numbers, which are, quietly, a crust.

A plant commissioned this year typically carries its full depreciation and interest from day one, while utilisation can take two or three years to ramp. An acquisition brings its integration costs years before its synergies. A company that deliberately shrinks a low-margin line looks, on paper, like a company that’s shrinking. The P&L records the cost the day it’s incurred and the benefit only when it arrives. Which is why some of the best multi-year returns begin exactly when the reported numbers look worst, and why the market, reading the surface, usually sells there.

The market prices the crust, because the crust is what gets reported.

Recruit or leak

Here the idea becomes dangerous. “The bad numbers are secretly good” is the oldest story in investing. Every management team with a falling share price tells it, and every holder of a broken position tells it to himself at two in the morning. An explanation that can turn any decline into a hidden recovery explains nothing. Take two companies whose return on capital has fallen from 22% to 14% after a big expansion. If the story calls both of them “healing”, it isn’t an explanation. It’s a caption.

The wound supplies the test, in the detail Enders noticed. A healing wound attracts what it needs. A dying one leaks. A business under the crust shows the same split, in places the P&L never summarises:

Customers. Are renewals holding, receivable days steady or shrinking, and are the new plant’s first buyers the demanding ones?

People. Are senior hires joining rather than leaving, and is the capable second line staying through the ugly year?

Capital. Are lenders keeping their terms, and are suppliers still extending credit rather than asking for cash up front?

The same reported decline means opposite things depending on the column. That makes the idea falsifiable: if the numbers drop while receivables stretch, key people leave and lenders tighten, it’s decay, whatever management says on the call.

There’s a final twist, the one George Soros built a career on. Capital is one of the things a healing business must attract. Price the crust harshly enough and the stock halves, lenders read the stock, suppliers read the lenders, and the business loses the very thing it needs to finish healing. A crust priced as decay can become decay. Shiva could hold the poison. Most balance sheets have to be built to hold it.

Strong at the broken places

Hemingway wrote that “the world breaks every one and afterward many are strong at the broken places.” The operative word is many, not all. Enders makes the same distinction about skin. Some scars overbuild, piling up more tissue than the injury needs. Some sink, because immune cells keep attacking the repair. Clean healing needs cover, calm and good nutrients.

Companies scar the same three ways. The overbuilders cut debt to zero and freeze every investment for a decade: safe, and permanently smaller. The self-attackers cycle through write-downs, CEOs and strategies, and never let one repair hold. The clean healers protect the wound, keep feeding it, and leave it alone long enough to work.

The largest crust in the market

In 1919, with Europe still raw from war, W. B. Yeats wrote the line that has been quoted at every crisis since: “Things fall apart; the centre cannot hold.”

Every few years, the market marks India down. Usually the argument is about the weather — a global panic, a scare over the current account, capital rushing home to safer shores. This time the argument is about the climate. With American Big Tech pouring itself into AI, and China pulling further ahead, the doubt has moved from when to whether: whether India converges with the rich world at all. That is a different kind of pessimism. It’s no longer a verdict on the wound. It’s a verdict on the patient.

And the surface hands the pessimists plenty. Trade wars, energy shocks out of the Gulf, a rupee that keeps slipping, markets well off their highs. India is still poor. Its courts, police and bureaucracy are still slow and too often corrupt. Its cities are hard to live in unless you’re well off, and its politics can still be cynical. That is the crust, and it is real, nobody serious should pretend otherwise.

So run the test. Is the system recruiting or leaking? Underneath, pipeline is being laid quickly. Human capital improves every year, and the gap with the rich world is narrowing. Tax reform and digital public rails are stitching nearly 1.5 billion people into a single market. Banks have repaired their balance sheets and companies have paid down debt. Inflation, though lifted by the oil shock, sits within the RBI’s target band. Founders are still raising risk capital, and trade keeps growing while spreading across more partners. And beneath all of it, something slower and stranger is happening: a country being knitted together across regions, languages, castes, sects and even classes, wearing down, one generation at a time, its old talent for getting in its own way.

Here Yeats earns his place. Large, plural places often fail the way he described: the centre gives way, and the parts drift toward whoever offers them security, energy or markets. Europe built a single market but never a single centre, and has spent the past decade discovering what that costs — in energy, in defence, in the sheer speed of a decision. China built the centre and paid for it in pluralism. India is attempting the improbable thing, holding both at once: by most linguists’ counts, more languages than Europe, under one union and one ballot, and, increasingly, one market. It has done it messily, imperfectly, and for longer than many predicted in 1947.

What holds it is older than the state. The Indian family, for all the change it badly needs, still teaches children to wait for things, which is to say, it teaches deferred gratification, the very discipline this essay keeps circling back to. What’s new is a state finally able to organise that patience at scale.

This is a forecast, not a wish. Talent is spread evenly across humanity; opportunity is not, and India is slowly closing the gap between the two. Add a demographic tailwind that blows well into the 2040s, and the likeliest outcome is growth that embarrasses the pessimists. Even the anxiety is a good sign. A country that keeps finding new things to worry about has usually fixed the old ones, and a wound that is watched constantly is a wound that is being tended.

Put India in the four-state grid and something unusual shows up. The system sits in the left-hand column, still attracting. The price, and the mood around it, have been drifting as though it were sliding toward the right. Rarely has something this large been judged so much on its crust, and so little on its roots.

The old man on the frontier

The Huainanzi, a Chinese text from the second century BCE, tells of an old man on the northern frontier, remembered as Sai Weng, whose horse ran away. The neighbours came to console him. “How do you know this isn’t a blessing?” he asked. The horse came back leading a fine horse from the nomads. The neighbours came to congratulate him. “How do you know this isn’t a disaster?” His son, who loved to ride, fell and broke his leg. “How do you know this isn’t a blessing?” Then the nomads invaded. The able-bodied young men went to fight, and nine in ten of them died. The son, lame, was spared.

Sai Weng isn’t indifferent. He simply refuses to read the surface of an event as its meaning. That is the whole discipline. It holds for grief, for a body after training, for a founder in the year after a failure, for a company in the year after it bet itself on a new plant, and for a country in the middle of being stitched together. Stillness is not evidence that nothing is happening. Often it is evidence that growth has done what Heraclitus said it would, and gone into hiding.

The compression

Markets price the crust. Returns come from the reorganisation underneath it. So the signal is never that the numbers are falling. It’s what the business can still attract while they fall.

One question stays open: how long is a crust allowed to last? Biology offers a first base rate. A skin wound’s final remodelling phase can run for a year or more, and even then scar tissue regains only about 80% of the original skin’s strength. Healed is not the same as restored. Somewhere there’s an equivalent base rate for businesses: how long reorganisations take before “healing” becomes an excuse, and how much of the old strength comes back. Whoever measures it properly will know when to wait, and when waiting has become another word for hoping.
