The Three-Body Problem of Investing

On why intelligence is a necessary but wildly insufficient condition for wealth, and the three variables that actually determine who gets paid.

Neel Chhabra
Mar 10
 
READ IN APP
 

In 1887, Henri Poincaré proved something that broke mathematics. The two-body gravitational problem — one planet orbiting one star, has a clean analytical solution. Newtonian mechanics can predict exactly where both bodies will be at any point in time, forever. Add a third body, a moon, another planet, anything, and the system becomes fundamentally unsolvable. Not harder. Not requiring more computation. Unsolvable. The interactions between three mutually gravitating objects produce trajectories so sensitive to initial conditions, so nonlinearly coupled, that no closed-form equation can track them. Small perturbations compound. Order dissolves into apparent chaos. The universe, it turns out, is computationally intractable at the most basic level.

Poincaré won the prize. He did not, however, solve the problem. Nobody has. The three-body problem remains open.

Investing has the same structure.

Generating investment returns… genuine, durable, risk-adjusted returns that persist over multiple cycles, requires solving three problems simultaneously. The first problem is correctness: your model of a business, a market, or a price must be materially more accurate than the consensus embedded in the current price. The second is convergence: the market must come to share your belief, through some specific identifiable mechanism, such that price moves toward value. The third is timing: both of the preceding must happen within a timeframe that makes the opportunity cost acceptable, which is a gentler way of saying before you run out of capital, patience, or mandate.

These are not variations on a single problem. They are not even closely adjacent problems.

Correctness is an epistemological challenge: it requires better models, cleaner thinking, genuine insight into how a business or market actually works. Convergence is a social dynamics problem: it requires understanding how beliefs propagate through networks of capital allocators, which narratives become legible to which kinds of investors, on what timelines institutional capital can move. Timing is a physics problem, specifically, a problem about the velocity of information diffusion through a complex adaptive system with feedback loops, attention constraints, and reflexive dynamics.

Different disciplines. Different temperaments. Different feedback loops for skill development. Conflating them is the most expensive intellectual error in investing. And it is, by wide margin, the most universal.

Call it the Three-Body Problem of investing. Three interacting variables, each influencing the others, none solvable in isolation. You can hold two fixed and optimize the third, but the moment you introduce all three simultaneously, the system becomes nonlinear. Small errors in one variable compound into large errors in outcome. The investor who is correct and has the right convergence mechanism but is six months early on timing may underperform the investor who is roughly correct with a mediocre convergence thesis but impeccably timed. Correctness does not dominate. Convergence does not dominate. Neither does timing. The outcome is determined by the interaction of all three, which is to say, the outcome is not fully predictable from any one of them.

This is, to understate it considerably, a problem.

The Epistemology of Correctness

Let’s start with the variable investors spend the most time on, and still get wrong in the most interesting ways.

Correctness is not the same as being right. That sounds like sophistry until you consider what it actually means to have a correct model. A correct model must be accurate and differentiated, accurate enough to have genuine information content, differentiated enough from consensus to be exploitable. An investor who correctly identifies that Apple is a great business is not in possession of a correct model in the investment sense; they are in possession of a fact that has been priced into an index fund available to any retail investor for three basis points. The model only matters if it’s non-consensus.

This generates the central epistemic tension in investing. The easier it is to confirm a thesis, the more likely it’s already priced. The harder it is to confirm, the more ambiguous the data, the more interpretation required, the more structural the argument, the greater the edge if you’re right, and the greater the risk of constructing an elaborate, internally coherent fiction.

Most professional investors resolve this tension by drifting toward the confirmable. Sell-side models with six decimal places. Comparable company matrices. EBITDA multiples against sector medians. These tools optimize for precision at the cost of accuracy, the financial equivalent of measuring the temperature to three decimal places in a room you misidentified as the kitchen. The comfort of granularity substitutes for the discomfort of genuine uncertainty.

The investors who actually achieve correctness, not just accuracy, but differentiated, exploitable accuracy, tend to do one of two things. Either they work from fundamentally different information (genuine primary research, scuttlebutt, access), or they work from the same information through fundamentally different analytical frameworks. The first path is increasingly hard in a world of alternative data and expert networks. The second is the durable path, and the one that compounds.

Frameworks are load-bearing. The right framework extracts signal from noise that a different framework would classify as noise. Consider what happens when you analyze a company like a DCF jockey versus a student of competitive dynamics: the DCF jockey sees a terminal value calculation; the competitive dynamics student sees whether the company’s position at year 10 will be stronger or weaker than at year 5, and why. Both are looking at the same numbers. Only one is looking at the right thing.

But frameworks have failure modes too, and the subtlest one is this: a framework that has worked becomes a template, and templates are the enemy of genuine understanding. The investor who made money understanding network effects in 2015 is at risk of seeing network effects everywhere in 2025, not because the framework is wrong, but because it has become pattern-matching rather than analysis. The framework now precedes the observation. The conclusion arrives before the evidence. This is the epistemological equivalent of a hammer in search of nails, except the hammer is invisible to the person wielding it.

The genuinely correct investor has to do something psychologically uncomfortable on a continuous basis: hold conviction and uncertainty simultaneously. Conviction that the model is better than consensus. Uncertainty about which parts of the model might be wrong and where. The investor who is certain is usually lying, to themselves more than to anyone else. The investor who is perpetually uncertain has no exploitable edge. The territory between these poles is not a golden mean; it is a specific, hard-to-maintain cognitive stance that requires active upkeep.

In Indian markets, correctness has a particular texture. The quality of public information is lower, the quantity of market participants who have conducted genuine primary research is smaller, and the history of businesses under the current regulatory and competitive environment is shorter. This makes correctness more achievable, there is genuine information asymmetry available to the diligent researcher, and less reliable, the base rates for how similar businesses have performed in analogous contexts are thinner. The edge is larger. The error bars are wider. These are not offsetting facts; they are a structural condition of the ecosystem that the skilled investor can exploit precisely because most people treat the wider error bars as a reason to stay away.

The Social Physics of Convergence

Here is where most sophisticated investors go wrong. Not because they don’t understand the concept, of course price must converge to value eventually, of course catalysts matter, but because they systematically underestimate how much of their return depends on a problem they haven’t formally defined.

Convergence is not simply “eventually the market realizes I’m right.” That statement contains three implicit assumptions: that the mechanism of realization is deterministic, that it happens in a reasonable timeframe, and that it happens to the full extent of the mispricing. Each assumption is load-bearing. All three are frequently wrong.

The correct formulation is more uncomfortable: convergence requires a specific identifiable mechanism by which new information about value gets propagated through the network of capital allocators who can act on it, on a timeline that is governed by the attention constraints, mandate structures, and incentive systems of those allocators. This is a social problem. It has nothing to do with whether you’re right.

Consider what it actually takes for the price of an Indian small-cap to reprice from 15x earnings to 25x earnings. The company needs to become relevant to a new category of investor… either larger domestic funds who previously ignored the market cap, or foreign institutional investors who can now justify the position size, or retail investors who learn the narrative. Each of these pathways requires a different catalyst. Larger domestic funds need either a benchmark weight change or a signal from a peer that creates career risk for not owning it. FIIs need a narrative that is legible in a global context — industry tailwind, regulatory change, management meeting that can be explained in fifteen minutes to an investment committee in London. Retail investors need a price chart that tells a story they can repeat to their friends and a social proof mechanism.

None of these have anything to do with the underlying business quality.

This is the piece that correctness-focused investors miss. They spend 500 hours understanding the business and 30 minutes thinking about the convergence pathway. They discover a genuinely excellent company with a durable structural moat, verify the thesis with primary research, model the financials with genuine care, and then assume that the excellence of their work will be rewarded by the market noticing what they noticed. But markets do not reward research. Markets reward the legibility of a thesis to the marginal buyer.

A thesis that is deeply correct but structurally illegible, one that requires understanding the interplay between regulatory tailwinds, a shift in business model architecture, and a long-duration moat that only becomes visible after year three of a transformation, will not converge until someone figures out how to explain it in a way the marginal allocator can process. The best investment ideas frequently sit mispriced not because the market is wrong about the fundamentals, but because the fundamentals are not packaged in a form that the market can use.

This sounds cynical. It is actually clarifying. If convergence is a social dynamics problem, then it can be analyzed the way sociologists analyze the spread of beliefs through networks: who are the influential nodes? What narratives propagate faster than others? What makes a thesis stickier, what makes it more likely to survive the game of telephone from analyst to portfolio manager to risk committee? The investor who understands this is not abandoning intellectual rigor; they are applying rigor to a dimension of the problem that most of their peers are not analyzing at all.

In Indian markets, convergence dynamics have specific structural features worth naming. The market is bifurcated: a thin layer of institutional attention — domestic mutual funds, SEBI-registered foreign investors, a small number of well-resourced domestic PMS operators, sitting atop a much larger base of retail and semi-informed capital. Convergence in Indian small/mid-caps typically has two phases: first, a small number of well-positioned institutional investors discover the thesis; second, a re-rating event (index inclusion, large fund buying that creates visible price action, or media coverage of the story) triggers broader attention. The gap between phase one and phase two is where the careful investor earns the highest returns, but only if they can survive it.

Survival is not metaphorical. It is financial, psychological, and institutional. Which brings us to the third variable.

The Thermodynamics of Timing

Timing is the variable investors most often refuse to name. The most common phrasing is “I don’t try to time the market”, a statement that is simultaneously a statement of investment philosophy and a failure to grapple with the fact that timing is, structurally, a different kind of problem than the other two.

Correctness and convergence operate in the domain of information. Better models, cleaner analysis, sharper thinking about social dynamics, these compound with effort. They are, in principle, skill-accumulating activities. Timing does not work this way. Timing is governed by the information diffusion rate through a complex adaptive system, which is a function of variables that are largely exogenous to the investor: market liquidity conditions, the attention of relevant capital allocators, macroeconomic backdrop, and the random-walk of which stories happen to be culturally salient at which moments.

This is the component of the three-body system that is closest to irreducible uncertainty. You can be systematically more correct than your peers. You can build genuine insight into convergence mechanisms. But the specific week in which the thesis reprices, the event that transforms a paper gain into a realized return, is a function of the world’s attention schedule, which no one controls.

The practical implication is brutal. A correct thesis that doesn’t converge within the investor’s capital cycle is economically equivalent to an incorrect thesis. The investor who identified the right Indian infrastructure, PSU’s plays in 2015 but held through a five-year period of multiple compression, index-wide risk-off, and fund redemptions that forced selling, that investor’s correctness purchased nothing. The returns are identical to someone who was wrong.

This is why timing is structurally different from the other two problems: it determines the exchange rate between correctness and financial reality. More precisely: it determines whether your intellectual capital converts into economic capital before the capital cycle resets. The investor’s correctness is a perishable good with an uncertain expiry date. Timing determines whether you consume it before it expires.

The size of the required edge on correctness and convergence is not fixed. It is a function of the likely timing distribution. An investor operating in a liquid market with frequent re-rating events can hold a smaller edge on correctness and still survive, the convergence mechanism fires often enough that small mispricings become profitable. An investor operating in illiquid markets with infrequent catalysts needs a larger correctness edge because the timing distribution is wider, the window between investment and realization is longer, and more things can go wrong.

Indian small/mid-caps occupy an interesting position on this spectrum. The market is liquid enough to enter and exit positions without catastrophic impact costs on the way in; illiquid enough that forced sellers can create temporary mispricings that take 18-36 months to correct. The timing distribution for convergence is neither the daily mean-reversion of large-cap liquid markets nor the 7-year hold of private equity. It occupies a peculiar middle ground, long enough that impatient capital abandons positions before realization, short enough that genuinely mispriced opportunities don’t require the stomach of a decade.

This middle ground is, properly understood, an edge. The investor who can hold a correct thesis with an identified convergence mechanism through 24 months of apparently confirming nothing is doing something that a meaningful fraction of professional capital cannot, not because those managers are less intelligent, but because their institutional structures make it structurally impossible. Redemptions. Benchmark tracking. Risk committee oversight that marks behavioral divergence from index as career risk. The timing edge in Indian small/mid-caps is not primarily analytical; it is temperamental and structural. It belongs to whoever has the capital structure and psychological architecture to survive the wait.

The Interaction Effects

Here is where Poincaré becomes relevant again. The three-body problem is not difficult because each body is difficult to track in isolation. It is unsolvable because the bodies are mutually gravitating… each one’s trajectory influences the other two, simultaneously, in ways that produce nonlinear feedback.

The same is true of correctness, convergence, and timing in investing. Each variable modifies the probability distribution of the other two in ways that are not additive but multiplicative.

Being early on timing — the most common failure mode for sophisticated investors, does not simply reduce returns by the cost of carry. It actively degrades the quality of the correctness variable, because the information environment changes during the wait. A thesis that was non-consensus in 2021 may be consensus by 2024, not because the business changed, but because the narrative propagated. The investor who was correct and early is now holding a correctly analyzed but fully priced asset. Their edge has been consumed by time, not by being wrong. Timing failure turns a correctness win into a convergence problem.

Conversely: a poor convergence thesis — one that depends on a catalyst that never materializes, can retroactively make a correct analysis worthless. The business performs exactly as modeled. The moat deepens. The cash flows arrive. The market doesn’t care because the mechanism that would have triggered institutional attention never fired: the index inclusion was delayed, the large-cap fund that would have become a natural buyer changed its mandate, the sector narrative went out of fashion. The investor holds a permanently mispriced asset that gets repriced only at the next liquidity crisis when it trades at a further discount. Correctness plus convergence failure equals a long position in a private company with public market liquidity risk.

The interaction effect that gets the least attention is how timing error modifies convergence quality. When an investor exits a correct thesis too early… impatience, redemption pressure, portfolio rotation, they often exit just before the convergence mechanism fires. This is not coincidence; it is structural. The period immediately before re-rating is precisely the period of maximum apparent stagnation: the catalyst is forming but not visible, the price has drifted sideways for six months, the thesis is beginning to feel like a mistake. The institutional structures that force exit, benchmark underperformance triggers, redemptions, risk-off signals, are calibrated to fire at this exact moment. They are, in effect, adversarially aligned with the timing of the convergence event. The market structure is designed, not conspiratorially but emergently, to transfer the convergence premium from impatient institutional capital to patient private capital.

This is the structural basis for the mid-cap premium in Indian markets. It is not a risk premium in the conventional sense, compensation for bearing factor exposure. It is a complexity premium: compensation for simultaneously solving three interacting problems that most professional capital, by its institutional structure, can only solve two of at once.

What This Means for Skill Development

The three-body framing has a practical implication that is more important than the analysis: it tells you where to invest your development effort, and why the conventional allocation is wrong.

Most investors spend the majority of their skill-building time on correctness. More industry research, better financial modeling, deeper understanding of the competitive dynamics of industries. This makes intuitive sense, it is the domain most amenable to effort, most legible as intellectual work, most likely to receive social validation (from peers, from sell-side, from the companies themselves who appreciate and reward analysts who understand their businesses deeply).

The return on this marginal effort, however, is typically lower than the return on comparable effort spent on convergence or timing… precisely because correctness is where everyone is investing. The improvement from being in the 70th percentile of correctness to the 80th percentile is much smaller than the improvement from being in the 30th percentile of convergence thinking to the 60th. The latter improvement is available because the field has been less crowded.

The deeper problem is that correctness, convergence, and timing require different cognitive modes that are in partial tension with each other. Deep correctness work requires slow, patient, analytical thinking — the epistemic equivalent of carefully building a structural model from atomic elements. Convergence thinking requires a kind of sociological imagination — the ability to model how other minds process information, what makes narratives sticky, which investors are likely to be natural buyers under which conditions. Timing requires a kind of comfortable uncertainty — the capacity to hold a probability distribution rather than a point estimate about when something will happen, and to size positions accordingly.

These modes are not mutually reinforcing. The analyst who is very good at deep research often develops a distaste for the social dynamics of convergence thinking… it feels less rigorous, more like market timing or narrative speculation. The investor who is attuned to convergence dynamics may struggle to maintain conviction through the slow-building periods where the correctness thesis is accumulating but not yet visible. The patient temperamentalist may hold positions so long that they miss the exit window where timing was on their side.

The great investors, not the ones who had a great decade, but the ones who compounded over multiple cycles, have solved some version of this cognitive integration problem. They have found a way to hold correctness rigor, convergence awareness, and timing probability simultaneously, and to make allocation decisions that reflect all three. This is not a skill that can be taught as a framework; it is a cognitive architecture that has to be built through experience, reflection, and repeated exposure to the specific failure modes of each variable.

The three-body problem, in other words, cannot be simplified into a checklist. It can only be internalized as a way of thinking.

A Note on Intelligence

Intelligence — in the sense of raw analytical capacity, the ability to model complex systems accurately, to synthesize information from disparate sources, to identify logical inconsistencies, is necessary for correctness. There is no way around this. The investor who cannot model a business accurately cannot achieve genuine differentiated insight.

But intelligence is profoundly insufficient for the full three-body problem. Intelligence optimizes for correctness. It has no special relationship with convergence, which is a problem of social modeling, political economy, and institutional knowledge. It has a near-zero relationship with timing, which is a problem of patience, probabilistic endurance, and capital structure management.

The investment track records of the smartest people on Wall Street and Dalal Street are not the best track records. They are frequently very good — intelligence does help, and the correlation is positive, but the relationship is far weaker than anyone who entered finance through the intelligence-selection channel would prefer to believe. The smartest fund managers, systematically, have an underperformance problem around convergence. They identify non-consensus ideas. They are early. They are frequently correct. They exit before or shortly after the convergence event because their mental models have moved on, and the position now feels fully understood rather than exciting.

This is the cognitive tax of intelligence: the signal that tells you a thesis is interesting and novel is the same signal that degrades as the thesis becomes well-understood. Smart investors are bored by their best ideas at exactly the wrong moment, when the idea is beginning to propagate through the market, when convergence is beginning to accelerate, when staying is worth more than moving to the next interesting thing.

The investor who compounds most reliably is not always the smartest. It is the investor who has built an architecture — intellectual, emotional, institutional, that keeps them correctly positioned across all three variables simultaneously, for longer than the typical cycle, with enough capital and psychological stability to survive the nonlinear interactions between them.

This is harder than being smart. It is also, ultimately, the thing that gets paid.

Coda: The Open Problem

Poincaré did not solve the three-body problem. But his work on it produced something arguably more valuable: he discovered that the question was not about finding a solution, but about understanding the geometry of the solution space. In studying why the problem was unsolvable, he invented topology and laid the foundations for modern dynamical systems theory. The inability to solve the problem led to a deeper understanding of what problems of this type actually are.

Something similar is available to the investor who takes this framing seriously. The three-body problem of investing cannot be solved in the sense of finding an algorithm that reliably optimizes all three variables simultaneously. But understanding the structure of the problem — that it is genuinely nonlinear, that correctness does not dominate, that intelligence is a ticket to the game rather than a guarantee of winning it, changes the cognitive posture you bring to every investment decision.

You start asking different questions. Not just “is this thesis correct?” but “what is the specific mechanism by which the market comes to see what I see, and when?” Not just “what is the intrinsic value?” but “what is the distribution of outcomes for the timing variable, and how does position sizing reflect that distribution?” Not just “is this a good business?” but “which of these three problems am I best equipped to solve in this specific situation, and where is the interaction between them most likely to produce a pleasant surprise or a painful one?”

These are harder questions than the ones most investors are asking. They are also, by a significant margin, the more important ones.

The three-body problem remains open. In physics, this is a provable mathematical truth. In investing, it is a practical one: the market persists precisely because the problem of simultaneously achieving correctness, convergence, and timing remains unsolved for most participants, most of the time.

The edge is available to anyone who truly internalizes the structure of the problem, not just anyone who is smart enough to identify it.

Those are very different things.

The irony of writing this is not lost on me: the act of publishing a framework for thinking about mispricings is, itself, a convergence mechanism. The thesis propagates. The edge, partially, closes. This is not a reason not to write it — the closing is slow, and the understanding of the framework is not the same as the capacity to apply it. But it is worth noting, at the end, that even the most accurate map has a reflexive relationship with the territory it describes. Poincaré would have appreciated that.
