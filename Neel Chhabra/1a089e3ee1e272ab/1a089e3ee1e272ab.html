Deep Tech Investing in India — with Vishesh Rajaram, Speciale Invest

Lessons on capital, time, and what a fund buys when there are no metrics.

Neel Chhabra
Sep 10
 
READ IN APP
 

Vishesh Rajaram invests in things that do not work yet.

Rocket engines printed rather than machined. Fermentation that makes protein without an animal. Electrolysers without membranes. Fusion. When Speciale Invest started nine years ago, none of it had a name yet, and raising the first five hundred thousand dollars for any of it was hard.

The problem with backing science is that every instrument other investors use to know whether they were right is missing. Public investors have price. Private equity has cash flows. Growth investors have multiples. A deep tech company can be worth half a billion dollars with no revenue, no product in market, and no way to check.

So what is he actually underwriting? And how does he know when he is wrong?

Manoj Dua and I put both questions to him for an hour.

Give David a catapult

Eighteen lessons on time, conviction, and betting on science.

1. Capital and time are the only two axes. Put them on x and y, and the whole job becomes one question: does the risk in this business decline as both get spent? Everything else is commentary. “The only thing that is fungible is time.”

2. Work backwards from earnings, not forwards from the idea. Shareholders eventually pay for earnings, so start at the far end and reverse the sequence. “If you have to make earnings ten, fifteen years from now, what should you be doing for the first one year, two years, three years, four years, five years? What is the dotted line that gets you to this holy grail of being a sustainable company?”

3. The question is never whether. It is when. Speciale invests believing every company in the portfolio will eventually be a sustainable business. The uncertainty being priced is timing, and the value an investor adds is compressing it. “The uncertainty is when — and our ability to add value, because we increasingly get better at unravelling their uncertainty.”

4. Returns live in the gap between true and verified. In a field with no metrics, the source of return is behavioural rather than quantitative. The more uncertainty and the wider the information asymmetry, the larger the prize for being early and correct. And then, one day, someone can explain it simply to a stranger and the market has its moment of recognition.

5. Irrationality is a relative measurement. Everyone describes deep tech founders as irrational. Vishesh will not. He does not think they are irrational at all; he thinks the word is a report on where the speaker is standing. “I am irrational when compared to where your rationality stands, but I am reasonably rational vis-à-vis my rationality.”

6. The trait is withstanding pain, and it does not come from insecurity. In a founder’s life there are a few moments of success and mostly there is failure, so the screening question is who can absorb ten days of pain for one good one. When I suggested it comes from a chip on the shoulder, he corrected me flatly: “I’ve never sensed any insecurity with the founders that I’ve dealt with. There’s a great zeal of conviction that this problem needs to be solved.”

7. Great founders hold a telescope and a microscope at once. Ask Agnikul’s Srinath about ten years and you get reusability, orbital vehicles, an entire architecture. Ask about the next twelve months and you get it week by week. Neither lens alone is worth much. “You are able to see far vision, but you are also able to see very granular now.”

8. Take the meeting looking for the one reason to say yes. Enter a room hunting for two reasons to decline and you will find them in the best company you ever see. The discipline runs the other way. “You do not need ten reasons. What is the one reason I am going to invest in this company?” Optimism here is a method, not a temperament.

9. Diligence protects you from mistakes. It cannot produce a decision. Legal, financial, integrity, KYC — all of it is table stakes, and all of it is negative information. “In my view those are things that prevent you from doing wrong things. They do not … help you do the right things.” If the file looks bad you will not invest. If the file looks good you still will not invest. Something else has to happen.

10. That something else is a question about losing. His personal tipping point is not a spreadsheet. It arrives when he looks at a founder and believes they will spend everything they have on this. “Is this a founder you’re okay to lose along with?” You can only win or lose a game. Losing one you played well is survivable.

11. In deep tech you make money by killing what can go wrong. Venture usually talks about what can go right, because the asymmetry does the work. Science inverts it. Value accrues each time a specific technical risk is retired, which is exactly why the next round prices higher — the buyer is purchasing a de-risked profile, not a bigger dream. He agreed without hedging.

12. Failure has two doors, and only one of them is dishonest. The first is running out of shots on goal before the science or the ecosystem is ready. That is the cost of doing business. The second is mal-intent, and in his run at Speciale he says he has not met it. The profile makes it unlikely: first-time founders, scarce capital, no fallback, nothing else being thought about.

13. A sector is ready when several levers move at once. Space opened because the cost of putting a kilogram in orbit collapsed at the same time as the payload shrank from a hundred kilograms to ten. Either alone is an improvement. Together they are a different industry. “Whenever the cost of technology comes down, adoption increases.”

14. David beat Goliath because he had a catapult. This is the sentence to keep. The lesson children are taught is about courage; the mechanism was a weapon that changed the terms of engagement. Agnikul could take on rockets because metal 3D printing arrived — old rocketry science, new manufacturing, a combination unavailable forty years ago. “There is no catapult, there is no way to win the game.” So the sector question becomes: what just arrived that lets a small company fight a large one?

15. Science is a means to economics, and the order cannot be reversed. Printing a rocket is science. Producing rockets faster is economics, and it is the point. Great science that is not economic is research, and research is not a venture asset. Put the economics before the science, he says, and you end up understanding neither.

16. Learning is the sourcing strategy. Access is temporary; a good founder will meet ten investors, not one. What survives is understanding the risk better than the other nine. Watch how that compounds: Speciale started in electric vehicles, which taught them storage, which taught them lithium, which sent them to hydrogen for trucks and ships, then to sodium-ion and zinc-air for energy held over time — and now to generation, because nuclear has opened to private companies. “Access is a short-term advantage.”

17. Capital is more intellectual than speculative. On the AI build-out he pushed back on the framing rather than the question. Capital mostly understands what it is doing; it only looks speculative when everyone crowds the same trade and game theory takes over. The risk is not that AI is unproductive. It is that five players spending trillions to win the same race can make a good technology into a bad business. Which is not a technology question at all.

18. Run a different race. He does not want India chasing general intelligence, because arriving second at someone else’s race is a plan to finish second. Build small models that are excellent at one domain: aerospace, defence, automotive, drug discovery. “Why do I need general intelligence? I am the general intelligence. I need specific intelligence.” A model that speeds up drug discovery does not need to be good at poetry.

And the one to leave with. Asked what someone in a completely different field should steal from his, he did not offer a framework. “You just have to assume you don’t know anything and be willing to learn.” Then, unprompted: he is not an engineer. He studied commerce and economics. He has come a long way toward appreciating technology without ever practising it, and thinks the distance is available to anyone who bothers to walk it.

About the guest

Vishesh Rajaram is Founding Partner at Speciale Invest, which he co-founded with Arjun Rao and which calls itself, without much competition for the title, the deep tech VC. Nine years in, the firm has deployed over $140 million across more than forty-five companies, writing roughly $1 million at pre-seed and seed and holding back half of each fund for follow-ons. It invests across five areas: novel energy and planetary health, human longevity and synthetic biology, space, AI-led software, and advanced manufacturing.

The portfolio includes Agnikul Cosmos (3D-printed rocket engines), Newtrace (membrane-free electrolysers), Fermbox (precision fermentation), Vyomic, InspeCity, CynLr, GalaxEye, Anubal Fusion, Peptris, E-TRNL Energy, and Ultraviolette Automotive. Offices in Chennai and Bengaluru.

Manoj Dua has been a full-time investor for twenty-five years, running a family office across public and private markets, and was one of the earliest LPs in Speciale.

The conversation starts where the instruments run out. Vishesh explains how a firm underwrites companies with no revenue, no product, and often no company — two and a half people and a business plan — by treating capital and time as the only real constraints and asking what the risk curve looks like against both.

From there: the founder trait that actually predicts survival and why he rejects the psychological story usually attached to it; what happens between a pitch and a first cheque; the ten minutes in a Chennai speed-dating session that produced Agnikul, and the launch that stopped at T-minus eleven seconds; why the seed problem in India is solved and the Series A problem is not; whether a country this early in its capital markets should build a separate exchange for pre-revenue companies; and where value sits in the space and physical AI value chains.
