In the first book of the Politics, Aristotle interrupts an argument about slavery to imagine a way out of it. Suppose tools could do their own work. If “the shuttle would weave and the plectrum touch the lyre” without a hand to guide them, he writes, then the master craftsman would have no need of workers.
He raises it in order to dismiss it. The passage is an argument for slavery. Since tools plainly cannot work themselves, Aristotle reasons, a household requires instruments that are human, and he concludes that some people are natural instruments.
The uncomfortable part is not the conclusion. It is the structure underneath it, which is correct. Aristotle had identified that self-working tools and coerced human labour are substitutes for one another, two ways of solving the same problem, and a society gets one or the other. Marx quoted the same passage in Capital when he arrived at machinery. Norbert Wiener reached for it again in 1950 and stated the equivalence without decoration: the automatic machine is “the precise economic equivalent of slave labor.”
Three writers, two millennia apart, using one image to make the same structural claim. The demand for cheap tractable labour and the demand for autonomous machines are the same demand. It has only ever been satisfied one way at a time, and in about forty words Aristotle isolated the condition that decides which: certain work cannot be separated from the person doing it.
Twenty-three centuries later, Michael Polanyi explained why the shuttle stayed silent. His formulation is six words long and it is the most important sentence in industrial economics: “we can know more than we can tell.” A person recognising a face, riding a bicycle, or judging when a weld has taken cannot fully articulate how. The knowledge is real, demonstrable, and unavailable to language. And what cannot be told cannot be written down. What cannot be written down cannot be copied.
That is why factories cluster. Not wages, which is what everyone measures, but the standing pool of people who already know how, and which can only be grown at the speed of apprenticeship. Nations have spent decades and enormous sums trying to import that pool and have mostly failed. Tacit knowledge is a physical constraint, not an economic one.
Hans Moravec restated Polanyi from the machine’s side in 1988. It was easy, he found, to give a computer adult performance on reasoning tasks and “difficult or impossible to give them the skills of a one-year-old” in perception and movement. Steven Pinker sharpened it six years later: the hard problems are easy and the easy problems are hard. A billion years of selection went into grasping and perhaps a hundred thousand into abstraction. The old code is dense and invisible to us. We undervalue what we do best.
What has changed, and what makes this a live question, is that a class of tacit skill is now being written down. Robot foundation models trained on demonstration are converting sensorimotor competence into weights. Open X-Embodiment pooled more than a million real trajectories across twenty-two different robot bodies and showed the skills transferring between them. Aristotle’s condition is being tested for the first time in twenty-three centuries.
But Polanyi’s paradox is not being repealed. It is being relocated.
The tacit knowledge leaving the assembly worker does not evaporate. It reappears somewhere else in the chain… in the grinding line that holds three microns across a ten-to-one bore, in the metallurgy of a steel cup that flexes a hundred million times without cracking, in the process a supplier learned over thirty years and cannot write down either. The thing that could not be copied has moved. It has not gone.
Which is where the oldest idea becomes the useful one. In 1817 David Ricardo asked why rent existed at all, and answered that it is paid for “the original and indestructible powers of the soil.” Rent does not accrue to what is productive. It accrues to what is scarce and cannot be reproduced. The productive thing gets a return on capital; the irreproducible thing gets the surplus.
Ask Ricardo’s question of a humanoid robot and the answer is not the model. Model weights are the most reproducible asset in the modern economy — Physical Intelligence open-sourced π0 four months after release, OpenVLA and RDT-1B are public, and Google DeepMind is offering Gemini Robotics to anyone who builds a body. That is a productive input. It is not an indestructible one.
The indestructible power in this industry is a threaded steel rod about the length of your forearm, called a planetary roller screw, and about four companies on earth can make one to specification.
The Telescope: Two problems that never close
In 1944 Erwin Schrödinger asked what living things actually do, thermodynamically, and gave an answer that has not been improved on. A living organism holds itself in an improbable arrangement against a universe that is relentlessly dismantling it. It survives, he wrote, because “it feeds upon negative entropy.” Order is not free. It has to be bought, continuously, or it decays.
Engineering and medicine are contained in that one sentence, which is why neither field ever finishes. Every other industry solves a problem that can, in principle, be solved. Entertainment can be satisfied. Transport can be adequate. Entropy and mortality cannot be satisfied or made adequate. They are permanent, compounding and universal, and every civilisation that has existed has spent most of its surplus on them without ever retiring the bill.
Which suggests a way of sorting industries that is more useful than the sector labels. There are two substrates: bodies, and systems. Keeping bodies alive and keeping systems running. Finance, culture, commerce and the rest are derivative, they are how a society organises the payment for those two things. Engineering and healthcare are not sectors sitting alongside the others. They are the platforms the others run on.
The twenty-first century is the first one holding tools sharp enough to attack both systematically. And so the capital, the talent and eventually the geopolitical weight move toward whoever builds the best instruments for seeing inside bodies and inside systems, and toward whoever owns the infrastructure those instruments run on.
Physical AI is the instrument for the second substrate. Jensen Huang’s framing of what changed is unusually precise for a keynote: “AI is now beginning to understand the laws of physics.” A model that has only read cannot maintain anything. A model that can predict how a bolt seats, how a weld cools, how a mass shifts when lifted, can be pointed at entropy directly.
The scale claim follows from the substrate, not from enthusiasm. Global automotive is roughly a hundred million units a year against a species of eight billion, and it addresses mobility, which is a solved-enough problem. A general-purpose machine that maintains systems addresses an unsolved one. That is the honest version of the argument that humanoids become a larger industry than cars, not that more units get sold, but that the underlying problem never closes.
Where the irreproducible thing went
Rollvis and GSA in Switzerland. Bosch Rexroth in Germany. Ewellix in Sweden, bought by Schaeffler in 2022, which tells you what Schaeffler thought it was worth. Between them, something like 70 to 80 percent of the world market. China builds more than half the world’s industrial robots and still imports roughly 80 percent of its roller screws.
The reason is not secrecy. The nut has a length-to-diameter ratio near ten to one, transmission error has to stay inside ±0.003mm across that length, and getting there takes nanoscale grinding followed by surface nitriding on a line tuned over decades. Polanyi would have recognised the situation immediately. The firm cannot fully tell you how it does this. It can only keep doing it.
A humanoid needs several. Total screw content runs to something like 28,000 yuan before anything else is bolted on.
Hold that against the labour it replaces. A robot that learns from human demonstration and from other robots breaks the copying constraint. Skill becomes a file. Once skill is a file, it transfers at the speed of a network rather than the speed of an apprenticeship, and the barriers to entry in goods production fall toward the cost of capital and the cost of the machine. The J-curve is not incremental automation. It is the conversion of a tacit input into a copyable one.
Wassily Leontief gave the uncomfortable version of what follows. Horses were not unemployed by tractors because they lacked retraining; they were rendered economically unnecessary, and the horse population collapsed. He raised it in 1983 as a serious question about labour, not a joke. Whether it applies to people turns on whether human capability has a floor that machines do not reach, which is precisely the question Moravec left open, and precisely why the dexterity argument is an economic argument rather than a technical one.
The rest of the consequences follow mechanically. Unit costs fall. Manufacturing de-concentrates and reshores, because proximity to a labour pool stops being the binding reason to build a plant somewhere. Productivity rises, employment in routine physical work falls, and goods prices deflate. Purchasing power goes up even as wages in affected categories go down. The shape is familiar. It is what happened to information goods after 1995, applied to atoms.
If skill stops being scarce, something else becomes scarce. The intellectual property in the motion hardware. The energy to run the fleet. Access to raw materials, and the capacity to process them into usable form. The bottleneck migrates from people to physics, and the rent that used to accrue to whoever had the workers accrues instead to whoever holds the new constraint. This is Ricardo’s structure, unchanged, with a different soil.
Jensen Huang frames the prize as “the technology industry’s first opportunity to address a $50 trillion industry.” Treat that number carefully, it is the size of the physical economy being touched, not a robot market, and it is a framing offered by the company selling the compute. But the direction is right, and the important question is not how big the pie is. It is which slice stays hard to make.
Follow one actuator into a knee
Abstractions about physical AI are cheap. Let’s walk one part through the chain instead.
A humanoid needs to extend its leg under load. Rotary motion is wrong for this, a knee taking body weight through a squat wants linear force with high stiffness and low backlash. So the joint is built as a linear actuator: a frameless torque motor drives a planetary roller screw through a preloaded thrust bearing, with an absolute encoder reading position and a force-torque sensor closing the loop. Tesla uses a planetary roller screw in Optimus.
Each of those five items is a separate industrial problem. The frameless motor needs high torque density in a tiny volume, which means neodymium-iron-boron magnets, which means China. The roller screw is the Swiss problem described above, and it costs up to an order of magnitude more than the ball screw it replaces. The thrust bearing is a cross-roller or thin-section design from THK, NSK, Schaeffler or Timken. The encoder must be absolute, not incremental, because a robot that loses joint position on power-cycle is a robot that falls over. And the force sensor is the piece almost nobody has solved cheaply.
Now do the rotary joints — shoulders, hips, elbows. Those want a strain-wave reducer, commonly called a harmonic drive: a flexible steel cup deforming inside a rigid ring to produce enormous reduction ratios with near-zero backlash in a package the size of a hockey puck. Harmonic Drive Systems of Japan has held roughly half the world market for decades, not on patents alone but on the metallurgy and grinding process of that flexspline. An articulated industrial robot uses four to six of them. A humanoid uses more.
Schaeffler’s number is the one to hold onto: “around half of all components in a humanoid robot” are actuators, at 25 to 30 units per machine. Add the hands, nineteen percent of the build sheet on Bank of America’s estimate and the hardest subsystem in the product, and you are near seventy percent of the cost before buying a single chip. That seventy is the upper bound and depends on counting hands as motion, which I think is right and which not every teardown does. Fifty is the number I would defend without qualification.
The metric that makes everything legible
Roughly fifty percent — actuator share of bill of materials, is not a statistic so much as a classification. It tells you that a humanoid robot is a motion product that happens to contain a computer, not a computer that happens to move. And motion products obey motion-product economics: long qualification cycles, process-knowledge moats, incumbent persistence measured in decades, and margin that pools at the component rather than the assembly.
Every framework you might apply to this industry changes depending on which classification you accept. Treat it as a software business and you conclude the winner is whoever has the best model, the sector commoditises quickly, and returns accrue to a platform. Treat it as a precision-motion business and you conclude the winner is whoever controls a component that takes seven years to qualify, the sector commoditises slowly and unevenly, and returns accrue to whoever owns the process.
Thirty-eight years on
Moravec’s paradox is holding, which is remarkable given everything that has happened to AI since. Large language models made the easy half nearly free. The hard half is still open. A hand closing on an unfamiliar object, with the right force, in a room it has never been in. Rodney Brooks put it plainly last September [ https://substack.com/redirect/f1da0ff7-5529-4021-af87-2db14e6f417e?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ]: “No human-like robot hands have demonstrated much in the way of dexterity” in any general sense.
There is a fair objection. Arvind Narayanan argues the paradox describes what the AI field found worth working on rather than what is intrinsically hard, and that it therefore predicts nothing. The caution is fair, and treating Moravec as physics would be a mistake. The industrial evidence carries the weight here rather than the philosophy: the money is still failing to buy dexterity, and it has had a great deal of money.
Moravec is usually read as a claim about cognition. It reads just as well as a claim about the value chain. Difficulty is where scarcity sits, and Ricardo already told us who collects when a factor is scarce and cannot be reproduced.
Who keeps the money
Start at the chokepoint, a chokepoint is the node that cannot be bypassed without the chain failing, and precision motion qualifies on every test. There is no humanoid without reducers, screws, torque motors and bearings. There is no substitute technology at the required torque density. There is no path around it through software. And the supplier set is genuinely small: roughly half the harmonic drive market sits with one Japanese firm, and 70 to 80 percent of roller screws sit with four European ones.
Challenge that. Could a large OEM go around it by vertically integrating? Tesla is trying — designing actuators, motors and control electronics in-house. Figure is doing the same. Unitree already did it, and the payoff is visible: SemiAnalysis put the Unitree G1’s bill of materials at $8,976 against a $27,300 education price, an implied gross margin near 67 percent, achieved because Unitree’s self-developed brushless motors cost 30 to 40 percent of comparable Western parts.
That is the strongest argument against the chokepoint, and it is only half right. Vertical integration does not dissolve the chokepoint. It relocates it inside the OEM, where it still has to be built, tuned and qualified over years, and where it consumes capital that the merchant-supply model would have avoided. Tesla’s own capex tells the story: roughly $25 billion committed for FY2026, around triple historical levels, with free cash flow negative $1.1 billion in the June quarter. Musk’s description of Optimus is the honest one — “the hardest product to scale manufacturing that we’ve ever made at Tesla.” That difficulty is the chokepoint being paid for rather than avoided.
Now the residual claimant, the node that absorbs the shock when volumes disappoint. It is the integrator. The humanoid OEM carries inventory risk on 25 to 30 actuators per unit, pre-committed capacity, brand promises, and a customer base that can simply defer. Component suppliers with diversified end-markets absorb a humanoid air-pocket by reallocating a line. Harmonic sells into semiconductors, Schaeffler into autos, THK into machine tools. The assembler cannot. When the correction comes, and it will, it arrives at the assembler first and hardest.
The India distortion in this chain is not an extra layer, which is the usual Indian pattern. It is a missing node. India has genuine capability at machined parts and sub-assembly, real emerging capability in magnets by policy fiat, and effectively nothing at precision motion IP. There is no Indian harmonic reducer at scale. No Indian frameless torque motor at scale. No Indian planetary roller screw in serial production.
In humanoid robotics, precision motion suppliers capture the structural rent because their qualification cycles are measured in years while their customers’ product cycles are measured in months, and that gap has widened rather than narrowed as OEM competition has intensified.
The arc, and the false dawn nobody mentions
This is not the first robotics wave, and the previous one settled a question that the current one is busy re-asking.
Inflection one: the articulated arm finds the car, 1961–1985. Unimate at General Motors, then Fanuc and Yaskawa industrialising it. The lesson buried in that period is where the money went. Not to the integrators. Fanuc built decades of 30-percent-plus operating margins on motion control; Keyence built 50-percent-plus operating margins on sensing; Harmonic Drive built half a global market on a flexspline. The people who bolted these into cells earned single digits.
Inflection two: China decides to own it, 2015–2024. Made in China 2025 named robotics a priority and the state followed with money. By 2024 China installed 295,000 industrial robots, 54 percent of the world and an all-time record, and for the first time domestic makers outsold foreign ones at home. The operational stock passed two million units, about four and a half times Japan’s. Domestic substitution moved up the stack: Chinese harmonic makers now hold perhaps 12 to 15 percent of global revenue and Leaderdrive alone holds 30 to 40 percent of the Chinese market.
Inflection three: the model layer arrives, 2023–2026. RT-2 showed a vision-language model could emit robot actions as tokens. Open X-Embodiment pooled more than a million real trajectories across 22 robot bodies from 34 labs and demonstrated transfer between them. Physical Intelligence’s π0.5 showed that training across roughly a hundred environments approaches the performance of a model trained directly on the test environment. Generalisation stopped being a research aspiration and became an engineering variable.
The false dawn
Rethink Robotics. Rodney Brooks founded it in 2008, the same Brooks now arguing that humanoids will not learn dexterity, to build a collaborative robot that ordinary factory workers could teach by demonstration. Baxter and Sawyer were technically admirable and commercially fatal. The company sold its assets in 2018. The reason matters: the robots were good enough to demonstrate and not good enough to depend on, and the gap between those two states is where robotics companies die. A Sawyer unit reportedly ran in an Oregon factory to 35,236 hours before being retired, proof the hardware worked, and proof that working hardware was not the constraint.
That gap is exactly where humanoids sit today. Impressive demonstration, unproven dependability. The market repeats this mistake because demonstrations are legible and mean-time-between-failure is not.
Phase diagnosis. Globally, humanoid robotics is squarely in Utterback’s fluid phase: no dominant design, competing architectures at every joint, entry and exit both cheap, competition on product rather than process. The component layer beneath it is in the specific phase, standardised, scale-driven, process-competitive, mature. That split is unusual and it is the single most useful structural fact in this primer. A fluid product layer sitting on a specific component layer means the product layer will churn violently while the component layer collects from every contestant.
The economics engine
Three forces determine who earns what here, and they point in different directions at different nodes.
Pricing power. At precision motion IP, it is real and rests on switching costs that are physical rather than contractual: requalifying a reducer means re-testing the joint, re-tuning the controller and re-certifying the machine. At the integrator layer, pricing power is close to zero and falling. Average humanoid selling prices dropped roughly 93 percent between 2020 and 2025 to about $58,000 on Wood Mackenzie’s estimate, and Unitree has since cut the G1 to around $13,500 with an R1 research platform at $4,900. Nothing about that trajectory suggests an assembler holds price.
Cost structure. Humanoid assembly is high fixed cost with violently uncertain volume, the worst combination in industrial economics. Component suppliers run high fixed cost too, but against diversified demand, which turns the same fixed-cost gearing from a liability into an option.
Capital intensity. Here the naive software framing does its most damage. A model company is capital-light and can compound on talent. A precision component line is capital-heavy, and its returns come from utilisation against a qualified order book rather than from growth alone. Harmonic Drive’s June 2026 quarter is the live example: orders up 55.7 percent year-on-year to ¥24.13bn, guidance lifted to ¥8.5bn operating profit, up 231 percent, and management still describing an 11.4 percent margin as, “by historical standards, this is still not satisfactory.” Read the composition before you read the headline. Roughly ¥3bn of those orders were one-time medical device business, and the recovery was driven by surgical-assist robots in North America and semiconductor equipment for generative AI. Humanoids are barely in this number yet. That is what fixed-cost gearing looks like on the way up from a trough, and what a company with a long memory sounds like.
That quarter contains the cycle in miniature, and most of it is not what the headline says. Orders up 55.7 percent, but with roughly an eighth of that one-time medical work and the rest driven by surgical robots and semiconductor equipment. The Chinese subsidiary down 17.9 percent, on a pullback from local robot makers now buying Leaderdrive instead. Harmonic’s own humanoid volumes remain small, with management guiding the meaningful ramp to FY2027. The order book is inflecting. It is not yet inflecting on humanoids, and anyone citing this print as proof of humanoid demand has not read past the first line.
The cost-down curve is the other engine, and it is genuinely steep. Goldman Sachs found manufacturing cost falling to a $30,000–150,000 range with a roughly 40 percent year-on-year decline against its own 15–20 percent forecast. Bank of America models a China-built 2025 bill of materials near $35,000 falling below $17,000 by 2030. Bain sees $10,000–20,000 by 2035.
But the single most revealing number in the entire cost literature is a counterfactual. Analysis cited by McKinsey found that a Tesla Optimus Gen 2 built without Chinese suppliers would cost roughly $131,000 instead of about $46,000. Nearly three times. Nothing about that is a cost curve. It is a geopolitical dependency wearing one as a disguise.
Albert Hirschman worked out the mechanism in 1945, studying how Germany had turned Balkan trade into control. The source of power between nations, he argued, is “the power to interrupt commercial or financial relations.” Not the volume of trade. The credible threat of stopping it. Dependence is only a weapon when the dependent party cannot re-source quickly, which is why refining capacity coerces and mining capacity does not.
The April 2025 controls covered seven medium and heavy rare earths plus magnets, and the effect was immediate, exports collapsed and vehicle plants in the United States, Europe and Japan cut utilisation. Musk spent part of a Tesla earnings call effectively negotiating in public, explaining that the magnets were going into a humanoid robot and “it’s not a weapon.” A chief executive lobbying a foreign ministry through an analyst call is the clearest possible statement of where the power in this chain actually sits.
The October 2025 escalation went further, applying a 0.1 percent de minimis rule to any foreign product containing Chinese-origin rare earths, the same extraterritorial logic the United States uses on semiconductors. That escalation is suspended until November 2026 under the truce. The April controls remain in force. The lever has been demonstrated and then holstered, which is a more useful position for China than firing it.
The rival claim: data is the real chokepoint
Claude Shannon opened his 1948 paper by defining the task: “reproducing at one point a message selected at another point.” He was describing telegraphy. He was also, without meaning to, describing cross-embodiment transfer, a skill acquired by one robot body reproduced in a different one. And Shannon’s central result is the one that matters here. Information is surprise. A message that could have been predicted carries almost nothing; the expensive bits are the unlikely ones.
Which tells you exactly why robot data costs what it does. A demonstration in a tidy laboratory is a low-surprise message. A demonstration in a kitchen the machine has never entered is a high-surprise one, and only the second kind teaches anything.
Components are hard but they are solved problems being re-solved — the physics is known, the process is transferable given enough time and capital, and China is already demonstrating that a determined national effort compresses the timeline. What is not solved, and may not be solvable by capital alone, is teaching a machine to act competently in a physical environment it has never seen. The scarce asset is not the screw. It is the trajectory data that teaches the screw what to do.
Ken Goldberg has framed robotics as facing something like a hundred-thousand-year data gap [ https://substack.com/redirect/e87b8fe3-8ea7-4ac9-b761-ba20964d0a15?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] relative to the corpus that trained language models, and his assessment of the obvious workaround is unsparing: simulation, he notes, is “notoriously unreliable for robot manipulation.” It works for things that fly or walk, where the physics is smooth. It breaks on contact-rich manipulation, where the physics is not.
Which leaves real-world data, collected by humans, at industrial cost. Teleoperation is now a labour market with a visible price curve. Fully-loaded high-quality teleoperation data fell from roughly $340 per hour in early 2024 to about $136 by the fourth quarter of 2025 and a $118 benchmark by March 2026. Hold those figures loosely: they are vendor-published and unaudited, and I have not been able to verify them independently. The levels recur across sources that do not appear to share an origin, which is the only reason I use them at all. Operators in India, the Philippines and Eastern Europe earn $22 to $55 an hour against $65 to $120 for United States operators with domain expertise. A skilled operator produces five to fifty episodes an hour; a fifty-thousand-episode dataset costs on the order of $67,000 in collection labour before quality assurance, and a five-thousand-demonstration whole-body programme runs $250,000 to $750,000.
That is why Figure and 1X build their own data pipelines, and why the industry’s sharpest strategic disagreement is about method rather than importance. Brett Adcock has committed Figure to autonomy without teleoperation in the field. Bernt Børnich has built the opposite: 1X ships NEO into homes with human operators behind it, on the explicit logic that “if we don’t have your data, we can’t make the product better.” Both agree data is the moat. They disagree completely on how to mine it.
Why it is a weaker moat than it looks
Three reasons, and they compound.
First, the field is publishing. Open X-Embodiment pooled over a million real trajectories across 22 robot bodies and made them public. π0 was open-sourced four months after release. OpenVLA and RDT-1B are freely available. Compare that to Harmonic Drive’s flexspline process, which has been proprietary for five decades and shows no sign of leaking.
Second, the economics run the wrong way. Data collection is a cost curve falling at roughly 30 percent a year, the $340-to-$118 path above. Moats built on things getting cheaper are not moats. Precision component capability is a cost curve that barely moves, because the binding input is time-in-process rather than labour rate.
Third, and most damaging, the data flywheel belongs to whoever is doing real work, not to whoever is building models. Physical Intelligence’s π0.6 with RECAP improves from deployment experience, reporting throughput gains up to 130 percent. That is a genuine advance, and it means the compounding asset accrues to the party with robots in the field earning revenue, not to the party with the best research team. Model developers are raising at valuations that assume the opposite. Physical Intelligence is reportedly near $11 billion, roughly double where it sat four months earlier.
Data is a real chokepoint at the level of capability and a weak one at the level of economics. It determines whether humanoids work. It does not determine who gets paid.
Who pays, and for what
Ronald Coase asked in 1937 why firms exist at all, given that markets are supposed to allocate everything efficiently. His answer was that “the operation of a market costs something” — search, negotiation, contracting, and a firm exists wherever doing the thing internally is cheaper than transacting for it. Every business model question in humanoid robotics is a version of that question. Does the customer buy the machine, or rent the work the machine does?
The industry has not settled it, and the models pay out very differently.
Coase’s test cuts cleanly here. A humanoid is worth buying when its work is predictable enough to specify in advance, and worth renting when it is not — which is why every serious deployment so far is a rental into a warehouse, where the task is legible and the failure mode is a dropped tote. Nobody is selling humanoids into unstructured work, because nobody can yet write the contract that would describe it.
Which returns the argument to where it started. The models that require dexterity have no revenue. The models that have revenue require none. And the supplier selling actuators into all six columns above is indifferent to which one wins.
The players, sorted by where they stand
The useful way to read this table is not by company quality. It is by node. A mediocre company at Node 02 has better structural economics than an excellent company at assembly, and the last robotics cycle proved it.
Harmonic Drive is the sector’s thermometer. It sells to nearly every serious humanoid programme in the West and it reports quarterly, which makes it the only public, real-time, un-hyped measure of whether this industry is building anything. Used properly, that means reading the segment composition rather than the order headline. Humanoid-linked orders have run at roughly ¥1.3bn in a quarter against a ¥24bn consolidated book, real, disclosed, and about five percent. Management guides the step-up to FY2027. When a company with fifty years of cycle memory calls an 11.4 percent margin not satisfactory after a 231 percent profit upgrade, it is telling you it does not think the humanoid cycle has arrived yet.
Schaeffler is the most instructive strategic bet in the industry. It bought Ewellix in 2022, before humanoids were a market. It has disclosed roughly 45 humanoid customer conversations and five signed contracts. And critically, it is sizing the opportunity at around 10 percent of the actuator addressable market rather than betting the company. A Tier-1 that recognises an option behaves exactly this way. Buy the capability early, price it conservatively, let the automotive business fund the wait. Hyundai Mobis is running the same play, agreeing at CES 2026 to supply actuators for Boston Dynamics’ next Atlas. The auto-parts-to-humanoid-actuator migration is the defining corporate move of this cycle, and no Indian tier-1 has made it.
How hard are they competing?
Differently at each node.
At assembly, this is already a price war and it has barely started. Prices fell 93 percent in five years. Unitree, well funded, has priced explicitly to take share. Product differentiation between humanoid bodies is collapsing toward the spec sheet. Chinese local governments are subsidising embodied intelligence as a strategic sector, with McKinsey putting state guidance-fund commitments to AI and robotics near $138 billion. Every condition for margin destruction is present.
At the component layer, the structure is a rational duopoly under attack. Harmonic Drive and Leaderdrive together account for more than 60 percent of global harmonic revenue. Pricing has held in the West and eroded in China. New entrant activity is high but concentrated in China and aimed at the domestic market. The signal to watch is whether Chinese reducer and screw makers begin exporting aggressively into Western humanoid programmes, that is the moment the component moat starts behaving like the solar value chain rather than the machine-tool one.
Competitive intensity at the integrator node is a full price war; at the precision component node it is a rational oligopoly with a visible Chinese challenger. The risk to that assessment is qualification-cycle compression, if a Chinese reducer maker gets qualified into a Western tier-1 programme in three years rather than seven, the moat argument weakens materially.
The India question, answered narrowly
Alexander Gerschenkron spent his career on a question India keeps asking itself. Writing in 1962 about how Germany and Russia industrialised after Britain, he identified “the advantages of economic backwardness” — the latecomer skips the dead ends, imports the mature technology, and jumps a generation. But he attached a condition that gets dropped whenever the phrase is quoted. Backwardness only converts into advantage where a substitute institution supplies what the latecomer lacks: German investment banks did it, the Russian state did it. Without the substituting institution, backwardness stays backwardness.
Which makes the India claim testable rather than patriotic. The claim is that unlike every previous technology wave, India has real capability here… precision machined components and sub-assemblies, housings, gear blanks, structural elements, on the back of a competitive automotive and aerospace machining base.
The claim is true. It is also considerably narrower than it sounds, and the narrowness is the investable part. Gerschenkron’s condition is where it gets decided: India has the machining base, and it does not yet have the substituting institution for the part it lacks.
Start with what India genuinely has. Three decades of supplying global automotive and aerospace tier-1s produced a real machining and forging base with real process discipline: Bharat Forge, Sundram Fasteners, Sona Comstar, Craftsman, Sansera, Happy Forgings, and a specialist aerospace cohort in Azad Engineering, MTAR and Paras Defence. These firms hold certifications, run statistical process control, and pass customer audits from Boeing, Airbus, GE and Siemens. Housings, structural elements and gear blanks for humanoids are directly within that envelope. This is not aspiration. It is existing capability pointed at a new customer.
Now the part the national conversation skips. Node 02 (Precision Motion IP) the actual margin pool, is essentially absent. There is no Indian harmonic reducer in serial production. No Indian frameless torque motor at scale. No Indian planetary roller screw shipping in volume. India’s machine tool base, which is the capital equipment you need to make any of these, is weak and largely imported. And until the rare earth magnet scheme delivers, every motor built in India will contain a Chinese magnet.
MTAR Technologies is the sharpest illustration of both halves at once. Its annual report notes roller screw first articles complete and “awaiting certification from customer to commence batch production.” Management told on the earnings call that ball screws are now qualified with multinational customers. These are two of the exact component families inside the actuator stack.
And it has never once described this as a robotics opportunity. The framing on every call is space, defence and clean energy import substitution. The only artificial intelligence reference on MTAR’s calls is to data centres as a power customer.
The bearings are worth pausing on, because this is where a screen goes wrong. Search Indian listed equities for “bearings” and you get a dozen names, and most of them are in a different business entirely. Menon Bearings makes bimetal engine bearings, bushes and thrust washers, twenty-eight million a year, competently, for diesel engines and compressors. Those are plain bearings: a sliding surface running on a hydrodynamic oil film. A robot joint needs a rolling-element bearing, where hardened rollers carry the load through point contact at defined preload. Same word, different physics, different qualification, different customer. Menon is a good company and it is not in this chain.
NRB Bearings is. It has roughly sixty percent of India’s needle roller bearing market, and needle rollers are the rolling elements themselves, the hardened cylinders that a cross-roller assembly arranges at alternating angles. More to the point, NRB is moving in the right direction on its own initiative: a seventy-five percent joint venture with Italy’s Unitec Group to make industrial cylindrical roller bearings with assured off-take, and the acquisition of Bangalore’s Mahant Toolroom for precision aerospace machining. FY26 revenue of ₹1,335 crore, ROCE at twenty percent, and a stated ambition of ₹2,500 crore by 2031 across six growth vectors including one management calls mission-critical friction solutions.
Read the vector list and robotics is not on it.
Then, in July 2026, one company stopped being illegible. On its Q1 FY27 call Sona Comstar named robotics and physical AI as its third growth engine, and it was careful about the tense: “We’re announcing that Sona Comstar has already entered.” Not an intention. Three signed orders — an advanced robotic subsystem, perception engineering services for autonomous mobile robots, and a complete short-range radar perception stack for an Indian commercial vehicle maker. Together they added ₹600 crore, taking the robotics order book to ₹800 crore, about three percent of a ₹24,000 crore total. Production on the first starts this quarter.
What makes it worth reading twice is the argument management used, because it is this primer’s argument arriving from the other direction. A reducer in an electric car runs a ratio near nine to one. The same reducer in a humanoid joint runs between a hundred and a hundred and sixty to one… smaller, far more precise, and in their framing fundamentally the same product. Motor companies, they observed, are the ones who end up making frameless torque motors. That is Node 02 described by an operator who has to build the thing, and it arrived without any prompting from the equity market.
Sona is not a pure play and nobody should pretend otherwise. Three percent of an order book is three percent, the robotics revenue is immaterial today, and the company’s own history suggests a new vertical takes seven or eight years to reach a hundred million dollars, though management thinks this one moves faster, and says the market is developing faster than they assumed. But the illegibility that made the name interesting is gone. It was priced as an auto ancillary with an EV kicker. It has now told the market, in numbers, that it is something else.
The rest of the pattern holds. Tube Investments of India talks about semiconductors and medical devices, not robotics. Sansera and Craftsman discuss non-auto diversification without naming this end-market. Bharat Forge signed an MoU with Germany’s Agile Robots [ https://substack.com/redirect/8fe563de-5c8f-4318-a66a-731df154c0fb?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] to work on AI-driven industrial automation and autonomous factory operations, and then held an earnings call a few weeks later without mentioning robotics at all. Azad Engineering makes precision actuating components and describes qualification as taking “years, sometimes a decade of process validation” — the single most relevant sentence any Indian executive has said about this industry, delivered about aerospace.
The one genuine Indian humanoid voice sits outside the listed market. Addverb, majority-owned by Reliance, announced a humanoid programme with an initial run of around a hundred units, and its chief executive is refreshingly plain about the demand base: “Reliance is our largest consumer of robots.” That is a captive-demand model, which is how Korean and Japanese robotics both started.
The state is solving the right bottleneck, slowly
Give the policy apparatus its due. The Rare Earth Permanent Magnet scheme approved in November 2025 [ https://substack.com/redirect/37457a52-bde3-4d92-a6c9-a10659bb8d49?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] commits ₹7,280 crore for 6,000 tonnes a year of sintered magnet capacity across five beneficiaries, each taking 600 to 1,200 tonnes. The global tender opened in March 2026 and bids closed in May. But the number that matters sits upstream of the scheme, not inside it. IREL’s NdPr oxide capacity is roughly 400 tonnes a year, plus a held stock of about 500 tonnes, together supporting something near 1,500 tonnes of finished magnet. The scheme targets 6,000. India is subsidising four times the magnet capacity its own oxide can feed, and the balance has to be imported, from the country the scheme exists to reduce dependence on. Against FY2025 imports of 53,748 tonnes, six thousand tonnes is about eleven percent of current demand, which sounds thin until you remember that magnets for robotics are a fraction of the total and that the strategic value is optionality, not self-sufficiency. Ashwini Vaishnaw’s claim that import dependence “will practically reduce to zero” is a political sentence, not an engineering one. But the direction is correct and the bottleneck chosen is the right one.
What is missing is anything comparable for precision motion. There is no reducer mission, no roller screw programme, no machine tool push. The draft National Strategy on Robotics went out for consultation in late 2023 and appears to still be a draft. India is funding the input it can buy its way into and ignoring the input that requires a decade of process learning, which is understandable politically and backwards industrially.
And there is a second Indian position: data. Indian and India-linked firms are already on the supply side of the teleoperation curve — iMerit moving into robot data collection, Human Archive out of San Francisco and Bengaluru, Neocambrian, Deccan AI. Indian physical-AI startups raised roughly $155 million across 31 deals in 2026 year-to-date. That is small money and a real position, and it is the one node where India’s cost structure is an advantage rather than an irrelevance.
The cycle and the clock
Three clocks are running and they disagree.
The capital cycle is early and heating. Capital is flooding into assembly and models, Physical Intelligence near $11 billion, Unitree at roughly 857 times projected 2026 earnings on Reuters Breakingviews’ arithmetic, against a STAR Market average near 130, with other outlets running the multiple past 1,000 depending on the earnings base used, and a retail tranche oversubscribed about eight thousand times. Capacity announcements exceed demonstrated demand by an order of magnitude. In capital cycle terms this is the build phase, which is precisely when the returns on new capacity are highest in narrative and lowest in reality. The component layer, by contrast, is emerging from a genuine trough: Harmonic’s China business is still declining, and Western orders have only just inflected.
The technology clock is fluid at the top and specific at the bottom. No dominant humanoid design exists. The reducer, the screw and the bearing are settled technologies. That split will persist for years.
The India formalisation clock has not started. India installed roughly 8,500 industrial robots in 2023, up 59 percent, and roughly seventh globally, against China’s 295,000. Robot density in India is a fraction of Korea’s 1,220 per ten thousand employees. India is not a deployment market for humanoids this decade at prevailing wage levels, and pretending otherwise is the fastest way to misread the opportunity.
Where that leaves us: the appropriate posture is owning the component layer through the assembly-layer correction, because the correction is coming to the residual claimant first and the component supplier’s diversified order book absorbs it. The signal that would change this posture is a Chinese precision-motion supplier winning qualification into a Western tier-1 humanoid programme in under four years.
What breaks this, and what to own
The bear case
Frank Knight drew the distinction in 1921 that most of this sector’s forecasting ignores. Uncertainty, he insisted, is “radically distinct from the familiar notion of risk.” Risk has a distribution you can price. Uncertainty does not, and dressing it in a distribution does not create one. Every humanoid number past 2027 is Knightian uncertainty wearing a probability mass. Profit, in Knight’s system, accrues precisely to whoever bears the second kind, which is an argument for owning the component supplier whose order book is real and against paying for a scenario.
Rodney Brooks is not a crank. He co-founded iRobot, he built the collaborative robot company that failed for exactly the reasons humanoids might fail, and his argument has a mechanism rather than a mood. Vision-trained models cannot learn dexterity because dexterity runs on force and touch, and there is no touch equivalent of the internet’s image corpus, no dataset that encodes what seventeen thousand receptors in a human hand are doing during a grasp. Robert Playter, running the most technically accomplished humanoid programme in the world, said the generality needed to handle a thousand different parts sensibly [ https://substack.com/redirect/f356be8d-ac26-43e7-8ecb-f4f23179f516?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] had not been proven. Neither man is short the sector. Both are telling you the hard part is still hard.
Add the base rates. Global industrial robot installations in 2024 were 542,000, the second-highest year ever, and still two percent below the 2022 peak, with the United States down nine percent, Japan down four and Germany down five. The existing, working, thirty-year-old robotics industry is not growing outside China. Humanoids are being forecast to add a billion units to a market that cannot currently grow at all in developed economies.
And the maintenance number: service and maintenance on industrial robots typically runs 10 to 20 percent of purchase price annually. At $17,000 a unit that is $2,000 to $3,400 a year against an Indian manufacturing wage that is frequently lower. The labour arbitrage is weakest exactly where labour is cheapest. India is a supplier to this industry, not a customer for it.
The acceleration case
The bull argument that deserves respect is not the TAM number. It is Physical Intelligence’s finding that training across roughly a hundred environments approaches the performance of training on the test environment itself. Sergey Levine concedes there are no standard benchmarks yet to verify it. But if generalisation really does scale faster than linearly with environmental diversity, the dexterity wall is a data problem rather than a physics problem. Data problems fall to capital. Everything about the timeline turns on that. Which kind of problem dexterity turns out to be is the question underneath every forecast in this sector.
The second-order beneficiary
If humanoids scale even to Goldman’s relatively sober 1.4 million units by 2035, the binding constraint is not robots. It is qualified precision machining capacity outside China. Every Western and Japanese humanoid programme will need housings, structural elements, gear blanks and machined sub-assemblies from a supplier that is audited, certified, and specifically not Chinese, because after April 2025 no serious industrial buyer will build a single-source Chinese dependency into a strategic product line.
India has roughly two hundred such suppliers already certified by aerospace and automotive primes. They are currently valued as auto ancillaries and defence adjacencies. None of them is valued as a physical-AI supplier, because none of them has said they are one. The re-rating does not require the humanoid thesis to be right about a billion units. It requires it to be right about a few hundred thousand, sourced defensively.
The investment angle:
The structural winner is the precision motion component supplier at Node 02, because its qualification cycle is seven to twelve years while its customers’ product cycles are six to eighteen months, and that asymmetry converts every new humanoid entrant into a customer rather than a competitor.
The current opportunity is the Indian precision machining and forging cohort at Node 03, where genuine certified capability trades at auto-ancillary multiples because management has not yet named the end-market, narrative illegibility, not a capability gap.
The thesis breaks if a Chinese reducer or roller screw maker gets qualified into a Western tier-1 humanoid programme inside four years. That would prove the process-knowledge moat is a capital problem rather than a time problem, and the component layer would then commoditise the way solar did rather than the way machine tools did.
Telescope: Manufacturing de-concentrates, goods deflate, and margin migrates to whoever holds the knowledge and resource chokepoints. On current evidence that is China and the United States.
Microscope: India’s realistic claim is Node 03: housings, gear blanks, structural sub-assemblies, forgings, plus the teleoperation labour on the supply side of a falling cost curve.
Parallax: India is being discussed as a demand market for humanoids, where the wage arbitrage is weakest. It is a supplier. Gerschenkron’s condition decides which one it stays.
Aristotle’s shuttle is finally moving. It took twenty-three centuries, and it is arriving not because anyone solved Polanyi’s paradox but because a narrow class of tacit skill turned out to be recordable after all. He would have understood the consequence better than most of the people forecasting it: the substitution he described runs in one direction at a time, and the countries that have been competing on tractable labour are about to find out which side of it they are on. Everything else that could not be told is still in the building. It has simply moved down the corridor, from the worker who could not explain his hands to the supplier who cannot explain his grinding line.
Ricardo would have found the current pricing strange. The market is paying eleven billion dollars for a model layer that publishes its own weights, and auto-ancillary multiples for firms that can hold three microns across a ten-to-one bore. One of those is the original and indestructible power. The other is a productive input with a public repository.
Which leaves the question the whole thing rests on. If the hard part is the part we were built not to notice, what else are we currently valuing at zero?
