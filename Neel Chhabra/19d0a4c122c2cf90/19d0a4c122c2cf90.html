The Gravity of Winners

A Brief History of How Value Learns to Concentrate, and What Comes Next

Neel Chhabra
Mar 20
 
READ IN APP
 

There is a pattern running through the entire history of commerce that is obvious in retrospect and nearly invisible in the moment.

A new technology eliminates some friction that previously capped how far a company’s value proposition could travel. The cap lifts. The market expands. And then, not gradually, not proportionally, but in a sudden lurch that looks inevitable only after it happens, the best competitor captures a share of value that would have seemed delusional to anyone reasoning from the prior equilibrium.

This is not disruption. What actually happens is closer to a phase transition: the system isn’t reformed, it’s restructured at a molecular level. The brewer becomes AB InBev. The bookseller becomes Amazon. The taxi dispatch becomes Uber, then maybe something larger. Each time, observers focus on the obvious thing, the new entrant, the new product, the new price, and miss the less obvious thing: the new geometry of the market, where distance, in some critical dimension, has just been zeroed out.

Understanding why value concentrates… why the pattern recurs across centuries, technologies, and geographies… is not just historically interesting. It is the most important structural fact about competitive capitalism. Every investment thesis that has ever compounded generational wealth has been, at its core, a bet on this pattern playing out in some specific domain, in some specific time window, before the market fully priced the new geometry.

But here is what most histories of this pattern miss, and what Joshua Ramo understood before almost anyone else: each successive wave doesn’t merely concentrate more value than the last. It changes the nature of what concentration means. The railroad baron owned more of one kind of market. The network platform owns something else entirely, something that has no prior name, that the old frameworks cannot measure, and that the old institutions cannot contain.

Connection changes the nature of an object.

Let’s run the tape.

The Medieval Equilibrium: Monopoly as Geography

The default state of pre-industrial commerce was, paradoxically, a world of local monopolies. Not because kings or guilds necessarily enforced them, though they did, often, and viciously… but because geography enforced them structurally, without needing any human intermediary.

A brewer in 1780 served a single town, if that. Not from lack of ambition, there’s no particular reason to believe medieval brewers were less acquisitive than their descendants, but because beer is heavy, perishable, and the roads were a catastrophe. The cost of moving product beyond cart-hauling distance consumed whatever margin might theoretically have been captured. Geography was overhead. Distance was pricing power for the local competitor who happened to exist.

This creates an interesting first-principles observation about those local monopolies: they were soft. Protected not by a genuine competitive advantage… a lower cost structure, a superior product, a proprietary relationship, but by a friction cost that shielded everyone equally. The bad brewer and the good brewer both survived, because the cost to the customer of accessing the competitor was exactly the same as the cost to the competitor of accessing new customers. Friction is symmetric. It protects incumbents from challengers in precisely the same proportion it protects bad incumbents from good ones.

This is the regime economists call “high transport costs” and that everyone else calls “before modernity.” Fragmentation. Local pricing power. Low capital intensity. A total absence of winner-take-most dynamics. Nobody accumulates absurd fractions of market value because nobody can. The market is defined by the physical circle reachable from your loading dock.

The implication runs deep. Most of the concentrated wealth that defines modern capitalism does not exist in this world. A Roman pottery magnate, an Athenian grain merchant, a medieval wool trader, rich men by the standards of their time, but structurally capped. You cannot own 40% of a market you cannot physically serve.

Then, over roughly two and a half centuries, a series of technologies eliminated that ceiling. Not all at once. In punctuated lurches, each of which restructured the geometry of competition and made the prior equilibrium’s value distribution look quaint.

The Railroad: When Geography Became a Variable

The railroad is not, primarily, a transportation technology. It is a market-creation technology that looks like transportation.

Before the American rail network knitted the continent together between 1840 and 1880, the United States was economically closer to a collection of regional economies than a single national market. The price of wheat in Chicago bore only a loose, lagged relationship to the price of wheat in New York, not because traders were irrational, but because the arbitrage was expensive. Moving wheat across that distance consumed the spread. Geography maintained the local equilibrium.

Rail dissolved regional price differentials. When you can move a bushel of wheat from Chicago to New York in three days for trivial cost, prices converge. The regional premium disappears. And the competitor who had been protected by that premium… the local miller, the regional distributor, the neighborhood general store, suddenly faces competition from someone he never had to think about before.

What happens next follows a template we will see repeat across every subsequent technology: the expanded market selects for a different type of competitor than the fragmented market did. The fragmented market selects for geographic depth… the ability to serve a specific local market with high reliability. The expanded market selects for operational scale… the ability to produce at lower unit cost than any local producer because you are spreading fixed costs across a dramatically larger volume base.

These are not incremental improvements on the same capability. They are different capabilities. The competitor who won under the prior regime often finds himself structurally disadvantaged in the new one. He has the wrong shape for the new geometry.

John D. Rockefeller understood this with a precision that bordered on the predatory. The oil business before Standard wasn’t dominated by incompetent people. It was dominated by regional actors doing the sensible thing… extracting oil from their region and selling it regionally… in a world where the cost of building national distribution was prohibitive. Rockefeller’s insight wasn’t that oil was valuable or that refining could be optimized. His insight was that the rail network had created the possibility of a national distribution system, and that whoever built that system first, at a scale that made it structurally impossible to replicate, would own the pricing relationship with everyone upstream and downstream.

Standard Oil, at its peak, controlled roughly 91% of US oil refining capacity. In the fragmented world of 1860, such a number would have been not just implausible but conceptually incoherent, you can’t own 91% of a market that doesn’t exist as a single market. The railroad created the single market. The single market created the winner. The winner captured 91%.

The template is established. We will not deviate from it.

Brands: The Invention of Cognitive Distance

The railroad solved the physical distribution problem. What it didn’t solve was the trust problem. A consumer in Boston buying a tin of crackers shipped from Ohio faces a question that had never existed before: how do I know this is good?

In the local market, the answer was social. You knew the baker. The baker’s reputation was embedded in the community in a way that was self-enforcing, sell a bad loaf, you told everyone, consequences were immediate and local.

In the national market, that mechanism breaks. The producer in Ohio and the consumer in Boston have no social relationship, no shared community, no mutual accountability structure. The transaction is anonymous. Anonymous transactions, in the absence of institutional infrastructure to enforce quality standards, trend toward lemons… the Akerlof problem, playing out in every product category simultaneously.

The solution was branding. Not branding in the modern sense of logo design and brand guidelines, but branding in the original sense: a mark of origin that transferred trust from a local, social relationship to a national, reputational one. Quaker Oats, Heinz, Coca-Cola, Campbell’s — these weren’t primarily marketing operations. They were trust infrastructure for anonymous markets. The logo on the tin was a credible commitment device: the company has invested enough in this name that adulterating the product would be net-negative even from a purely selfish profit-maximization standpoint.

This solved the trust problem, but it also created something new: a durable competitive advantage that had never existed before at scale. A brand, once established in a consumer’s mind, creates switching costs that are psychological rather than physical. The railroad created access to the consumer; the brand created retention. And retention compounds. Every satisfied purchase reinforces the brand; every reinforcement reduces the probability of trial with a competitor; every reduced trial probability makes the brand more valuable, which justifies more investment in the brand’s credibility, which makes it harder to dislodge.

One of the first appearances in business history of what we would now call a feedback loop creating sustainable competitive advantage. And feedback loops, unlike static cost advantages, do not dissipate with scale, they strengthen with it. The bigger Coca-Cola gets, the more mind-share it occupies, the more retailers it can demand prominent shelf placement from, the more consumers encounter it as the default choice, the less space there is for a challenger to establish its own loop.

The lesson compounds the railroad lesson: the technology that eliminates distribution friction enables a new kind of advantage, one that is cognitive rather than geographic. And cognitive advantages, properly established, are harder to erode than geographic ones. You can build a rival railroad. You cannot easily build a rival 120-year-old brand that people associate with childhood and summer and the specific taste of a thing they’ve consumed since they could hold a cup.

The Electrification Interlude: Infrastructure vs. Application

Here is a historical observation that should disturb anyone with a strong prior about how technological transitions create wealth: the companies that built the electrical grid did not become the most valuable companies of the electrical era.

Electrification was one of the most significant technology transitions in history, comparable in its impact on productivity and industrial organization to the railroad. And yet the wealth accrued not primarily to the utility companies and electrical infrastructure builders, but to the companies that used electricity to do things that were impossible without it. Refrigeration. Electric motors replacing belts and driveshafts in factories. Radio, then television. The assembly line, which required reliable, flexible power that steam couldn’t provide with sufficient precision.

The infrastructure builders built the capability. The capability enabled applications. The applications captured most of the value.

This pattern — infrastructure-application value split — is a constant across technology transitions, and it creates a systematic investment error that recurs in each generation. Investors, observing the transformative potential of a new infrastructure technology, pour capital into the infrastructure layer, which eventually earns commodity returns because infrastructure, once built, competes on price. Meanwhile, the application layer… the companies that figure out what to do with the new capability, earns structural returns because their advantages accrue from usage, which accumulates into the kinds of brand, network, and switching-cost advantages that compound rather than dissipate.

The internet bubble is the electrification story run in fast-forward, with worse accounting. Capital misallocated to fiber optic cable and server farms created infrastructure that ultimately priced at commodity. The companies that figured out what to do with that infrastructure — Google, Amazon, Facebook, eventually, accumulated the brand, network, and data advantages that compound into something resembling permanent competitive advantage.

You can understand the 2000 crash and the 2010s technology boom as a single continuous event: first, capital misallocated to infrastructure; second, the infrastructure became cheap; third, application-layer companies used cheap infrastructure to build advantages that could not have existed when infrastructure was expensive. AWS exists because the fiber-optic glut of 2001 made bandwidth essentially free. The sequence matters enormously. Miss it and you will invest in the wrong layer of every subsequent transition.

Containerization: The Collapse of Distance for Atoms

The most important technology in the history of global trade is one almost nobody talks about: the standardized shipping container.

Malcolm McLean’s 1956 invention of the intermodal container is, by several estimates, the single most significant factor in the post-WWII explosion of global trade. Before containers, loading and unloading a cargo ship was a labor-intensive, weeks-long process dominated by port handling costs, not ocean passage costs. Ships spent half their operational lives sitting in port.

The container reduced the cost of port handling by approximately 90%. This is not a rounding error. It is the difference between global trade as a marginal economic activity and global trade as the organizing principle of industrial production.

Once the cost of shipping a component across an ocean becomes trivially small relative to the cost of producing it, production geography decouples from consumption geography. A factory in Ohio no longer competes only with other Ohio factories; it competes with factories wherever on earth labor, land, capital, and regulatory costs combine most favorably. “Globalization” is used as if it were an ideology rather than what it actually is: the commercial consequence of containerization making distance approximately free for manufactured goods.

Apple is, structurally, a containerization company. It designs in California, manufactures in China via a multi-tier supply chain, and sells globally wherever consumers can afford the product. The gross margin Apple captures, is in part a function of its brand and switching costs and ecosystem lock-in, but it exists because the container made it possible to source components and manufacturing globally without transport costs consuming the margin differential. Strip out containerization and the Apple business model is not merely less profitable. It does not exist.

The geographic fragmentation that characterized pre-rail commerce has not just shrunk, it has, for many product categories, effectively disappeared. The market is now the world, which means the potential scale of winner concentration is orders of magnitude larger than anything that was structurally possible before.

The Internet: When Connection Changed the Nature of the Object

Everything up to the internet is preparation. The railroad collapsed geographic friction for physical goods. Branding collapsed trust friction for consumer products. Containerization collapsed distance costs for manufacturing. But in all of these cases, something still cost money. Physical distribution was cheaper, not free. Brand-building required massive advertising expenditure. Manufacturing globalization required capital-intensive supply chains.

The internet collapsed marginal reproduction costs to approximately zero for anything that can be expressed as bits. This is qualitatively different from anything that preceded it.

But here is where most histories of the internet go wrong, and where Ramo’s insight from The Seventh Sense is indispensable. The standard account focuses on what the internet eliminated — distribution costs, information asymmetry, geographic friction. What it misses is what the internet created, which is categorically different from any prior friction-elimination event: connection changed the nature of the objects being connected.

Ramo’s formulation is precise and load-bearing. When you connect a patient, a doctor, a currency, a flying machine, each is twisted and changed as a result. Some become great. Others snap. The pulling network action accounts for our greatest new fortunes but also the tumbling of old ideas and institutions. This is why our age is so uneasy: as the network grows, it locks everything it touches into a new structure.

The pre-internet bookseller was a bookseller. Amazon is not a better bookseller, it is an entity that had no conceptual predecessor, whose competitive advantages (data, logistics, ecosystem lock-in, cloud infrastructure) would be incomprehensible to a Barnes & Noble executive reasoning from first principles in 1994. The object changed. The industry changed. The nature of advantage changed.

What replaced geographic friction was something more durable and more interesting: network effects.

In a network-effects business, each additional user makes the product more valuable for all existing users. This is not a static advantage; it is an advantage that compounds with scale. The larger the network, the more valuable it is, which attracts more users, which makes the network larger, which makes it more valuable, which makes it harder to attack from outside because any challenger starts from a base of zero network value.

The valuations that look absurd from a traditional multiple-of-earnings perspective are exactly right from an options-pricing perspective. What you’re paying for is the option value of being the entity that captures the network-effects flywheel in the largest available market. If you get there, the returns are extraordinary. If you don’t… if you’re MySpace when Facebook comes along, or Friendster when MySpace came along… the value is approximately zero. Winner-take-most means most of the prize goes to one winner, which means the value of being in second place is not “half of first place.” It’s closer to a decimal fraction of first place.

Google captures roughly 90% of global search advertising revenue. Facebook and Instagram together capture roughly 60-70% of social advertising spend. Amazon captures approximately 38% of US e-commerce. Apple captures approximately 90% of global smartphone profits while holding perhaps 20% of unit market share. These numbers are not aberrations. They are the logical endpoint of applying network-effects logic to markets that were previously fragmented by geography, information costs, or physical distribution constraints.

The brewer in 1800 who served a single town was not less capable than Jeff Bezos. He operated in a different geometry. The geometry of 2025 selects, systematically and brutally, for the competitor who can accumulate the largest network fastest, because that competitor earns returns that are structurally inaccessible to anyone else in the market.

The New Castes of Gateland

Here is where the simple version of the story… technology eliminates friction, winners capture more value, repeat… becomes insufficient. Because what the standard account of tech concentration misses, is that the internet didn’t just create a new breed of winner. It created a new species of power.

The industrial-era winner competed within a market. The network-era winner competes for the right to define the market… to become the gate through which others must pass.

Ramo calls this Gateland, and it is worth dwelling on what it actually means. Rockefeller owned the refinery. Google owns the attention mechanism through which the internet becomes legible. Amazon owns the commerce infrastructure through which online retail functions. Apple owns the device layer through which apps reach users. These are not competitive positions within an industry. They are structural positions that sit above industries, positions from which the holder can extract rent from every participant in every downstream market simultaneously.

“Connectivity is power,” Ramo writes. “It is the power to extend advantage even further. Gatekeeping is, at the end of the day, our most powerful point of control.”

The iron law of Gateland: once you control a gate, you can use that position to create still more gates. Of the twelve most popular mobile apps in 2016, nine were linked to Google, Apple, or Microsoft. This is not coincidence. Gates compound. A platform that captures enough users can dictate terms to the app developers who need those users, who can then dictate terms to the businesses that need those apps, who can then dictate terms to the suppliers who need those businesses. The structural leverage is recursive, nesting into itself across every downstream transaction.

Compare this to Rockefeller. Standard Oil controlled 91% of US refining. Dominant, yes. But the structural position was within the oil industry, upstream suppliers and downstream retailers could, in principle, organize alternatives (and eventually did, with antitrust intervention). The network-era gate doesn’t just dominate an industry; it extracts rent from the mechanism of commercial coordination itself.

Ancient hydraulic empires understood a version of this. To invoke the historian Karl Wittfogel’s insight: whoever controls the water infrastructure in an agrarian society controls everything downstream… literally and economically. China’s Yu the Great rose to power around 2800 BCE not because he won military battles, but because he mastered the Yangtze. Control the commons of coordination, and the rest of power follows.

The internet built the most consequential commons of coordination in human history. And then a handful of companies captured the gates through which that commons is accessed. The water lords returned, wearing hoodies.

The Sandpile and the Avalanche

There is a structural feature of each technology transition that the sequential narrative above obscures: transitions don’t happen gradually. They happen suddenly, after long periods of invisible accumulation.

To draw on the complexity science of John Holland and the sandpile metaphor from Per Bak: pour sand grain by grain onto a pile, and for a long time nothing changes in any visible way. Then, triggered by a single grain indistinguishable from all preceding grains, the pile avalanches. Not because that particular grain was special. Because the system had accumulated to a critical state.

Technological transitions work this way. The railroad had been building for decades before the national market equilibrium suddenly broke. The internet had been accumulating infrastructure for a decade before the network-effects flywheels suddenly became self-sustaining. Each technology crosses an invisible threshold… a critical mass of users, a critical density of connections, a critical reduction in friction, and then the system reorganizes rapidly into a new equilibrium with a very different distribution of power.

The practical consequence: transitions are systematically underestimated before the threshold and overestimated at the threshold. In the lead-up, it is easy to mistake slow accumulation for stagnation. After the threshold, it is easy to mistake the avalanche for a permanent state of continuous upheaval rather than the rapid reorganization toward a new, ultimately stable equilibrium dominated by the new winners.

The investors and operators who win the transition are those who recognize the critical state accumulating before the avalanche. Not after. After is too late: the gates are already installed, the flywheels already spinning, the network effects already compounding.

The critical question, for any technology, is not “is this real?” but “how close is this to the threshold, and who is positioned at the gate when the avalanche comes?”

AI and the Collapse of Expertise Distance

Every friction-eliminating technology points toward the next one by revealing what it didn’t eliminate.

The internet collapsed distribution costs for bits, but it didn’t collapse production costs for good bits. Writing a useful legal contract, diagnosing a medical condition, writing functional code, conducting financial analysis… these require expertise, and expertise is scarce, unevenly distributed, and expensive to deploy. Even in a world where distribution is free, the limitation is production.

AI appears to be collapsing the marginal cost of deploying expertise the same way the internet collapsed the marginal cost of distributing information.

If that framing is right, and the directional claim seems increasingly robust… we are at the beginning of a friction-elimination event that is structurally larger than any preceding one. The market for legal advice, medical advice, software development, creative work, financial analysis, and educational instruction is, collectively, an enormous fraction of global GDP. These markets have been protected not by geography or information asymmetry or distribution costs, but by the fundamental scarcity of human expertise itself.

Expertise scarcity is a stronger friction than geography. You can build roads and railroads. You cannot, in a single generation, train enough surgeons, lawyers, and software engineers to meet global demand at the price that unscalable supply enforces.

If AI eliminates that friction, it doesn’t just make expertise cheaper. It changes the nature of expertise as an object. Connected expertise — AI-augmented, continuously learning, accessible to anyone with an API key, is not the same thing as the human expertise it replaces. It is something new: expertise without the credentialing bottleneck, the geographic constraint, the individual learning curve. An object transformed by connection.

The structural implications follow the template, but with an important twist from the infrastructure-application lesson. The companies building AI models are building infrastructure. Infrastructure, once built, competes on price. The history of every prior technology transition suggests that the application-layer companies… those who figure out what to do with cheap expertise, will capture most of the value. But the Gateland framework complicates this. Some AI applications are not merely applications; they are new gates. A company that becomes the AI layer through which a professional domain is accessed… legal research, medical diagnosis, financial analysis, is not competing within that domain. It is becoming the gate above it.

The sandpile is accumulating. The threshold is not yet visible. But every grain of AI capability that drops onto the pile is indistinguishable, in the moment, from the grain that will trigger the avalanche.

The Geometry of the Next Hundred Years

There is one more dimension to this pattern that the sequential narrative obscures: the geographic distribution of where concentration happens.

Every friction-eliminating technology has produced winners concentrated in the places where capital, infrastructure, and regulatory environment were most conducive to exploiting the new geometry. The railroad era’s winners were American because the American market was the largest contiguous market available for national-scale consolidation. The internet era’s winners were American and Chinese… with the Great Firewall creating a separate competitive ecosystem, because the ecosystem advantages in capital allocation and talent density made it structurally most likely to produce the companies that could exploit network effects at global scale.

The AI transition creates a different geographic question. In a world where expertise deployment becomes cheap, the countries and markets that have historically been limited by the scarcity of local expertise face the friction-elimination event in a more radical way than mature markets.

India is the clearest case. The market friction that has limited value creation in legal services, healthcare, financial advisory, and software in India is not primarily distribution or branding or transport… it is expertise scarcity combined with regulatory complexity and geographic concentration. If AI collapses expertise scarcity, and if Indian market conditions mean the pre-AI equilibrium was even more distorted by that scarcity than in mature markets, then the delta between pre- and post-AI value creation in India is correspondingly larger.

This logic compounds with the broader digital infrastructure build-out that has been underway since 2016. UPI created a payments commons. Aadhaar created an identity commons. ONDC is attempting to create a commerce commons. These are not marginal infrastructure improvements. They are Baran’s fishnet — distributed, survivable, uncuttable… being laid down across the world’s most complex economy. Each gate that gets built in that infrastructure layer becomes a candidate for the Gateland dynamic: connectivity extending advantage further, gates compounding into more gates, the hydraulic bureaucracy of the digital age assembling itself in real time.

The companies sitting at the middleware layer of India’s digital transformation — the unsexy orchestration layer between public infrastructure (UPI, Aadhaar, ONDC) and application (consumer fintech, insurance, healthcare, commerce), are not just toll collectors on existing traffic. They are building the road-building equipment in a country that has decided to pave everything, at a moment when the nature of the road itself is changing.

The Underlying Structure

Strip away the specific technologies and the pattern becomes visible in its general form.

Every friction-eliminating technology does the same thing: it expands the effective market… the set of buyers and sellers who can realistically transact, and in doing so, it changes the nature of competitive advantage. Pre-friction-elimination, advantage comes from local presence, local knowledge, local relationships. Post-friction-elimination, advantage comes from scale, network position, or brand, characteristics that are not locally bounded and therefore not locally replicable.

The transition between these regimes is always painful for incumbents, for exactly the reason that makes it profitable for the right newcomer: the incumbent’s advantages were real, but they were real in a geometry that no longer exists. The competitive moat that protected the regional beer distributor was not illusory, it was a genuine advantage against competitors operating in the same physical geography. The problem is that “competitors operating in the same physical geography” ceased to be the relevant competitive set the moment distribution costs collapsed.

This is why technological disruption is so systematically underestimated. The incumbents aren’t wrong that they have advantages; they’re wrong about which competition those advantages protect against. Every technology that eliminates a friction simultaneously makes previously irrelevant competitors suddenly relevant.

But there is a deeper structure here that the technology-transition framing slightly obscures. It is not just that each technology creates a new winner. Each technology creates a new kind of winner, a winner whose advantage operates through a different mechanism than any winner before them.

Railroad winners win through distribution scale and fixed-cost leverage. Brand winners win through psychological switching costs and trust accumulation. Containerization winners win through supply chain optimization and labor arbitrage. Internet winners win through network effects and data accumulation. AI winners, if the pattern holds, will win through something we are still learning to name: the compounding advantage of a system that teaches itself, that improves with every interaction, that creates a data moat from usage itself.

Each mechanism is harder to dislodge than the previous one. You can build a rival railroad, it’s expensive, but physically possible. You cannot build a rival Google by building better search; you have to somehow convince 90% of the world’s searchers to switch simultaneously before Google can use its data advantage to match whatever improvement you’ve made. The moat is not the algorithm. The moat is the self-reinforcing loop between search volume, click data, algorithmic improvement, and advertiser concentration that makes any challenger start the race already losing.

If AI creates a winner whose moat is self-improving intelligence — a system that becomes smarter and more capable with every unit of usage, then the resulting competitive advantage may be structurally stickier than anything that preceded it. The railroad gave its winner physical infrastructure. The brand gave its winner psychological infrastructure. AI may give its winner cognitive infrastructure: an ever-improving model of the world, trained on usage data, that no challenger can replicate regardless of capital, talent, or time.

The gate would not merely restrict access. The gate would become indistinguishable from the road.

Coda: What the Brewer Knew

The brewer in 1800 who served a single town was not naive. He was rational. He had correctly identified his competitive environment, correctly assessed his advantages within it, and correctly concluded that the investment required to serve a second town exceeded the returns it would generate. His local monopoly was not laziness; it was the right strategy for the geometry he inhabited.

The tragedy, if it is a tragedy, is that he could not see the geometry changing. Nobody could, not in real time. The railroad looked like a transportation efficiency improvement, not a restructuring of competitive possibility space. The internet looked like a communication efficiency improvement, not a mechanism for producing winner-take-most network effects in every industry it touched. AI looks like an automation tool, not, or not yet, a fundamental restructuring of which frictions determine competitive advantage.

In each case, the people who saw the geometry changing before it was obvious did not need to be smarter than the incumbents. They needed to be asking a different question. Not “how do I compete in the current market?” but “what does competition look like after this technology reaches its natural limit of adoption?” Not “who is winning now?” but “what kind of winner can exist in the new geometry that couldn’t exist before?”

Ramo frames this as the Seventh Sense: the instinct to feel how connection changes the nature of the objects being connected, to see the gate before it is installed, to feel the sandpile accumulating before the avalanche. It is not a technical skill. It is a sensibility, a way of reading the world that sees structure where others see surface, that feels the tectonic before the earthquake, that understands why the animals ran uphill before the water came.

The history of business is the history of increasing concentration of value, accelerated in spurts by technological change. The spurts look different every time. The underlying pattern does not. But with each successive wave, the winner accumulates not just more value, but a qualitatively different kind of power, power that is harder to dislodge, harder to regulate, harder to conceptualize using the frameworks built for the prior era.

The brewer served his town. Some future version of him, working in a domain we haven’t fully identified yet, will serve the world. The distance between those two facts is the whole of the story.

The most interesting question this raises isn’t which companies win the AI transition. It’s whether the winner-take-most geometry itself… which has been strengthening continuously since 1800, from physical monopoly to cognitive monopoly to network monopoly, eventually produces a kind of structural power that is qualitatively different from anything the prior template predicts. Not just the largest winner in history. But the first winner whose gate is, in some meaningful sense, everywhere.

That would be a genuinely new regime. We don’t have a good historical template for what comes next.
