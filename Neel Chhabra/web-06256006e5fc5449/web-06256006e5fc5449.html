The best businesses create value in ways that aren’t immediately obvious to most investors. I’ve always believed that understanding how companies create, and capture value is more important than any short-term earnings forecast. Take the Value Stick framework. It’s elegantly simple but profoundly useful. At the top, you’ve got what the customer would be willing to pay… their perceived value. Below that sits the actual price they pay. That gap? That’s consumer surplus, and smart companies understand they need to leave some on the table. You can’t extract every dollar, or you destroy the relationship. Below price, you’ve got your costs. And beneath that, what suppliers and employees would accept as their minimum. The gap between what you pay them and their willingness-to-sell? That’s your margin for value creation on the supply side. The magic happens when you expand both gaps simultaneously. Amazon did this brilliantly… lowering customer prices while creating such attractive opportunities for suppliers and employees that they captured talent and partnerships others couldn’t. The total value created kept expanding. The framework clarifies what matters: expanding value gaps and defending them. Everything else is motion without progress.​​​​​​​​​​​​​​​​

This connects directly to Hamilton Helmer’s Seven Powers framework, which I find tremendously useful. Seven Powers is Hamilton Helmer’s catalog of moats: scale economies, network effects, counter-positioning, switching costs, branding, cornered resources, process power. These aren’t just competitive advantages… they’re compounding advantages. The kind that make #2 structurally incapable of becoming #1. Winner-doesn’t-just-take-all; winner-makes-all-others-irrelevant. Scale economies, network effects, brand… these aren’t just competitive advantages. They’re mechanisms for widening those gaps in the Value Stick. A strong brand means customers will pay more for the same underlying cost structure. Network effects mean the willingness-to-pay increases with every additional user, while your incremental costs approach zero.

The bundling concept is equally powerful and chronically underappreciated by markets. The switching costs and interoperability create enormous value that shows up in pricing power. Bundling Theory is about mathematical impossibility becoming market reality. When you combine products A and B, the willingness-to-pay shouldn’t exceed the sum. Except it does. Microsoft Office. Amazon Prime. The iPhone. Users value the integration itself… the eliminated friction, the emergent convenience, the status of having everything in one place. One plus one equals three. Arbitrage against human psychology. Most investors focus on the wrong things… quarterly earnings beats, marginal guidance changes. But the real money is made understanding these structural frameworks. Does this business have expanding gaps in its Value Stick? Are its competitive moats widening or narrowing? Is the bundle getting stronger? These mental models function like an operating system for investment analysis. Once you internalize them, you start seeing business quality differently. You stop worrying about whether a stock is up or down 10% and start asking: Is this company increasing the total value in its ecosystem while capturing more of it over time? That’s the question that matters.

Excellence often lies not in having the answer, but in asking better questions… in taking the second-order, third-order derivative of a problem while everyone else is still doing arithmetic.

The best investors, operators, builders… they’re contrarians in process, not just conclusions. They inhabit multiple mental models simultaneously: physicist thinking about first principles, historian pattern-matching across decades, game theorist running scenarios, psychologist probing incentives and biases.

They’re obsessed with what’s hidden… the non-obvious dependencies, the second-order effects, the asymmetries others miss. They ask: What would have to be true? What’s the base rate? Where’s the optionality? What breaks this?

Deep thinking compounds. Surface-level analysis commoditizes. The edge isn’t working harder… it’s systematically deconstructing complexity into its fundamental components, then reassembling it with intellectual honesty about what you know, what you don’t, and what actually matters.

Most people think in straight lines. Exceptional ones think in networks, feedback loops, and phase transitions. They’re comfortable holding tension between competing frameworks, updating on new information, and being precisely wrong rather than vaguely right. That’s systematic depth.
