Everyone is right about India. Few will get paid.

Inevitable is not investable: a field guide to who will run short of what, and for how long.

Neel Chhabra
Oct 25

The essay in ten lines

Inevitable is a line; investable is a curve. If you only have a few minutes, here is what investors should know about betting on India's inevitable future.

  1. Certainty is not a business model. Flight, railways, fibre and solar all changed the world, and much of the money that built them was lost. A trend can be right and still ruinous for the capital that funds it.

  2. Follow the queue. Value pools at the narrowest point of a system, the layer the trend cannot make faster. Own that, and you are paid for every unit that passes.

  3. Scarcity migrates. The moment a bottleneck pays, capital rushes in, and the profit moves on to the next scarce layer.

  4. Demand sizes the prize; supply decides who keeps it. The useful question is not how big a market gets, but who else can serve it, how fast, and with how much money.

  5. Prize what money can’t rebuild. Qualification, trust, licences, land and habit outlast the moats a well-funded rival can simply copy. India’s paint leaders are learning what a rebuildable moat costs.

  6. A multiple is a belief, compressed. It prices growth, returns, duration and certainty, and stocks move when that belief changes, not when it is confirmed.

  7. Watch the slope, not the level. Shares peak when growth peaks. Trent’s profits kept rising while its stock fell by almost half; TD Power re-rated from under ten times earnings when nobody wanted it.

  8. Mind the atom. No theme pays unless its smallest unit (one order, one SIP, one qualified part) makes money.

  9. The toll sits one layer back. Across India’s six great themes, the winners stand behind the visible play: the exchange behind the broker, the cell behind the module, the qualified part behind the phone.

  10. Every queue has a clock. The work is not predicting whether the future arrives. It is knowing who it will have to wait for, and for how long.

You have a right to your actions, but never to their fruits.

Bhagavad Gita, 2.47, paraphrased

Few lines have been misread as often. Most people hear resignation. Read again, it is a precise statement about cause and effect: the action is yours, but the outcome belongs to a larger system that owes you nothing for your effort.

Markets run on the same law. You can be right about the direction of history and still have no claim on its fruit. The fruit goes to whoever owns the part of the system the trend cannot get around.

Warren Buffett put the commercial version more sharply, looking back on a century of an industry that shrank the planet:

If a farsighted capitalist had been present at Kitty Hawk, he would have done his successors a huge favor by shooting Orville down. — Warren Buffett, Berkshire Hathaway letter, 2007

Flight was inevitable. It was also, for most of its history, one of the worst places on earth to keep capital. Railways in the 1840s, fibre optics in the 1990s and solar panels in the 2010s tell the same story. Each trend was real. Each changed the world. Each destroyed a large share of the money that built it.

In 1850 the French economist Frédéric Bastiat wrote an essay called That Which Is Seen, and That Which Is Not Seen. The trend is what is seen. What is not seen is the plumbing underneath: who has to queue, who sets the price, who can be copied by next spring. This essay is about the unseen part, and where it sits in India today.

The law of the narrowest point

“An hour lost at a bottleneck is an hour lost for the total system. An hour saved at a non-bottleneck is a mirage.

— Eliyahu Goldratt, The Goal, 1984

Goldratt was writing about a factory floor: a system moves only as fast as its slowest step. Speed up any other step and you just build a bigger pile of work in front of the constraint.

Now stretch the factory to the size of a country. A trend is a flow of electrons, rupees, patients or parts. Most of the system has spare room. One point does not, and everything has to queue there.

In a factory, the bottleneck is a problem to fix. In a market, it is a toll booth. Whoever owns the narrowest point is paid for every unit that passes, and paid more the longer the queue.

Network theory gives the same answer in its own language. The most a network of pipes can carry is set by its minimum cut, the narrowest set of pipes, and value flows the same way. The most profitable position is rarely the one generating the most value; it is the one controlling the minimum cut. Grapplers learn it on the mat: find the wrist that controls the arm, and a two-inch movement controls a man twice your size. The smallest lock often secures the largest door.

So the most useful question about any trend is not how big it will be. It is this: what can’t it make faster? Software can be copied overnight. A solar module plant goes up in about a year. A supplier of aero-engine parts needs years of certification before its first order. A specialist doctor takes more than a decade to train. The longer the lead time, the deeper the queue, and the more of the trend’s value collects in it.

Not every narrow point is made of steel. Some of the slowest things to build sit in people’s heads: an installer’s confidence that a film won’t yellow, a parent’s trust in a school, a family’s belief that a care home will treat their mother well. Rory Sutherland calls the habit of attacking such problems with engineering alone “physics envy”. His favourite example is the slow lift: you can spend a fortune on a faster motor, or put a mirror in the lobby so people stop noticing the wait. The problem was never the lift. It was the wait.

→ lead time is the moat. Trust has the longest lead time of all.

But a narrow point does not stay narrow forever. The moment it starts paying, the profit begins to move.

Profit is never destroyed, only moved

Clayton Christensen spent his career on why good companies lose. One of his least quoted ideas may be his most useful: the law of conservation of attractive profits. When one stage of a value chain becomes a commodity and its profits disappear, attractive profits appear at an adjacent stage, the one that is now hard to do well.

Profit behaves like energy: it isn’t destroyed, only moved to wherever the system is still constrained. Adrian Slywotzky called the same flow value migration: value leaves business designs built for yesterday's customer and settles in the ones built for today's.

Artificial intelligence is the live case. The cost of a unit of machine intelligence keeps falling by large multiples. In 1865 William Stanley Jevons noticed that more efficient steam engines did not reduce Britain’s coal use; they increased it, because cheaper power found new uses. Cheap intelligence will be used everywhere too, which makes intelligence itself a poor place to stand.

The profit moves outward: to chips, then power, then the grid, then the electricians and engineers who can install it. Each step is less glamorous and, for a while, more scarce.

Electricity ran the same play a century ago. The people who wired America were right about the technology, and many of the utility empires built on it, Samuel Insull’s among them, collapsed in the 1930s; some of the steadiest money went to those selling what ran on the current. Every friction removed reveals the one it didn’t remove. Robots will repeat the pattern. As AI learns to move, the shortage relocates to the precision parts that let a machine move at all, the roller screw and the harmonic reducer. David Ricardo’s theory of rent, in a sentence: what can be reproduced earns a return on capital; what can’t be reproduced collects the surplus.

Global parallel: Netherlands

Every leading-edge chip in the world is printed on a machine that only ASML makes. Its extreme-ultraviolet lithography tools took decades and a global supply chain to build, and no rival has one. In the AI build-out, profit has kept settling at narrow points like this, at the leading-edge fab, and now at power and grid connections, rather than in the applications everyone talks about.

Which raises an awkward question. If everyone can see where the profit is moving, why doesn’t everyone get paid?

Your margin is my opportunity

Jeff Bezos meant the line as a threat to incumbents. It is also the most compact description of the capital cycle ever written. High returns are a flare in the night sky: they show every competitor where to build.

Inside one boardroom the logic is flawless. Demand is certain, the forecast is probably right, and the bank is happy to lend. Now multiply that boardroom by every company in the sector, each approving a plan with the same demand forecast in the appendix. Each plan is rational. Together they are a glut. And capacity that everyone must add just to stay in the game is a tax, not a moat.

Solar is the cleanest example. Through the 2010s, module prices fell by roughly 90%. Customers got the cheapest electricity ever produced. Manufacturers got a decade of losses and a run of bankruptcies, among them Suntech in 2013, which only two years earlier had been the world’s largest panel maker. Global Crossing laid roughly 100,000 miles of fibre for an internet that grew exactly as promised, and filed for bankruptcy in January 2002.

American shale is the closest recent rhyme. Fracking turned the United States into the world's largest oil producer in the 2010s, exactly as promised, while much of the industry spent more cash than it generated for years. China's electric cars are the rhyme now playing: a world-changing industry, more than a hundred brands at its peak, and a price war that is consolidating it into a few survivors.

Charlie Munger liked to say: show me the incentive and I will show you the outcome. The incentive in a certain trend is to build, so the outcome is too much capacity. Certainty is not an asset. It is a public good, and public goods earn nothing.

So the working question is not “is it scarce?” but “for how long?” A two-year queue with a four-year capacity build is a good trade. A two-year queue with a nine-month build is a trap with a nice chart.

Telling the two apart means looking away from demand, which is exactly where most people never look.

Supply decides who keeps the prize

“We might as reasonably dispute whether it is the upper or the under blade of a pair of scissors that cuts a piece of paper, as whether value is governed by utility or cost of production.”

— Alfred Marshall, Principles of Economics, 1890

Marshall settled an old argument with a pair of scissors: price is cut by demand and supply together. Investors still mostly watch one blade. They size the market, draw the penetration curve and chart spending per person. Demand matters, but everyone can see it, so it is usually already in the price.

The blade they miss is supply. Marathon Asset Management built an investment discipline on it, set out in Capital Returns (edited by Edward Chancellor): returns depend less on how fast demand grows than on how much capital rushes in to meet it, and on whether that capital can actually compete. What owners keep depends on who else can serve a market, how quickly, and with how much money.

Demand tells you the size of the prize. Supply decides who gets to keep it. This is why a big market and a big profit pool are different things: a flood of capital can drown the pool while revenue keeps growing.

Strategists have a tidy way to draw this. Adam Brandenburger and Harborne Stuart measure the value a business creates as the gap between what a customer would pay and the least its suppliers would accept. Price and cost split that gap three ways: the customer keeps what sits above the price, suppliers what sits below the cost, and the firm the slice between. A trend lifts what customers will pay, for everyone. It does not decide where the price line falls. Supply does. Peter Thiel’s version is blunter: creating value is not enough; you also need to capture some of the value you create. The best businesses lengthen the stick at both ends, raising what customers will pay and lowering what suppliers need to accept. Amazon did the first with selection and faster delivery, and the stick it grew was long enough for its own slice to grow too.

One molecule, two businesses

PVC makes the point at Indian scale. The same white powder, polyvinyl chloride, is a commodity at one end of its chain and a brand at the other.

The resin end is open to the world. India uses well over 4 million tonnes a year and makes only around 1.6 million, with Reliance alone making about half of that. In the first ten months of 2025 China exported more than 3 million tonnes of PVC, roughly 42% of it to India, and India’s imports for the year passed 3.1 million tonnes, nearly double 2022’s. Landed prices fell from about $757 a tonne in January 2025 to roughly $600 by the year’s end. Protection kept failing to arrive: a recommended anti-dumping duty lapsed without being imposed, and a quality-control order was withdrawn in November 2025. Only in July 2026 did a six-month minimum import price of $766 a tonne come in.

The pipe end is a different business. The person who pays for a pipe rarely chooses it; the plumber does, and the plumber picks what he trusts behind a tiled wall. Astral built its position on exactly that, with a loyalty programme for plumbers, contractors and dealers.

The accounts follow the chain. Chemplast Sanmar, a resin maker, went from ₹649 crore of profit in FY22 to a loss of ₹280 crore in FY26, and its return on capital from about 50% to roughly nothing. Over the same years Astral’s profit moved from ₹490 crore to ₹535 crore and Supreme’s from ₹968 crore to ₹954 crore. For them, the flood of cheap resin was simply a cheaper input.

The story has an honest footnote. Prince Pipes, a smaller branded maker, saw profit fall from ₹249 crore to ₹75 crore. A brand protects only when the plumber actually prefers it. A brand that is merely a name on the pipe is just a more expensive commodity.

The same story plays out in polyester film. Packaging film is the big market and a crowded one: capital is the only real barrier, and a funded rival can put up a new line in about eighteen months. Specialty film for car paint and windows is a fraction of the size, but installers who carry the warranty buy only films with years of road behind them. Garware Hi-Tech Films, the specialty maker, doubled its profit from ₹167 crore in FY22 to ₹338 crore in FY26. Over the same years the packaging makers gave back their peak: Polyplex fell from ₹965 crore to ₹41 crore, and Jindal Poly Films went from a ₹1,196 crore profit to a loss of about ₹1,060 crore in FY26. The smaller market kept the bigger profit.

Two questions about supply

The harder skill is seeing this before the accounts do. We can boil the supply down to two questions. The first: can capital copy this? If a rival with deep pockets could rebuild the position, supply will eventually spread across many hands and returns will become ordinary; if it can’t, a few will keep the profit. The second: which way is capital flowing right now? Even a business that can’t be copied has spells when money floods in and squeezes everyone, and even one that can be copied enjoys a season when capital is fleeing.

Think of it as the end state and the current state of supply, and its rule is to act only when both are favourable. A durable business does not become a poor business overnight, its partners write, but it can become a poor investment when the supply cycle turns against it.

The first answer sets where returns settle over a whole cycle; the second, where they sit today. Keep them apart: a return depressed by a wave of capital will mend, and one depressed because the business can be copied will not.

That leaves the deepest question of all. What exactly is it that capital cannot copy?

What capital cannot buy

Power: the set of conditions creating the potential for persistent differential returns.

— Hamilton Helmer, 7 Powers

Hamilton Helmer spent decades asking why a few companies earn high returns for years while most cannot. He came back with only seven answers, each a benefit paired with a barrier that stops rivals copying it, however much money they bring. The test is blunt: would a rival with unlimited money and five years still fail to catch up?

Helmer’s list leaves out something that matters a great deal in India: regulation. Only two licensed depositories, NSDL and CDSL, may hold India’s shares in electronic form. CDSL holds about four-fifths of demat accounts and earns an operating margin above 50%. That position is real, but it is granted rather than built, and what a rule gives, a rule can take back. Give built advantages more weight than granted ones.

Built advantages share a property worth naming: qualification is a stock, not a flow. It accumulates through audits, years and shipped parts, and money cannot buy it in a hurry. A rival can build a bigger stadium; it cannot build a century of Saturday afternoons.

High margins and returns on capital are the moat's effects, not its causes: they show where an advantage was, not how long it will last. What matters is the competitive advantage period, the years before competition drags returns back towards the cost of capital. Markets usually assume that drag arrives on schedule, so the businesses that defy mean reversion are the ones they most often underprice. The market is poor at pricing how long barriers to entry last.

A window, not a wall

The oldest version of this story is a gold rush. In 1848, before most of San Francisco knew there was gold in the American River, a storekeeper named Sam Brannan quietly bought up the town’s shovels, pans and pickaxes. Then, the story goes, he walked down the street waving a bottle of gold dust. The rush ran through his shop, and by some accounts he became California’s first millionaire without mining an ounce. His toll lasted exactly as long as he was the only one holding shovels. Soon every ship into the bay carried tools. He died in 1889, penniless.

A dry-goods wholesaler who arrived in 1853 did better. In 1873 Levi Strauss and a Reno tailor, Jacob Davis, were granted a patent for work trousers reinforced with copper rivets. For seventeen years the patent made riveted trousers a shovel nobody else could make. “Sell picks and shovels” is half a rule. The whole rule: sell the shovel nobody else can make, for as long as nobody else can make it.

Indian solar has lived through both halves, and it shows a granted barrier in motion. From April 2024, India’s ALMM rules required most supported projects to use modules from an approved list of makers. From June 2026 the requirement reaches solar cells, and from June 2028 wafers. Inside that wall, profits soared. Waaree Energies’ net profit rose from ₹1,274 crore in FY24 to ₹3,884 crore in FY26, its operating margin from 14% to 22%; Premier Energies’ profit rose from ₹231 crore to ₹1,510 crore as its margin doubled to about 30%. By September 2026, modules built on Indian cells were selling for roughly two-thirds more than those using imported cells.

Then the capital cycle arrived, on schedule. By the late 2025 that India could already make 60–65 GW of modules a year against 45–50 GW of installations, with nameplate capacity heading past 165 GW by March 2027. Modules are now crowded; cells, the next layer up, are still short. Scarcity moved upstream. What each maker builds before the window closes, in cells, wafers and a service record, will decide who is still earning when it does.

There is a second window, in security cameras. From April 2025 every IP camera sold in India needed STQC cybersecurity certification, including a review of its source code, and enforcement tightened in April 2026. Chinese brands’ share collapsed. Aditya Infotech, which sells as CP PLUS, had started preparing about eighteen months early; by the letter’s account its share more than doubled, from about 21% to about 46%, and its return on capital reached about 30%. The real point: the company used the window to sign distributors, train installers and build service centres, the assets that will still stand when rivals certify.

→ a rule can open the door. Only what you build inside keeps it open.

The queue in the mind

“A flower is simply a weed with an advertising budget.”

— Rory Sutherland

Brand deserves a closer look, for a reason logic tends to miss. Joel Raphaelson, one of David Ogilvy’s copywriters, put it in a line Sutherland likes to repeat: people do not choose Brand A over Brand B because they think Brand A is better, but because they are more certain that it is good. The premium buys a narrower range of outcomes, not a higher average.

That explains the installer who keeps reappearing: the one fitting pipes to bathrooms, wires to walls, paint to ceilings. He is not maximising. In Herbert Simon’s word, he is satisficing: when a bad job comes back to him with a warranty claim and a lost customer, the rational choice is the product least likely to fail, not the one most likely to impress.

Sutherland’s second lesson concerns signals. A signal is believed only when it is costly to fake, which is why some of the most “wasteful” spending in business works. When Tanishq put karatmeters in its stores and invited customers to test their own gold, it spent money to make cheating impossible to hide, a signal no fly-by-night jeweller could afford to copy. A pipe or paint brand that advertises on prime-time television, to homeowners who will never pick the product themselves, is doing the same work for the tradesperson: telling him the brand will still be there when the warranty is called.

The test for this kind of moat is simple. If a rival copied the product exactly, would the installer trust it equally? If not, the gap between the two is the asset, and no accounting statement shows it until the margins do.

→ the scarce layer, seen from inside the customer’s head.

There is a catch. Every part of a moat that money can rebuild has a price, and in India someone has started paying it.

The multiple is a price list

“The whole of the advantages and disadvantages of the different employments of labour and stock must, in the same neighbourhood, be either perfectly equal or continually tending to equality.”

— Adam Smith, The Wealth of Nations, 1776

Smith’s law has a quiet consequence: a return far above the average is a signal, and capital reads signals. Tobin’s q, formalised in 1969, measures the signal: the market value of a business over the cost of rebuilding it. That denominator is harder to read than it looks. Trust networks, tacit knowledge and regulatory standing were never capitalised; they were earned slowly and never booked, so the true cost of rebuilding them can sit far above book value.

Biology found it too. In 1976 Eric Charnov showed that a foraging bee should leave a patch of flowers once its dwindling yield falls below the average it could get elsewhere. Companies advertise their nectar through return on capital, and the market value multiple is the brightest flower in the field.

So a high multiple is two things at once. Part of it pays for genuine scarcity: patents, process know-how, networks, qualifications. Part of it pays for assets that money can rebuild: factories, dealer networks, advertising-built brands. The second part is not a fortress. It is a price list, and India’s conglomerates have started reading it.

When UltraTech announced a ₹1,800 crore wire plant in late February 2025, Polycab and KEI alone shed about ₹19,000 crore of market value within hours of the next session’s open, more than ten times the plant’s budget. For an entrant, building beats buying whenever the market prices a position at many times what it costs to construct.

Three conditions for an attack

Entry needs all three of these together:

1. A high q. The market pays many times capital employed, so building the position is cheaper than buying it. 2. A moat that money can rebuild. Capacity, distribution and advertising, with little that a patent office or a utility’s approval list would recognise. 3. An intermediary the entrant already reaches. A painter, electrician, plumber or carpenter whose loyalty is priced in margin, and who already visits the entrant’s counter for cement.

Paints met all three. Birla Opus did not attack at the consumer, where the incumbents’ defences were strongest. It attacked from the side, at the dealer: free tinting machines that dealers normally pay ₹1.5–2 lakh for, dealer margins of 12–16% against about 4% from the leader, and credit stretched from five days to twenty. One Kolkata dealer’s sales of the leader’s paint fell from 70% of his business to 30% in a year. The leader’s share fell from 59% to 52% in the twelve months to March 2025, and its return on capital fell by more than ten points, nearly double the share it lost. The Competition Commission ordered an investigation in July 2025 into allegations that the leader threatened dealers who stocked Opus; those remain allegations. JSW, meanwhile, agreed to buy Akzo Nobel India in a deal worth up to about ₹13,000 crore including the open offer.

Kautilya would have recognised the move. In the Arthashastra, the madhyama, the middle king, borders two rivals and can tip the balance to whichever side wins him over. In Indian building materials, the middle king is the dealer and the tradesperson.

Who owns the fence

Between 1604 and 1914, Parliament passed more than 5,200 enclosure acts, fencing about 6.8 million acres, roughly a fifth of England. The fights were rarely about what grew in the fields; they were about who owned the fence. The paint war is the same: the prize is the dealer’s shelf, not the factory.

The historian Fernand Braudel described economic life as a house with three floors: material life, everyday production and exchange, on the ground; the market economy, where firms compete in the open, in the middle; and on top, capitalism proper, where a few actors become nodes the market must route through, as the Medici bank and the Dutch East India Company once did.

A conglomerate’s advantage runs across categories through one counter: each painter or electrician who visits for cement is a spoke that can carry another product. Jomini would call it interior lines: the attacker sits at the centre and can reinforce any front faster than incumbents defending from outside. It also has the long purse. Lester Telser showed in 1966 that a deep-pocketed rival can rationally fight at a loss for years, and a conglomerate can bury paint losses inside a cement business. Grasim has said it wants ₹10,000 crore of paint revenue within three years of full-scale operation, aiming only to break even at the EBITDA level by then.

Global parallel: Korea and the card networks

Samsung entered memory chips in 1983 and lost money for years before becoming the world’s largest memory maker, funded by a group that could carry the losses. The top floor has its global tenants too: Visa and Mastercard own the route every card payment must pass through, and the banks that issue the cards, competing in the open below them, earn far less on capital.

Testing the mechanism

A real mechanism changes its forecast when you swap a fact. This one does:

The same flower grows in other gardens. Reliance revived Campa and pushed it through more than 5,000 distributors to ₹4,700 crore of sales in FY26, making it India’s fourth-largest soft-drink maker. Jio launched free voice and data in September 2016; data fell from about ₹250 per GB to about ₹10, and about a dozen operators became three private players within a few years.

The next flowers in reach share the same intermediary: switches, fans, circuit breakers and lighting, which the same electrician fits; pipes and fittings, which the plumber chooses and whose resin conglomerates already make; and waterproofing and construction chemicals, the most exposed corner of Pidilite’s 13.5×. Innerwear has the richest nectar at Page’s 23×, but there the shopper, not a tradesperson, chooses the brand.

Three facts would prove this wrong: a new wire brand winning meaningful utility-grade cable orders within three years at house-wire speed; Indriya reaching 10% of organised jewellery within three years; or no conglomerate entering switches, fans, pipes or construction chemicals by 2029 while those categories keep their current multiples.

→ a moat built from capital is a lease, and q is the rent.

So far this has all been about where to stand. The next question is when.

The slope, not the level

“We tend to overestimate the effect of a technology in the short run and underestimate the effect in the long run.”

— Roy Amara, Amara’s law

Adoption curves fool people twice. Early on, excitement runs ahead of the numbers and most bets fail. Later, the numbers run ahead of the excitement and the market underprices how long growth will last.

Howard Marks adds the warning for the early stage: being too far ahead of your time is indistinguishable from being wrong.

The money sits in the middle of the curve, between roughly 10% and 25% penetration. That is also where a company’s reported multiple looks expensive while its normalised multiple, measured against the earnings capacity it is building, is cheap. The level tells you where a trend is. The slope tells you what it is worth.

Price is earnings times belief

The slope matters even more in the share price than in the business. A price is earnings times a multiple, so a shareholder makes money in only three ways: earnings grow, the multiple rises, or the company pays out cash. The second, re-rating, is the most violent.

A multiple is compressed belief about four things: how fast earnings will grow, what the business earns on the capital that growth needs, how long that advantage lasts before supply arrives, and how certain all of it is. The textbook version fits on one line, P/E ≈ (1 − g ÷ ROIC) ÷ (r − g), and it says something people forget: growth is worth paying for only when the return on capital beats its cost. Every re-rating is a change in one of those four beliefs. Every de-rating is a belief being withdrawn.

Here is the part that costs people money. Markets don’t pay for growth. They pay for changes in growth. Whatever the current growth rate is, it gets extrapolated into the multiple almost immediately, so the surprise, and with it the price move, lives in the second derivative. Three habits turn that gap into cycles. People extrapolate the recent slope. Analysts revise in steps, anchored to their last estimate, so good news arrives in waves of upgrades. And a company that keeps beating looks safer, so it earns a lower discount rate on top of a higher growth rate.

The double whammy

Acceleration rarely arrives alone: it brings a higher multiple, and the two multiply. Follow one illustrative company through a cycle:

For example, four Indian stocks have played this out in public.

Trent is the purest case. Zudio’s store rollout drove revenue up 83%, then 50%, and the multiple doubled with it. Then growth eased to 38% and 17%. Profits kept rising, and the stock still lost almost half its value, because the price had been paying for the slope. PI’s investors met the same arithmetic in slow motion: earnings doubled and the stock went nowhere for two years. Deepak’s phenol plant ran above capacity on fat spreads, and the multiple priced those spreads as if they were permanent. Its own management later put the problem in one line: “phenol and acetone are commodity chemicals. And spreads are dictated by a lot of things.”

Bajaj Finance is the subtlest case, because nothing went wrong with the business. A lender is measured against its book, since the balance sheet is the business. In October 2021 the market paid about 13 times book value, pricing both the Covid rebound and another decade of the same slope. The rebound came. Book value has since more than tripled and annual profit almost tripled, and the shareholder who bought near the peak has made about 22% before dividends, roughly 4% a year. The business compounded; the multiple handed the gain back. A great company and a great investment are different things, and the difference is the price paid for the slope.

The cycle has four phases. In acceleration, earnings and the multiple rise together. In deceleration, earnings keep rising but the multiple shrinks, and results that look good are met with a falling price. In collapse, both fall. And in the phase almost everyone misses, less bad, earnings are still falling but more slowly, and the re-rating begins before the turn shows in any annual report.

The reverse journey is just as real, and it pays more, because it starts where nobody wants to look.

TD Power's managing director, opening the company's August 2026 earnings call, sounded like a man standing at a scarce layer: "there is a tremendous shortage of power generation equipment." Nobody said that in 2020, when the stock traded below ten times earnings.

Two lessons hide in these numbers. The first is that the trailing multiple lies at the turn. Laurus traded at 251 times trailing earnings in January 2025, and Neuland at 160 times in October 2025, each at the bottom of its earnings, just before profit multiplied. Expensive on the trailing number was cheap on the next one. The second is that where you measure from decides what did the work. From Laurus’s price low in March 2023, almost all of its sevenfold gain was the multiple; from its earnings low a year later, almost all of it was earnings. CarTrade’s earnings per share rose almost sevenfold while its P/E barely moved. Measured from 2020, Neuland and TD Power were paid twice: TD Power’s multiple rose about ninefold and its earnings about elevenfold, and Neuland’s earnings rose some thirtyfold while its multiple more than doubled. That is the double whammy in full, and it starts only where the multiple is already low. Nassim Taleb would call that position convex: at ten times earnings, being wrong costs a little and being right pays twice. Magnitude matters as much as odds. A 40% chance of a tenfold gain is worth far more than a 70% chance of doubling, yet most investors weigh only the probability.

Both lessons say the multiple moves on the slope of earnings, not their level. The market re-rates when it revises its belief about how long growth will last and where it comes from. That is why most of the value in a high-multiple stock lies in how long the advantage lasts, not in next year’s number. Markets reward the journey, not the destination: the destination is priced by the time it is obvious.

The adoption curve above hides the same trap. An S-curve adds the most units a year at its inflection, roughly halfway up, and its percentage growth peaks well before that, so the de-rating of a theme can begin while its volumes are still rising fast. Cyclicals invert the reading altogether. Peter Lynch’s advice to buy them when the P/E is high and sell when it is low follows directly: at the trough the multiple sits on depressed earnings, and at the peak on earnings that won’t last. The honest measure is the multiple on normalised earnings.

Where acceleration comes from

An acceleration is only as durable as its mechanism. Fixed costs are the classic: with them, each new rupee of revenue earns more than the average rupee. Utilisation is its cousin: filling a plant that already exists is almost pure margin. Mix is sticky, and while it shifts it hides: a company ramping a new category, channel or plant reports one blended margin across businesses of different ages, and the market prices the average. Neuland’s and Laurus’s contract-manufacturing ramps were hidden exactly this way. Price is fragile, because a price rise invites the supply that will take it back. New capacity makes growth arrive in steps, so acceleration follows commissioning dates; watch the quarter Hitachi Energy’s new transformer factory starts up. And base effects are the most common false signal: growth off a depressed year looks like acceleration but is only recovery.

Deceleration runs the same list backwards: a high base, full plants, prices normalising, and above all, supply responding.

The shape of the payoff

Every item on that list is really about one property: shape. In a linear business a forecast is enough, because being 10% wrong costs about 10%. Few businesses are linear. Where the curve bends upward, through fixed costs, networks, a funnel of options or a shortage, the business is convex: good surprises compound faster than bad ones subtract, so volatility in its drivers pays. Where it bends downward, it is concave. A lender earns a spread when things go right and loses principal when they go wrong; a capacity business's high returns invite the supply that caps them. In a convex business the average outcome understates the value; in a concave one it flatters it. W. Brian Arthur drew the same line in 1996 between two economies: one of diminishing returns, where getting ahead runs into rising costs, and one of increasing returns, where whatever gets ahead gets further ahead. You need to forecast less if you know the shape.

The multiple adds a second bend. Drop the reinvestment term from the formula above, as for a business that needs little capital to grow, and a steady-state multiple is roughly one over the gap between the discount rate and long-run growth. At a 14% discount rate, a belief in 10% growth for decades is worth 25 times earnings; 12% is worth 50 times; 13%, a hundred. Near the top, one point of belief about the distant future doubles the price, which is why expensive stocks trade on the second derivative.

Convexity and asymmetry are different things. Convexity is the shape the business makes; asymmetry is what the entry price does to it. Price can turn a convex business into a concave position. At 190 times earnings, Trent’s upside was already paid for, so execution earned a little and a slowdown cost half. TD Power below ten times was a convex business bought before anyone had priced it. Bajaj Finance at 13 times book was the mirror-image mistake: a convex price for a concave business. Magnitude matters as much as odds. A 40% chance of a tenfold gain is worth far more than a 70% chance of doubling, yet most investors weigh only the probability.

Time adds the last shape. Investment is expensed before it pays, so a business building its next engine draws a J: earnings dip, then compound, and the market prices the dip as decline. Systems compound through loops: scale lowers cost, lower cost wins share, share funds more scale, and accounting records only the cost side of each turn. That is how a high reported multiple on depressed earnings can be a low multiple on what the assets will earn once the investment phase ends.

Not every dip is a J. Some are an L. The test is the marginal unit. In a J, the next unit already makes money while fixed costs and investment depress the total. In an L, the next unit loses money too, and scale only multiplies the loss. The investor’s real job is not choosing between the P&L and the system, but reading the P&L as the earliest evidence of which system is winning.

One loop, two windows

George Soros called it reflexivity: prices do not just reflect fundamentals, they change them. A re-rating lowers a company’s cost of capital, so it can raise money, buy rivals, build plants and pay staff in stock. It also raises the industry’s q, the price list, and invites new capacity. That capacity bends growth, the stock de-rates, capital leaves, the decline turns less bad, and the next acceleration begins.

The re-rating cycle is the capital cycle seen from the stock screen. The capital cycle is the re-rating cycle seen from the factory floor.

Not every higher multiple is cyclical, though. A cyclical re-rating is extrapolated growth, and it mean-reverts, because very few companies sustain high growth for a decade. A structural re-rating is a lasting change in duration, return on capital or the discount rate: a business that becomes the scarce layer in its chain, or moves to recurring revenue, or a whole market whose risk premium falls as steady monthly SIP flows replace hot money. Paying a cyclical multiple for a structural change is the opportunity. Paying a structural multiple for a cyclical one is the trap. The great re-ratings are almost always the market changing its mind from cyclical to structural.

The signals that catch the turns all measure the slope, not the level: how many analysts are upgrading against downgrading; incremental margins against average margins; order inflow against revenue; new capacity as a share of the industry; the growth implied by today’s price against the growth the business can deliver; and the reported multiple against the normalised one.

→ buy the slope when it turns up, sell it when it bends, and never confuse a rising level with a rising slope.

Why the gap survives

If the slope shows up in the data, why does the price lag it? Because most of the market is not paid to see it. The median investor optimises for legibility (can I explain this?), defensibility (will I be fired for it?) and closeness to the consensus story. The alpha seeker optimises for expected value, however the position reads. The two objectives cannot be reconciled, which is why the second is lonely.

The evaluation horizon is the mechanism. Capital judged every quarter avoids ambiguity, and Richard Zeckhauser argued that the richest returns lie in situations that are unknown and unknowable, where institutions are deterred by ambiguity rather than mere risk. Laurus in 2023 and TD Power in 2020 were embarrassing to own for many quarters before they were obvious. The horizon mismatch is not the cost of capturing that return; it is its source. Judge everyone on five-year outcomes and the gap would close.

The market prices the photograph; the investor is trying to read the film. Most investors hunt for companies the market has mispriced. The richer seam is companies it has mis-framed: the right answer to the wrong question. Returns live in the gap between what is true and what is verified. Markets price visible growth well and invisible compounding badly, which is why margins and returns on capital often turn before the story does. Four coordinates keep the reading honest.

One check remains before the map. A theme can be right, early and protected, and still lose money one transaction at a time.

The smallest unit

“If, in some cataclysm, all of scientific knowledge were to be destroyed, and only one sentence passed on to the next generations of creatures, what statement would contain the most information in the fewest words? I believe it is the atomic hypothesis (or the atomic fact, or whatever you wish to call it) that all things are made of atoms…”

— Richard Feynman, The Feynman Lectures on Physics, 1963

Businesses are made of atoms too. A theme, however large and however certain, is only a sum of very small transactions: one order, one bed-day, one case, one folio. If that smallest unit loses money, scale only multiplies the loss.

So every theme gets a second lens. The telescope picks the theme and sees a capability being built; the microscope checks the atom, the equation that decides whether it earns, and the price worth paying. Holding both, without letting either distort the other, is the actual skill.

Four queues in India

The future is already here — it’s just not very evenly distributed.

— William Gibson

That is India in 2026. A Bengaluru data centre and a village without reliable power sit in the same economy. The gap between them is the trend, and the trend is close to certain: more electricity, more factories, more savings in markets, more care for an ageing middle class.

India will become a great economy and a mediocre index at the same time. At about $4 trillion, the state can finally fund factories, roads and a floor under household incomes all at once, which makes the government the economy's largest buyer of first resort. But the forces that make the economy strong, cheap capital, state money and new entrants, are the same forces that compete profits away. Growth will flow through the country, not into its earnings. The money will be made at specific nodes of the value chain, not in the index.

Most stages of a value chain sit in one of three positions:

1. Power and the grid

Atom: one MVA of transformer capacity; MVA shipped × price per MVA − cost per MVA; lever: lead time, and so price.

Living standards track energy per person, and India’s electricity use per person is still a small fraction of China’s. New loads keep arriving on top of old ones: AI data centres, EV charging and cooling in a hotter country. India’s operational data-centre capacity reached 1.8 GW IT in the first half of 2026 and is projected to pass 7 GW IT by 2030. The gigawatt measures the container, not the contents: watch contracted megawatts, not announced gigawatts. Renewables push the constraint onto the wires, because solar and wind sit far from cities and produce intermittently.

The queue shows up in order books. Hitachi Energy India reported a record backlog of ₹29,553 crore on 31 March 2026, up 53.5% from a year earlier, and approved ₹2,000 crore for new plants, including a greenfield transformer factory. New orders, by contrast, grew just 1.6%: customers are waiting for delivery slots.

That capex is also the capital cycle starting. The question for every grid-equipment name is whether demand keeps outgrowing the new plants until they are all running, around the end of the decade.

Cables split the same way. UltraTech’s Ultravolt, launched in September 2026 with 5,000 counters and a target of 100,000 retailers, can buy into house wiring, where the electrician chooses. It cannot quickly buy into high-voltage cables, which utility engineers approve only after type tests. The grid’s queue protects the qualified end; the house’s is open to anyone with a counter.

Global parallel, United States and Europe

The same queue exists in the rich world. Utilities there have waited two years or more for large power transformers, and new data centres in places like northern Virginia wait for grid connections rather than for land or chips. The global grid-equipment makers, including the parents of several Indian listed names, report record backlogs for the same reason. India’s queue is one end of a global shortage.

→ watch lead times, not headlines. When they shorten, the trade is ending.

2. Manufacturing, defence and aerospace

Atom: one qualified part number; part numbers × annual volume × margin per part; lever: qualifications won.

India’s demographic window lasts about two decades, and only manufacturing employs people at that scale, so policy will keep pushing it. China+1 and export controls move orders to trusted capacity elsewhere, and trust takes years to earn. Value-add climbs a ladder from assembly to components to design, and each rung has fewer competitors. Underneath it all, machines make machines, and India still imports most of its high-end machine tools.

The telescope is now backed by paperwork. Buyers want a second source outside China, and Europe, squeezed by energy costs and an ageing workforce, is moving machining, chemistry and engineering east: call it Europe+1. India and the EU concluded their free trade agreement on 27 January 2026; the EU side covers 99.3% of tariff lines, and duties on textiles and chemicals largely go when it takes effect. In February 2026 the US announced a framework to cut tariffs on Indian goods from 50% to 18%. Weeks later its Supreme Court struck down the tariffs the deal was built on; since February most Indian goods have paid an extra 10% on top of normal duties, first as a temporary levy and from July under Section 301, while a final agreement is negotiated.

The phone shows how far the volume has moved, and how far the value has not. India’s smartphone exports reached about ₹2.6 lakh crore ($29 billion) in the first eleven months of FY26, more than three-quarters of it iPhones; four years earlier iPhone exports were under ₹10,000 crore. Most of the parts inside are still imported. Tim Cook explained why the second factory is hard to build, and it had nothing to do with wages:

In the US you could have a meeting of tooling engineers and I’m not sure we could fill the room. In China you could fill multiple football fields. — Tim Cook, CEO, Apple, Fortune Global Forum, Guangzhou, December 2017

That is the gate. The scarce input is the tooling engineer, the process chemist, the metallurgist and the quality auditor, the people who turn a buyer’s trial order into a qualification. What they carry is what the physicist David Deutsch calls knowledge with reach: a plant that learns to make one qualified part makes the next one faster.

Watch value added, not value shipped. Assembling an iPhone 3G in China earned about $6.50 of a roughly $179 manufacturing cost, yet trade statistics counted the whole phone as a Chinese export. India’s phone makers are climbing the same ladder, from assembly towards components. Companies capture the brand; countries keep the craft. And being “not China” has a half-life: an order a buyer moves for diversification stays only if the plant goes on to earn it on quality and cost.

The ladder explains the margins. A phone assembler earns low single-digit margins because any qualified line can do the job. A forger making aero-engine rings earns far more, because its customer cannot switch suppliers for years without re-certifying a part that keeps a plane in the air.

Peter Drucker wrote: “There is surely nothing quite so useless as doing with great efficiency what should not be done at all.” Capital spent racing to assemble one more phone is efficient and, for shareholders, mostly useless. Capital spent earning a qualification is slow, and it compounds.

Buffett learned the first half in his own mill. Each proposal to buy more efficient machinery promised lower costs, but competitors were buying the same machines, so the savings became the industry’s new, lower price. The benefits went to customers. He shut the mill in 1985.

Qualification is a form of what the Austrian economist Eugen von Böhm-Bawerk called roundabout production. Picture Crusoe: he stops catching fish by hand and spends days weaving a net; he catches more ever after. The net shows up as an expense long before it shows up as fish, which is why the most consequential capabilities are underpriced while they are being built.

The same ladder runs through every China+1 and Europe+1 chain. The visible layer competes; the qualified layer collects.

A closer look: pharma's contract makers. A CDMO develops and manufactures a drug for the company that owns it. The work starts small, a few kilograms for a clinical trial, and most molecules fail. The few that succeed are where the money is, because the regulator approves a drug together with the process and the site that make it. Switching supplier means revalidating, refiling and risking the launch. Laurus's founder put the buyer's view plainly: "They don't want to see change of manufacturing lines, change of manufacturing sites to minimize the regulatory complexity.

Atom: one commercial molecule. The unit equation is molecules in the pipeline × the share that reach market × years of supply at volume × margin per year. The scarce input is the record: process chemists, clean inspection histories and the spare capacity customers want to see before they award a programme. The clock is the patent. When a molecule goes off patent, generic makers bid for it and the toll ends, so the pipeline has to keep refilling. For Divi's, Sai Life, Neuland and Laurus, the number to watch is the funnel, not this year's sales.

Two cautions. The US BIOSECURE Act, meant to steer biotech work away from Chinese suppliers, became law in December 2025 in a diluted form that names no company, so Indian CDMOs will see a slow trickle rather than a flood. And in electric vehicles, China’s 2025 export curbs on rare-earth magnets showed where the real chokepoint sits: not in the scooter, but in the magnet inside its motor, and in the cell.

Follow the subsidy one link back. Subsidies land on the visible link, the yard, the fab, the cell plant, while returns settle on the quiet supplier the state cannot do without. Shipbuilding is the cleanest case. In September 2025 the cabinet approved a ₹69,725 crore package, spread over about a decade, for an industry whose listed yards earn roughly ₹20,000–25,000 crore a year: the state is pre-funding about three times the industry’s annual revenue. But ship orders are lumpy and yards fight for them. The steadier claim is on what every yard must buy: dredging, port EPC, the bulb-bar steel that stiffens a hull, welding. Platforms follow the same logic: let the delivery apps fight, and own the rails they all run on.

So map the incentive-to-revenue ratio. Where state support is a multiple of an industry’s current revenue, its suppliers’ multiples look high on today’s P&L and low on the demand the state is pre-funding. Then remember the migration. The profit pool moves to the slowest link to expand, so today’s toll collector, the transformer, the dredger, the hospital bed, is tomorrow’s crowded trade.

Textiles show what fixing policy can unlock. In 2024 the world bought about $558 billion of clothing; China sold $165 billion, Bangladesh $38 billion and India only $16 billion. The gap is more about rules than wages: labour law kept Indian factories small while Bangladesh’s grew to tens of thousands of workers. The new labour codes, in force since November 2025, the withdrawal of quality-control orders on synthetic fibres, a cotton duty waiver, and the UK and EU trade deals remove much of that excuse. As China’s share migrates, a tenth of it would double India’s exports.

Global parallel: United States

In 2016 Berkshire Hathaway completed its roughly $32 billion purchase of Precision Castparts, a maker of castings and forgings for jet engines, which was then its largest acquisition. It was a bet on qualification: a part certified on an engine that flies for thirty years is very hard to re-source. East Asia shows the national version. Korea and Taiwan turned their demographic dividends into wealth by climbing the manufacturing ladder, and China is climbing the same one; much of Latin America, without that climb, stalled at middle income.

→ the real queue is people: machinists, and the machines they run.

3 · The financialisation of savings

Atom: a folio and a rupee of assets; on the rails, a contract traded, AUM × fee yield − cost per folio; lever: SIP count and average ticket.

Indian households still keep most of their wealth in gold and property, and the shift to financial assets will take decades. Digital rails (Aadhaar KYC, UPI, app-based brokers) made a ₹500 monthly SIP profitable to serve. Trust builds slowly: a family that holds its SIP through one crash usually stays for life. And activity businesses behave differently from asset businesses, because trading volumes swing while assets compound.

The flow now shows up every month. SIP inflows hit a record ₹32,490 crore in September 2026, up from ₹29,361 crore a year earlier, and contributing SIP accounts crossed 10 crore in August. Demat accounts reached 23.4 crore in July, about 29 lakh of them opened that month. Follow one SIP down the river and count the toll booths it passes.

Liquidity concentrates, because traders go where other traders already are. That is why the rails are the scarce layer. NSE made the point on 24 September 2026, when it listed on BSE at ₹1,800, just above its ₹1,785 issue price, with a market value of about ₹4.5 lakh crore, among India’s ten most valuable listed companies on listing day. The ₹22,562 crore issue was entirely an offer for sale and was subscribed about 5.7 times.

Here the fee model sets the behaviour. A broker paid per order is paid to make clients trade more. A wealth manager paid on assets is paid when clients stay. The clock on this ecosystem is the regulator, not new capacity: a single circular on derivatives or fees can reset a rail’s revenue overnight. One irony runs through the whole river: the lowest barriers to entry build the highest barriers to scale. Passing an exam is enough to distribute funds; very few firms can run the plumbing every fund depends on.

Global parallel: United States and Japan

The world’s big exchange groups, such as CME, ICE and the London Stock Exchange Group, have turned themselves into data, index and clearing businesses, because trading volumes swing while data subscriptions compound. American fund management shows the other lesson: as low-cost index funds took share, fees across the industry fell for decades, and the winners were those with scale and distribution. Japan’s expanded tax-free NISA accounts in 2024 are a reminder that policy can push household savings into markets, just as India’s rails did.

→ asset-linked beats activity-linked when the rules change.

4. Healthcare

Atom: an occupied bed-day, revenue per bed × occupancy − fixed cost per bed; lever: insured share, case mix, and specialists per campus.

An ageing middle class moves into chronic disease: heart, cancer, kidney and diabetic care, all of which need hospitals. Supply is concentrated in metros while incomes and insurance cover spread to smaller cities. Insurance turns need into demand that can pay. And trust is the product: patients choose on doctors and reputation, not price.

A tier-2 hospital serves patients who used to take a train to a metro, or went untreated. But the scarce input is not the building. A hospital takes three or four years to build; a cardiologist takes more than ten to train. The chain that can recruit and keep specialists in Lucknow, Indore or Coimbatore owns a position that capital alone cannot copy.

Hospital economics also reward the second building on a campus more than the first, because it shares doctors, equipment and a referral base that already exist. The risk is the same capital cycle: when every listed chain announces thousands of beds in the same cities at once, occupancy falls before demand catches up.

Those four queues are the supply side. Now turn the telescope around, towards the households whose rising incomes pay for all of it.

Ten atoms of the Indian consumer

There is only one valid definition of business purpose: to create a customer.

— Peter Drucker, The Practice of Management, 1954

Rising income creates customers for bigger homes, better food, faster delivery, longer holidays. Drucker’s definition is a useful filter, because a theme can create millions of customers and still leave the businesses serving them with a poor atom.

Premiumisation and formalisation

Atom: a square foot of premium space.

As incomes rise, spending moves from unbranded to branded and from informal shops to organised ones. Trust is the product in categories like jewellery, where a hallmark replaces the family jeweller’s word. Grade-A retail space in big cities takes years to assemble and build, so the landlord with the right catchment sets the rent.

Jewellery shows where trust stops capital. Paints and wires carry financial switching costs, which a rival can refund with a fatter dealer margin. Jewellery carries relational ones: the family jeweller, the old gold handed in, the purity checked at the counter. A ₹5,000 crore entrant can open stores quickly; it cannot open trust as fast. The Lindy effect, as Taleb frames it, explains why: a trust that has lasted three generations is likely to outlast the next entrant.

→ trading density is the atom; the landlord owns the denominator. Trust owns the customer.

Buying time

Atom: one order.

India is turning urban time into a tradable commodity. Its cities have started selling time by the minute, and the buyer is anyone whose hour is worth more than a rider’s. Value goes first to whoever owns density in that market, then to whoever owns the shelf on top of it. Sutherland would point out that the Uber map never made a car arrive sooner; it made the wait bearable by removing the uncertainty. Much of what quick commerce sells is the same thing: certainty about when the milk will come, not the minutes saved. The cheapest improvement to this unit equation may be psychological. But the order only pays when basket × take-rate beats rider and store cost per order. Speed can be bought with cash; density cannot. Whoever fills each dark store first gets the lowest cost per order, and every new category added to a dense network lowers the cost of every order already on it, so the leader’s growth feeds itself. Three well-funded platforms are racing for that position, and the scarce input is dense, well-located dark stores.

Step back and the order is the smaller story. Quick commerce is no longer a sales channel; it is India’s new distribution layer, rented by the order. It wins by skipping steps India never built properly: embarrassment-free retail, trusted subscriptions and affordable general trade. FMCG’s old moat was reaching a million kiranas; the new one is a few thousand dark stores run by a few companies, and distribution and awareness are now cheap to rent. Once a household is used to ten minutes, it rarely goes back to the monthly shopping trip, and that habit becomes the platform’s pricing power: in a pseudo-subscription, the customer belongs to the app, not the label. Quick commerce lowers the cost of starting a brand and raises the cost of staying independent: rent access to millions, and the landlord eventually sets the rent. These companies will reach the indices by taking a cut of the brands already there, not by beating them.

The window has a clock, and it is labour. The model runs on the gap between the buyer’s wage and the rider’s, so it is quietly a bet that Indian inequality persists. Every rise in minimum wages is a vote against it, and the rider is the one cost no algorithm can remove. Until that gap closes, the convenience economy turns India’s growing wealth into a demand for time, and whoever owns the infrastructure that delivers it will tax both the consumer and the brand.

India ships about two parcels per person a year, against roughly 65 in the United States and about 140 in China. For years a crowd of venture-funded operators bought volume below cost, and through FY23 most scaled e-commerce logistics firms lost money. When funding tightened, Delhivery bought Ecom Express in 2025 for about ₹1,407 crore, nearly 80% below its last private valuation of ₹7,300 crore.

With the weakest network gone, so was its habit of pricing below cost. In FY26 Shadowfax grew revenue about 69% to roughly ₹4,202 crore and earned its first net profit above ₹100 crore; Delhivery earned ₹347 crore before Ecom Express integration costs and one-offs (₹153 crore reported, against ₹162 crore a year earlier), and its free cash flow turned positive. The two are now the clear leaders of the independent market. Concentration itself is public and usually priced; a change in it, from a crowd to two, often is not. Their atom, the parcel, improves with density: the more parcels a network carries over the same fixed footprint, the lower its cost per parcel.

Global parallel: Europe and the United States

The West ran this experiment first and it failed. Ten-minute grocery start-ups such as Gorillas, Getir and Jokr raised billions in 2021. By 2024 Gorillas had been absorbed by Getir, Jokr had left the United States, and Getir had pulled out of the US, Britain and Germany. The theme was the same; the atom was not. Low density and high rider wages meant the order never covered its cost. India’s dense cities and lower delivery cost give the same theme a different unit equation. Business models do not travel across density gradients.

→ a bigger basket is no help if rider cost grows as fast. Density is what bends the cost.

Housing and home upgrade

Atom: one home fitted out.

Each new home pulls a long tail of spending: pipes, wires, tiles, paint, adhesives, fans. Each existing home repaints and refits on a cycle, so the installed base keeps paying. Land with approvals is the slowest input. At the product end, the brands that contractors and painters ask for by name hold the price.

Paints answer the two supply questions in opposite ways. Capital can rent the dealer for a while, but it has never copied the business for good; and capital is arriving anyway. Asian Paints holds about half of decorative paints and Berger about a sixth. What protects them is mostly invisible from the consumer’s side: the painter’s habits, a tinting machine in the corner of every dealer’s shop, and the credit that keeps those dealers loyal. On that base the leaders earned 25–40% on capital for years.

The accounts show what the Birla Opus attack, backed by about ₹10,000 crore and close to 40% more industry capacity, costs everyone. Opus reached about ₹2,400–2,500 crore of revenue and about 7% share in its first full year, while still losing money. Asian Paints’ standalone revenue fell 5.4% in FY25, its first decline in over a decade; standalone profit fell 32%, from ₹5,315 crore to ₹3,588 crore, and return on capital dropped from about 40% to 27%.

Paints is can be used as an example of a cycle turning against an industry, and history favours the incumbents: earlier challengers bought share for a while, and returns came back when they tired of paying for it. What no one can know is how long a challenger backed by a cement group is willing to pay. That puts paints in the framework’s “wait” box. The evidence that the wave is passing will show up at the dealer before it shows up in the results: thinner schemes, quieter advertising, price increases that stick.

In the other building categories, the installer turns a commodity product into a non-commodity business. Astral, whose plumbers specify the brand rather than the pipe; APL Apollo, with steel tubes sold by weight; and Havells, with switches and cables. Whoever carries the job’s warranty chooses the brand.

Global parallel: United States and Switzerland

Sherwin-Williams answered the dealer question by owning the fence: it runs thousands of its own paint stores across the Americas, so no rival can buy its shelf. Geberit, the Swiss maker of concealed cisterns and pipes, built some of the highest returns in building products by training generations of plumbers, who then specify it. Both show the two ways to defend a tradesperson-picked brand: own the counter, or own the tradesperson’s habits.

That is also why these categories are exposed. A brand the tradesperson picks can be attacked through the tradesperson, and price list applies: Astral trades near 9× capital employed, Supreme near 7×.

Mobility

Atom: one vehicle.

Vehicle volumes grow with income, but value per vehicle grows faster, because buyers trade up to SUVs and higher trims and every model carries more electronics. That makes content per vehicle a better atom than units. Electric two-wheelers show what happens when capital floods in and then leaves.

In FY22 electric scooters looked like a textbook glut: dozens of makers, well over a billion dollars of venture money into Indian EV start-ups, and a FAME-II subsidy of ₹15,000 per kWh. Then, supply changed. The subsidy fell to about ₹2,500 per kWh, an 83% cut, and fresh funding dried up. Ola Electric reached 52% of monthly sales in 2024, sold far more scooters than it could service, and fell to 11.7% for FY26 and 5.3% by March 2026.

What is left is a short list. By FY26 the top five held about 84% of the market: TVS 24%, Bajaj 21%, Ather 17%, Ola 12% and Hero 10%. Annual electric two-wheeler registrations rose from about 41,000 in FY21 to about 1.4 million, roughly one two-wheeler in fifteen. Ola’s fall was a lesson in the atom. A scooter that cannot be serviced is not a cheaper scooter; it is a warranty claim waiting to happen, and every unrepaired one parked outside a home became an advertisement against the brand. The makers that survived were the ones that could fix what they sold. Ather’s share is highest where its stores are densest, near 23% in the South against 17% nationally, and it doubled its store count from about 350 to 700 in FY26.

→ in a scooter, the service network is part of the product.

Experiences

Atom: a room-night or a passenger.

After needs are met, extra income goes to time: travel, stays, entertainment. Rooms in good locations take four to six years to build, while demand for them can rise in two. Airlines face the opposite: aircraft are leased quickly and seats are perishable, which is why Buffett’s Kitty Hawk line was about them.

Protein and nutrition

Atom: a litre of milk.

India is the world’s largest milk producer, but liquid milk is a commodity with thin margins. The value is in what the litre becomes (curd, paneer, cheese, whey) and in the procurement network that collects it from millions of small farmers every morning. That network takes decades to build and is the real moat.

For decades, Indian dairy’s value sat close to the farm gate. Milk was abundant but hard to monetise: perishable, local, uneven in quality, and moved through a fragmented chain. The next decade moves that value downstream: from loose to packaged, from commodity to brand, from local to national, from basic nutrition to convenience, indulgence and protein. That is why value-added dairy (paneer, curd, cheese, yogurt, high-protein formats) will outgrow dairy itself.

Paneer is still 94–95% unorganised for one reason: it spoils. The next decade of Indian dairy belongs to whoever turns perishable milk into branded food people trust.

Premium spirits

Atom: one case.

Drinkers trade up in price tier long before they drink more, so realisation per case rises faster than volume. Three things are slow to copy: brands, state-by-state route-to-market licences, and aged stock, because a malt whisky cannot be made faster than it ages.

Spirits are where what Sutherland calls psychic value is easiest to see. When the convicted counterfeiter Rudy Kurniawan blended cheaper wines into fake great Burgundies, experts could not tell them apart by taste. Much of what a drinker pays for is the story, the label and the occasion, and that is why realisation per case can rise faster than anything in the bottle changes.

Global parallel: Scotland and Japan

Scotch whisky must, by law, mature for at least three years, so a distiller’s stock is a balance-sheet moat that takes years to build. Japan showed what happens when demand outruns aged stock: after Japanese whisky won global acclaim, makers ran so short that Suntory discontinued well-known aged expressions, including Hibiki 17, in 2018.

→ time is an ingredient. So is the story. Competitors can buy neither.

Fewer kids, more per kid

Atom: one child.

Families are smaller, so spending per child rises even as the number of children falls. Parents pay most for what they trust and cannot easily switch: a good school seat, a children’s hospital. Test prep and edtech have low switching costs and went through a boom and bust. Much of the scarce layer, the schools, sits in trusts that cannot be listed.

Global parallel: China

In July 2021 China barred for-profit tutoring in core school subjects. Listed education companies such as New Oriental and TAL lost most of their market value within months. Demand for education did not change at all. The regulator’s clock simply ran out on the business model.

Ageing affluence

Atom: a resident-year or an annuity.

A generation that built savings now needs income and care for twenty or more years after work. The scarce inputs are trust and trained carers. Families move a parent only once, and only to a place they believe in, which makes senior living a small, slow and sticky market.

Global parallel: Japan

Japan, the world’s oldest large society, launched public long-term care insurance in 2000 and built facilities steadily. Its binding constraint since has been people: a persistent shortage of care workers.

Cooling and home electronics

Atom: one AC unit.

A hotter country with rising incomes buys its first air conditioner, and only a small share of Indian homes own one today. Assembly capacity is being added fast, so contract manufacturers compete hard. The parts that are slow to copy are compressor and component localisation, and a service network that turns one sale into years of repeat income.

Global parallel: Japan

Daikin, the world’s largest air-conditioner maker, makes its own compressors, the most complex part of the machine. Owning the component that decides efficiency and reliability is how it kept margins while assemblers competed on price.

Across the ten, where the scarce input is a brand, a licence, a location or trust, the atom keeps its margin as income rises: malls, hotels, spirits, adhesives, children’s hospitals. Where it is capital, as in quick commerce, airlines and contract assembly, rising income mostly funds the next competitor. Paints show the wave arriving; scooters, the wave receding.

Many of those levers are psychological. A richer household does not only buy more; it buys less risk, more certainty and a better story: the hallmark on the necklace, the label on the bottle, the brand on the pipe behind the bathroom wall. A discounted cash flow model sees that psychic value only after it has turned into margin, which is why it is so often underpriced while it is building.

→ income pulls the lever. The scarce layer keeps the gain.

The platforms on top

Competition is for losers. — Peter Thiel

India’s disorder is the feedstock for the platforms. Fragmented retail is what lets Meesho exist; traffic is what makes ten-minute delivery worth paying for; thin trust is what makes verification valuable. Eric Beinhocker defines value creation as rearranging things into more useful order, and Indian chaos supplies plenty to rearrange. Where production is abundant, the customer interface is scarce, and value pools at the tap, not the reservoir, but only where turning the tap costs almost nothing.

The tap has a landlord problem of its own. On a marketplace, every sale a brand makes is a data point the platform collects on how to replace it with a label of its own. The only exit is to be asked for by name. Owning the reservoir is a bet on one use of the water; owning the tap collects on every use someone invents.

Above all ten atoms sits a layer of apps, and under the microscope they split in two. In the first, the next transaction costs almost nothing. A job listing on Naukri or a supplier lead on IndiaMART needs no warehouse, no rider and no loan. Once buyers have the habit of looking there, sellers must be there too. The network is the moat, and the margin rises with scale.

In the second, every transaction carries a physical cost: a rider, a picker, a dark store. That atom has to be earned again on every order, and it turns positive only with density.

Payments are a stranger case. UPI carries no merchant fee, so the atom of a payment is negative; payment firms earn only by selling something else to the people they reach, such as loans, insurance and investments. The 2024–26 listing wave means almost the whole chain now trades in India: Swiggy, Urban Company, Lenskart, Groww, Pine Labs, PhysicsWallah and Meesho joined Eternal, Nykaa, PB Fintech and Paytm.

A cross-current runs under all of it. AI is deflationary at the atom of classic IT services, because the same work needs fewer billable hours. Global companies are also pulling high-end work into their own Indian centres, the GCCs. That helps whoever rents them offices (Embassy, Mindspace, Knowledge Realty Trust) and the engineering firms that build products alongside them (KPIT, Tata Elxsi). For outsourcers priced by the hour, it is far less clear.

→ where the next order costs nothing, scale is a moat.

Every queue has a clock

Heraclitus said no one steps into the same river twice. A bottleneck is a river too: the return it pays is real, and temporary. Each of the four queues ends on its own schedule.

India adds two clocks that run beneath all four. The first is demographic. The young workforce is a window, not a permanent gift; if factories don’t fill in time, the demographic dividend becomes a bill. The second is the state. Subsidies, defence orders and tariffs can start an industry, and the same pen can withdraw them. Policy can start a business; only customers keep it alive. Watch, too, for the rename. Telecom billed minutes, then bytes, and AI now bills tokens: the pipe stayed, the payment moved, and each switch reset who got paid.

A third clock runs above the queues. The test for any incumbent is whether it would survive a rival willing to lose ₹1,000 crore a year for five years. The early warnings appear before revenue moves: dealer margins, credit days, and new capacity as a share of the industry’s.

Deutsch also has a test for a good explanation: it is hard to vary. Change a detail and it breaks. A thesis that survives any outcome explains nothing; “strong management” becomes “execution concerns” and the analysis never changes. Munger’s habit makes the test practical: invert, always invert. So each queue gets a tripwire, the data point that would tell you the thesis has broken.

Back to the fruit

The key to investing is not assessing how much an industry is going to affect society, or how much it will grow, but rather determining the competitive advantage of any given company and, above all, the durability of that advantage. — Warren Buffett, Fortune, November 1999

Buffett said this at the height of the internet bubble, about the very trend that would soon bankrupt Global Crossing. It is the Bhagavad Gita’s line in the language of capital. The trend is the action, and it is open to everyone. The fruit belongs to the few who own a constraint the trend has to pass through, for as long as it has to pass through it. Deutsch has the optimist’s version: problems are inevitable, and problems are soluble. Every queue in this essay is a problem someone will solve. The solver is paid only while the problem lasts.

The edge, then, is rarely a better answer. It is a better question, asked one derivative deeper while everyone else is still doing arithmetic. What would have to be true? What is the base rate? Where is the optionality? What breaks this? The best investors are contrarians in process, not just in conclusions: physicist on first principles, historian on base rates, game theorist on rivals, psychologist on incentives. Most people think in straight lines. The exceptional think in networks, feedback loops and phase transitions, hold competing frameworks in tension, and would rather be precisely wrong than vaguely right. Deep thinking compounds; surface analysis commoditises.

Everything above comes down to two instruments. The first reads the theme, and it starts from the essay’s argument, compressed: inevitable is a line; investable is a curve. A trend is forecast in a straight line, and everyone draws the same one. Returns arrive in bends, and the money is made by knowing which bend you are on. At Resight we read the bend with five letters, CURVE.

The first two letters are the theme’s economics, the third is its slope, and the last two are the price. A great constraint at a belief-saturated price fails the test, and so does a cheap stock whose next unit loses money.

The second instrument reads the company. We call it the Prism, because a prism adds nothing to white light. It separates what was already there into bands you can count. The same public filing, read four independent ways rather than blended into one impression, resolves into a position you can defend clause by clause to someone trying to talk you out of it.

Most ideas should die before the research starts, so the Prism begins with a gate, SIGHT. Signal: why now, or is it just a name someone mentioned? Illegibility: why is the market structurally unable to see it? “I noticed first” is not a reason. Gap: which way, and roughly how far, does the story sit from the operating reality? Horizon: what closes the gap, and roughly when? Thesis-worthy: swap the mechanism for another one; if the thesis still holds, it explained nothing. That last question is Deutsch’s hard-to-vary test, and it kills the most.

What survives the gate is split into four bands, QGVD. Quality is a durable engine: the constraint capital cannot rebuild. Growth is a surface for that engine to compound on: the queue the theme creates. Value is a price that doesn’t yet reflect either: the multiple that lies at the turn. Delta is a clock starting to move: the slope, and the event that forces the market to look.

The bands then combine into two products, never one score. Conviction is quality × value: a good engine at a price that doesn’t reflect it. Convexity is growth × delta: a surface to compound on, and a clock that has started to move. Multiply, don’t average. Addition lets a 5 and a 1 settle into a respectable 3; multiplication refuses. A zero anywhere is a zero everywhere, which is the only honest arithmetic for a moat with no margin of safety, or a discount with nothing to close it.

The test is one sentence, said before a rupee moves. This business has a durable engine, an expanding surface for that engine to compound on, a price that doesn’t yet reflect either, and a clock that’s starting to move. Press on every clause. If one collapses into a story, the idea is not ready. The score was never the output; the four separate answers were. SIGHT is the gate, CURVE reads the theme, and QGVD splits the light.

→ split the light. Find the gap. Own the clock.

Being right about the future is common. Getting paid for it means knowing exactly who will run short of what, and for how long.

So the question to ask of any theme is not whether it will happen. It probably will. The question is the one never asked: when the future arrives, who will it have to wait for?
