The Machine Behind The Machine

Apple taught China to assemble the iPhone. India’s real test is building what builds it.

Neel Chhabra
Oct 10
 
READ IN APP
 

Before you begin

A note on length. This is a long essay. It is long because the machine it describes is long: thirteen value chains inside one phone, the tools behind those, and the history behind the tools. You do not have to read it in one sitting. Below is the essay at a glance and a map of its six parts, then the whole argument in ten sentences; the case for acting on it fits on a napkin at the end. Start there, then follow whichever thread pulls you in.

The essay at a glance

Apple spent two decades teaching China to make the iPhone. The phones shipped out; the skill stayed, and it fed Huawei, Xiaomi and China’s electric-car lines. Now Apple is teaching India. This essay argues that the real prize is not the assembly line India is winning, but the machine behind it: the tools, moulds, controllers and people that build the line. It follows that thread from Adam Smith’s pin factory to the servo motor inside a humanoid robot, through the story of how each workshop of the world was made, and why those who climbed tend to pull the ladder up behind them. It ends with India’s exam paper: what it already makes, what it still imports, and the questions that will decide whether it graduates or stays a supplier.

What you will find

  1. The tuition: How Apple paid for China’s industrial education, and why know-how never travels by email.

  2. The rules of the game: Why almost every rich country climbed behind tariffs, and why India, sitting between two powers, is now courted by both.

  3. Where the money sits: Why assembly is about 3% of a phone’s cost, what 1,285 parts have to do with a pencil, and what turns scarce when AI makes thinking cheap.

  4. How skill compounds: Why volume is the best teacher, why clusters beat companies, and why the strongest firms grow from one deep root.

  5. The machines behind the machines: The mother machines, the servo drive inside every one of them, the humanoid robot that is really a machine tool that learned to walk, and the printer that adds instead of cuts.

  6. India’s exam: The boring foundations, the order in which East Asia got rich, where India already stands, why shelter breeds dwarfs, and what a robot age does to the bottleneck.

Along the way: a Paris gunsmith’s bins of musket parts, two steel tiles a boy could not pull apart, a turbine blade worth ₹1 crore a kilogram, and a closing question the essay leaves open.

“The power of producing wealth is therefore infinitely more important than wealth itself.”

— Friedrich List, The National System of Political Economy, 1841

In 1841 a German economist who had watched England industrialise made an argument that sounds obvious until you notice how rarely anyone acts on it. A nation’s riches, Friedrich List said, are not its pile of goods. They are its capacity to make goods. Wealth can be spent, stolen or taxed away. The power to produce it replaces itself.

List was arguing with Adam Smith, and he was arguing about Britain. Arthur Kroeber, in China’s Economy, traces the recipe that Japan, Korea and Taiwan followed, and China adapted, straight back to List and to Alexander Hamilton. But the line List drew, between owning the output and owning the capability, is the cleanest lens there is for the most expensive education in corporate history. Apple spent two decades accumulating wealth in China. China spent the same two decades accumulating the power of producing it.

The course is now being taught again, to a new student. To see why India, and why now, it helps to start with a few ideas older than any company involved.

Three conditions, once a generation

Industrial revolutions are rarely planned into existence. They are coincided into. Every country that became a workshop to the world got there when three conditions lined up at once: a manufacturing base already running at global scale, a home market big enough to absorb the ugly, low-yield first years of a new industry, and a world actively shopping for somewhere else to buy from.

Any one of these is common. Two together are rare. All three at once happen roughly once a generation: Britain in the late 1700s, Japan in the 1960s, China in the 2000s.

Today India is the only place on earth where all three have arrived together.

Until the eighteenth century India’s economy was nearly as large as China’s, and India dominated the world’s trade in cotton cloth. Same scale of population, same centuries of craft. Yet by the late 2010s India’s exports were about a seventh of China’s, and it ran a trade deficit of more than $100 billion a year. History alone does not explain the gap. Luck and policy do.

Rich neighbours had already written the playbook for China. Hong Kong next door supplied a world-class port, courts and banks. The shipping container arrived just as China opened, making it cheap to split production across borders. And in the late 1990s Taiwan’s electronics industry moved to the mainland almost wholesale, the killer app that built a world-class electronics base nearly overnight. Lay India’s 2020s beside China’s 1990s and the rhyme is close, though not exact.

The division of labour, and its hidden invoice

“The greatest improvement in the productive powers of labour, and the greater part of the skill, dexterity, and judgment with which it is anywhere directed, or applied, seem to have been the effects of the division of labour.”

— Adam Smith, The Wealth of Nations, 1776

Smith’s insight was that splitting work into specialised steps multiplies output. His pin factory, ten workers making some 48,000 pins a day, became the founding image of modern economics. What the image leaves out is that whoever performs the steps ends up holding the skill.

Two centuries later Apple ran the largest pin factory in history, and split it across an ocean: design in Cupertino, making in Shenzhen. The arithmetic was intoxicating. Apple launched its first iPhone, made in China, in 2007; by 2015 it was selling more than 200 million a year.

The ocean was the easy part, thanks to a metal box. On 26 April 1956 a crane in Newark lifted fifty-eight aluminium truck bodies onto an ageing tanker, the Ideal-X. Loading loose cargo onto a ship then cost $5.83 a ton; Malcom McLean’s experts put the Ideal-X at 15.8 cents. Marc Levinson, the historian of the shipping container, gives the consequence in one line: “The container made shipping cheap, and by doing so changed the shape of the world economy.” Plants that made a product from start to finish gave way to specialised ones that shipped parts to each other along ever longer chains. The internet then did for instructions what the box did for freight. Together they turned Smith’s pin factory into a planetary one.

What grew out of it has three tiers. The political economist Herman Mark Schwartz calls it the franchise economy. At the top sit firms rich in intellectual property and light on factories, chiefly in the United States; they own the designs, the software and the brand. In the middle sit capital-intensive makers, chiefly in Japan, Europe and parts of China, that own the machines and make the hard parts. At the bottom sit labour-intensive assemblers and service firms, mostly in emerging markets, working on contract for thin margins. The tiers repeat inside countries too. Apple is the archetype of the top tier. Corning, whose Kentucky plant makes its cover glass, sits in the middle. The contract firms that supply labour to Apple’s own headquarters sit at the bottom, inside Cupertino itself.

Levinson puts the arithmetic on a single phone. In 2009 an iPhone 3G cost an estimated $178.96 to make. The Chinese factory that assembled it received $6.50 of that, or 3.6%. The phone sold in America for $500, and about $321, two-thirds of the price, went to Apple, roughly $95 of it as profit. Trade statistics told the opposite story. China exported about $2 billion of the phones to the United States, a $1.9 billion “surplus”, while the value actually added in China came to about $73 million. “The bilateral merchandise trade balance in iPhones was meaningless,” Levinson concludes. It is worth remembering whenever India’s iPhone exports make headlines. The number that matters is value added, not value shipped.

Tim Cook would later explain the choice in terms of skill, not cost, a point this essay returns to.

Apple did not just place orders. Its designs (unibody aluminium, seamless glass, tolerances in microns) were so hard to make that its engineers lived inside suppliers’ factories, invented the processes and bought the machines. In one year it bought more than 10,000 CNC machines and secured FANUC’s entire pipeline of machines. Apple says it has trained more than 28 million Chinese workers since 2008, more than California’s entire labour force. By 2015, by its own internal accounting, it was spending about $55 billion a year in China.

Foxconn’s founder, Terry Gou, understood what this was before anyone in Cupertino did. Apple work was low-margin. It was also free tuition. Apple engineers would come back to train a line and find strangers on it; Gou had moved the Apple-trained staff onto other customers’ lines.

Kroeber explains why China needed foreign firms this badly. Japan, Korea and Taiwan sat inside the American alliance and received technology, training and market access almost as a gift; their exports were booked overwhelmingly by their own companies. China had no such patron. Buying turnkey factories brought in machines but not the knowledge of how to run them, so China opened its doors to the firms that carried that knowledge in their people. From 1985 to 2005, foreign direct investment ran at nearly 3% of China’s GDP a year, against about 0.5% in Korea and Taiwan and almost nothing in Japan. At the 2005 peak foreign firms made 58% of China’s exports, and well over 80% of its high-tech exports.

The return on all that hosting shows up in one number. Kroeber notes that Chinese-made parts were about 4% of the wholesale value of an early iPhone in 2009 and about 25% of the iPhone X in 2018. That climb, not the assembly line, was the real prize.

Peter Drucker had a sentence for this kind of mistake: “There is surely nothing quite so useless as doing with great efficiency what should not be done at all.” Apple executed brilliantly, every launch, every ramp, every quarter. The trade itself was the problem. It optimised the cost of each phone while giving away the capability to make them.

The most dangerous dependencies are the ones that make you the most money.

We know more than we can tell

“We can know more than we can tell.”

— Michael Polanyi, The Tacit Dimension, 1966

Michael Polanyi, a chemist turned philosopher, spent his later years on a simple puzzle. You can recognise one face among a million, but you cannot explain how. A cyclist stays upright without knowing the physics. Much of what we know is tacit: carried in hands, habits and judgement, never fully written down.

Manufacturing is mostly tacit knowledge. A drawing ships in an email. Knowing which cutting tool wears first, how hard to push an anneal, or which supervisor to call at 2 a.m. does not. It lives in people, and people live somewhere.

This is why a country cannot simply buy its way into making iPhones. Importing turnkey plants is self-limiting, because the plant arrives without the people who know how to run it. In 2025 China gave India a demonstration. It slowed exports of specialised equipment bound for Indian lines and recalled Chinese engineers from them. The factories had land, labour and subsidies. They were short of everything below the waterline.

Brian Potter, who studies how production gets cheaper, has collected the evidence in The Origins of Efficiency. Processes travel badly. In one 1990 study of 32 firms moving production, six failed outright and four had severe problems. Transferring the know-how averaged 19% of a project’s cost and ran as high as 59%. When one firm installed new machines, only 2 of 27 teething problems could be fixed without an engineer on site. TSMC’s first American fab still trailed its Taiwan plants after 25 years, and GM never managed to copy what it learned from its Toyota joint venture at its other plants. One of Potter’s witnesses is an iron historian who spent many hours studying puddling, could describe every step, and admitted he could never have earned a living doing it.

Which explains a strange fact about the iPhone. It is one of the easiest products on earth to move: one shipping container holds roughly $10 million of iPhones, against about $30,000 worth of house. The phone travels anywhere. The knowledge of how to make it barely travels at all.

Ask a master transformer winder or a turbine-blade caster to explain what they do, and they will fail you, not from unwillingness but from genuine impossibility. The knowledge lives below the neck. The world’s most advanced technology companies are now waiting on exactly such people: large power transformers are still built largely by hand, and buyers now wait two years or more for one.

Dan Wang, who spent six years in China studying how it builds things, gives the cleanest anatomy of a technology. He splits it into three parts: tools and equipment, codified instructions such as blueprints and formulas, and process knowledge, the skills “that can only be learned by doing.” His analogy is a kitchen. Producing a new technology is like preparing an omelette: “ingredients, instructions, and a well-equipped kitchen are helpful, but they will not in themselves guarantee a good result.” Money buys the first two parts. Only years buy the third. China’s own counterexample proves it: COMAC, its answer to Airbus and Boeing, absorbed an estimated $71 billion of state money over fifteen years and had, by Wang’s account, scarcely begun producing its first operational airliner. Money, he concludes, “is only the beginning.”

Organisational capability is closer to a sourdough starter than to a recipe. It has to be fed continuously, and it carries the small adaptations of the kitchen it grew in. A rival can buy the same CNC machines, hire consultants with the same playbook and poach engineers with identical degrees. It cannot buy the supply-chain manager who has been through three cycles, or the unwritten habits that let a factory floor switch from consumer electronics to medical devices in weeks. China did not receive a data dump of manufacturing knowledge. It grew organisational muscle memory, with its labs, prototype shops, component suppliers and logistics within bicycle distance of one another. Experience cannot be downloaded.

Goethe put the corollary plainly: “Knowing is not enough; we must apply. Willing is not enough; we must do.” The knowledge exists only in the doing, and this is also why science and the factory cannot be split for long. Andy Grove warned in 2010 that “abandoning today’s ‘commodity’ manufacturing can lock you out of tomorrow’s emerging industry.” Germany, Taiwan and Japan lead in both making and inventing because the two form one feedback loop. Every scaling problem reveals new physics. Every yield problem demands new chemistry. The invention happens during the making.

Wang puts Grove’s warning as a metaphor. America strikes the spark; China sets the prairie fire. The silicon solar cell was invented at Bell Labs in New Jersey in 1954. America treated it as a science project rather than an industry, and by Wang’s count about 90% of the solar chain, from polysilicon to finished modules, now sits in China. The breathtaking fall in solar costs, he writes, “has been driven by manufacturing innovations in China.” The cost of forgetting this is concrete. Since 2000 the United States has lost about five million manufacturing jobs, a quarter of the workforce, and when the pandemic came its firms struggled to retool lines for face masks and cotton swabs. His prescription applies to India with equal force: “the mass production of new technologies needs to be seen as equal in importance to the innovations themselves.”

Winning without fighting

“Supreme excellence consists in breaking the enemy’s resistance without fighting.”

— Sun Tzu, The Art of War

Chess and Go are different games.

Chess rewards the best move on the board in front of you. Go rewards territory: patient encirclement, where a stone placed today matters thirty moves later. Apple optimised each year’s launch. Beijing optimised each decade’s capability, under Deng Xiaoping’s old instruction to hide your strength and bide your time. Both sides won their own game, which is exactly why neither noticed they were playing different ones.

The long game had names. In 2006 Beijing launched “Indigenous Innovation”, aimed explicitly at escaping the role of last-stage assembler of other people’s components. Made in China 2025 followed in 2015, with targets for Chinese firms to hold 40–80% of domestic and global markets in ten high-tech sectors, backed by some 2,000 government guidance funds.

Underneath runs an older pattern that the Japanese economist Kaname Akamatsu called the flying geese. Industrial skill migrates in formation. America taught Japan to build electronics after the war. When the yen rose, Japanese firms carried the skill to Taiwan and Korea. When Taiwan ran short of land and labour, Taiwanese entrepreneurs carried it to mainland China.

The next goose is obvious. Vietnam is too small; India is not. Beijing knows it too, and wants the gate to swing one way: know-how in, never out. The next decade will not be fought over who assembles the phone. It will be fought over who can build, run and fix the machines that assemble it.

The ladder and the gate

“It is a very common clever device that when anyone has attained the summit of greatness, he kicks away the ladder by which he has climbed up.”

— Friedrich List, The National System of Political Economy, 1841

Ha-Joon Chang’s Kicking Away the Ladder takes List’s line and tests it against five centuries of evidence. Almost every rich country climbed with the tools it later told poorer countries to give up: tariffs, subsidies, state model factories, poached workers, smuggled machines and ignored patents. Britain’s tariffs on manufactures were still 45–55% in 1820, two generations into the Industrial Revolution. America, in the historian Paul Bairoch’s description, was the mother country of modern protectionism; its tariff on manufactures was about 40% by 1820 and still 44% in 1913. Then, having reached the top, each began preaching free trade. Chang calls them poachers turned gamekeepers.

Chang’s most surprising finding is where List got the idea. List began as a free trader. He converted to the infant-industry argument only during his exile in the United States, from 1825 to 1830, where he read Alexander Hamilton.

Hamilton wrote his Report on Manufactures in December 1791, when the United States was an agricultural backwater with a handful of mills. He could see that Britain’s power came less from its accumulated wealth than from its ability to make things, which let it project force across the world. “Not only the wealth; but the independence and security of a Country, appear to be materially connected with the prosperity of manufactures.” From that followed the climber’s creed: every nation “ought to endeavour to possess within itself all the essentials of national supply.” Two centuries later the list of essentials includes chips, power equipment and the machines that make phones.

Hamilton also saw why manufacturing compounds. He listed seven ways it enriches a nation, among them the wider use of machinery and “greater scope for the diversity of talents.” In modern terms, factories build technical capability, employ people in skilled work and throw off innovations that spill into other trades. Kenneth Arrow gave the mechanism its name in 1962: learning by doing. Its corollary is the one India keeps relearning. No country theorises its way to industrial capability. It has to build things, badly at first, then less badly.

There is a deeper reason, and it is an investor’s reason. A country that only extracts resources or sells services depends on others for everything it cannot make. A country with factories, supply chains and know-how has options. It can switch suppliers, arm itself, answer a demand no one forecast, or refuse a bad deal. Manufacturing capability is optionality in the truest sense: a call option on futures nobody can yet name. Wealth is a position. Capability is the option to take new ones.

Hamilton’s heirs followed his playbook for a century. Abraham Lincoln, in notes on the tariff written around 1847, warned that abandoning protection “must produce want and ruin among our people.” Bismarck built German heavy industry behind the tariff of 1879. Meiji Japan built state model factories and later sold them to private owners. Then, at the top, each discovered the virtues of free trade, for others. In the 1980s and 1990s the doctrine was institutionalised. The Washington Consensus, enforced through the loan conditions of the IMF and the World Bank, asked poor countries to open their markets, privatise and step back, policies the rich had rarely followed while they climbed. Free trade was never only a philosophy. It was also the rulebook that suited the players already ahead.

Then the asymmetry eroded. Industrial policy came back into fashion in Washington at about the moment China began winning in solar panels, batteries and chips. In 2022 the CHIPS and Science Act put $52.7 billion behind American chipmaking, and the Inflation Reduction Act roughly $370 billion behind clean energy. State support stopped being a “market distortion” the moment the West needed it. Ideology is a tool, not a religion; when the tool stops working, the powerful reach for another. Rules for thee, strategy for me. The ladder is no longer being kicked away. It is being pulled up.

For India the lesson cuts both ways. The referees are now playing, which loosens the rulebook for everyone; no one in Washington can lecture New Delhi about production subsidies. But a ladder that is being pulled up has to be replaced by one India builds itself, rung by rung, out of machines, suppliers and people of its own.

The part that matters for Apple is how Britain guarded its lead. In 1719 it made it a crime to lure skilled workers abroad, and emigrants who ignored an order to come home could lose their land and their citizenship. As know-how moved from hands into machines, the bans followed it: tools for wool and silk in 1750, cotton and linen machinery in 1774, and from 1781 “any machine, engine, tool, press, paper, utensil or implement whatsoever.” The Tools Act of 1785 widened the net again. The ban on workers lasted until 1825, the ban on machines until 1842.

Read 2025 against that list. Beijing’s recall of its engineers and its slowdown of equipment exports are 1719 and 1785 at once: hold back the people, then hold back the machines.

India has an older name for this move. In the Mahabharata, a tribal boy called Ekalavya asks the royal teacher Drona to take him as a pupil and is refused. He goes into the forest, shapes a clay image of Drona, and practises before it until he can outshoot Drona’s favourite student, Arjuna. When Drona discovers him, he asks for the teacher’s fee, the guru-dakshina: Ekalavya’s right thumb. Ekalavya cuts it off and gives it, and never draws a bow as well again. It is the oldest account of a gate. A master who cannot stop a student from learning by watching can still stop him from using what he learned.

Britain demanded that fee of its European rivals in 1719. Beijing asked India for it in 2025. The lesson for a country learning by watching Apple’s lines is not to resent the teacher. It is to make sure that, when the fee is demanded, there is nothing left that the teacher can take.

History also shows how such gates end. They leak. Samuel Slater slipped out of England in 1789 with Arkwright’s mill designs in his head and built America’s first successful cotton mill. The Dutch tried their own ban on machinery and skilled emigrants in 1751, and it failed. Britain dropped its bans once technology grew too complex to copy by smuggling a man or a machine, and licensing took over. A gate buys time. It rarely buys permanence.

The old bans also explain why India’s path is narrower than Britain’s or America’s was. The climbers of the nineteenth century copied freely. The United States did not recognise foreigners’ copyrights until 1891, and Switzerland had no real patent law until 1907, partly so its chemists could use German inventions. India cannot climb that way: it signed up to strict global patent rules decades ago. For India, the legal route to know-how runs through hosting the people who have it, which makes Apple’s engineers on Indian lines worth more than any subsidy.

And there is a sharper irony underneath. In 1700 Britain banned imports of Indian calicoes, the product of what Chang calls arguably the world’s most efficient cotton industry. By 1873 India took 40–45% of Britain’s cotton textile exports, and it was not allowed a protective tariff on cotton imports until 1917. India has watched this film before, from the other side of the screen.

Latecomers have a way back in, and Japan left the measuring stick. It hired foreign experts in bulk, then replaced them: foreign technical advisers in its service peaked at 527 in 1875 and fell to 155 a decade later, a clean measure of how fast the knowledge was absorbed. India could track the same number each September: how many Chinese and Taiwanese technicians its lines still need.

No eternal allies

“We have no eternal allies, and we have no perpetual enemies. Our interests are eternal and perpetual.”

— Lord Palmerston, House of Commons, 1848

Kautilya’s Arthashastra, compiled some two thousand years ago, drew the same map in circles. Your neighbour is your natural rival; your neighbour’s neighbour, your natural friend. And the king who sits between two powers holds a position both must court. Palmerston gave the British version: interests are permanent, friendships are not.

Churchill gave the supply-chain version. Moving the Royal Navy from Welsh coal to foreign oil in 1913, he told Parliament that the fleet must depend on no one country and no one route: “Safety and certainty in oil lie in variety and variety alone.” Apple’s China-plus-one strategy is that sentence applied to factories. India is the variety.

Modern conventional wisdom forgets all of this. It says being caught between two superpowers is bad. If two 800-pound gorillas are throwing punches, the 150-pound human between them gets squashed. Better to pick a gorilla and hope for protection.

But what if that is backwards? When the US and China compete, both court everyone else. With 1.4 billion people, a growing economy and a working democracy, India becomes the belle of the ball. Europe, rearming after decades of outsourcing its security, wants the same dance. Competition even changes the price of technology. In a calm world, advanced countries guard their know-how. In a contested one, “would you like some fighter-jet technology?” becomes a sentence people actually say.

China grew without an American patron, which is exactly why it had to open its doors to foreign firms. India stands outside any alliance too, so its route to know-how also runs through foreign factories on its own soil. The difference is the price. China had to bargain alone. India is being bid for.

Chang supplies an uncomfortable footnote. India adopted universal suffrage at independence, at an income of about $641 a head; Britain waited until 1928, at about $5,115, and the United States until 1965. Britain in 1820, somewhat richer than India was in the 1990s, had no central bank in the modern sense, no permanent income tax and no professional civil service. By historical standards India is over-institutionalised for its income. Chang’s evidence also says investors follow market size more than institutions, which China proved by drawing vast foreign investment with institutions the textbooks called poor. Democracy is not why Apple is coming. It may be why Apple can afford to stay through the next crisis.

Finance has a precise word for this position. Most countries are short the superpower conflict: every escalation costs them. India is long volatility. Each tariff, export control or decoupling announcement raises the value of the one large democracy both sides still need.

A long-volatility position only pays if you can monetise it. Being courted is not being chosen. India can lose this position the boring way: slow approvals, unreliable power, factories that miss their yield targets.

Levinson’s history suggests the ground under that position is shifting. He dates the third globalisation, the age of long-distance value chains, from the late 1980s to the early 2010s, and argues that it began to retreat in 2008, when trade collapsed and, unusually, never fully recovered. Firms had learned the price of distance the hard way. In 2011 the Tohoku earthquake shut the plants of Renesas, the largest maker of microcontrollers for the car industry, and assembly lines on three continents stopped. What now crosses borders fastest, in his account, is ideas: research, engineering and design.

India sits on both sides of that shift. It largely missed the factory globalisation, behind its own walls. It won the globalisation of offices early: by 2003, 285 of America’s 500 largest companies were sending office work to India. Apple in India is a chance to go back for what it missed, just as the factory game turns regional and starts to price reliability above cheapness. China-plus-one is what a value chain looks like once it has read the risk report.

India’s foreign minister has put the cost of that choice more bluntly than any economist. S. Jaishankar called the long focus on services “an elegant excuse for being incompetent in manufacturing.” “No major country in the world,” he said, “has sustained or enhanced its global position without some commensurate buildup of manufacturing.” He described Make in India not as an economic programme or a manufacturing programme but as “a strategic statement.” It is Hamilton’s argument two centuries on, made by the minister whose job is India’s independence and security.

Behind the shift in trade sits a shift in ideas. In 1989 Francis Fukuyama predicted the end of history: a world of liberal democracy and technocratically managed markets whose main danger would be “centuries of boredom.” Four years later Samuel Huntington answered that the great conflicts of the future “will occur along the cultural fault lines separating these civilizations from one another.” For two decades Fukuyama looked right. The historian Nils Gilman now argues that Huntington was merely premature. The dream of a universal consensus around liberal democracy and global capitalism, he writes, “is dead,” replaced by zero-sum transactionalism, Thucydidean power politics and “civilizational states”, with the United States, once the order’s champion, now among the revisionists. “Hegemony is not the same as consensus.” Such pivotal moments, he warns, are ones where “charismatic opportunists rather than competent operators shine.”

Eric Li, the Shanghai venture capitalist and one of Beijing’s most fluent defenders, draws the economic map of that world. The globalisation now ending, he argues, ran on three drivers: American capital and technology, Chinese production and a global market. The next one, he predicts, will run on Chinese capital and technology, world production and a global market. BYD’s plants in Thailand, Indonesia, Brazil and Hungary are the first instalment, and Li insists the motive is economics rather than geopolitics: at home BYD fights dozens of rival carmakers, abroad it is often the only one in town. He claims China needed five years to upend a car industry that Germany, Japan and America took 75 years to dominate. One can discount the boast and still take the map seriously.

India’s question is which column it fills. On the old map it was mostly absent. On Li’s new one, a country of 1.4 billion could easily become the “world production” into which Chinese capital and technology flow, as South-east Asia is becoming: factories without teeth, the Malaysia route. India has kept Chinese investment on a short leash since 2020, even as its electronics makers lobby for Chinese partners who know how to make the components. Apple offers a third map: Western capital and design, Indian production and, if the climb succeeds, Indian technology. In a world of civilisational states, a country’s suppliers are part of its sovereignty. Li supplies the right advice himself, addressed to the West but useful to anyone: when things are not working, “don’t just blame other people. Look at the kind of reforms that you could do.”

Underneath the shifting maps, the model of the system has changed. The old world ran on linear logic: make in one place, ship to another, optimise for cost and speed, hold almost no inventory. Just-in-time assumed stable conditions. The new world is a complex adaptive system in which resilience beats efficiency and supply chains are fought over: rare-earth refineries, chip-fabrication capacity, the ingredients of medicines. India’s own drug industry, the world’s pharmacy, still buys most of its active ingredients from China. The shift is from just-in-time to just-in-case. A supply chain is no longer a logistics problem; it is a living network that must keep working, and heal itself, under fire.

That world is not a new Cold War between two blocs, which is the lazy reading borrowed from the last century. It is closer to a quadrille, a dance of several great powers with distinct civilisational logics, the partners changing with each figure. India and China trade and depend on each other in medicines while competing in electronics and chips. Alignment follows interest, as Palmerston said it would. India brings its own grammar to the dance, Vasudhaiva Kutumbakam, the world is one family, which it chose as the motto of its G20 presidency in 2023. In a world where every power faces constraints at once, the capacity to strike win-win deals is an advantage, not a softness. But the gorilla paradox still applies: harmony persuades only from a position of capability. India’s path is not to become the next China or the next America. It is to become the first India, and the first India will still need machines of its own.

The smile, and its teeth

“Not everything that can be counted counts, and not everything that counts can be counted.”

— William Bruce Cameron, Informal Sociology, 1963

In 1992 Stan Shih, Acer’s founder, drew a curve every electronics executive now knows. Plot value added along a product’s chain and you get a smile. The ends are high: research and design on one side, marketing and brand on the other. The middle, where things are made, sags.

The smile has deepened with time. In the 1970s the curve was shallow: making a thing paid almost as well as designing or selling it. By this century the ends had pulled far away from the middle. The smartphone company furthest from the factory floor, Apple, takes around 80% of the industry’s profits.

That is true, and it is incomplete. Look closely at the bottom of the modern smile and it is not smooth. It has teeth.

A CNC machine cutting titanium for a jet engine is not low-value work. It rests on decades of materials science and tolerances where failure means catastrophe. Snapping pre-made plastic parts together is a different activity that happens to share the word “manufacturing”. Rich economies are not losing manufacturing. They are losing the simple kind and holding on hard to the complex kind. That is why Germany still dominates machine tools, why ASML alone makes the most advanced lithography machines, and why SpaceX builds rockets in California instead of tendering them to the lowest bidder.

Some manufacturing is t-shirts, and some is magnets. The teeth are the magnets. They are chokepoints, hard to copy and stubbornly tied to a place, because they depend on institutional memory, deep supplier networks and workers who understand both the science and the craft.

Kroeber’s audit shows how hard the teeth are to grow, even for the world’s workshop. By 2018 China made over 40% of the world’s electronics exports, up from 5% in 2000. Yet most of that was final assembly, run largely by Taiwanese firms like Foxconn, while chip design, software and the finished product stayed with Apple, Samsung, Intel and Microsoft. Foreign joint ventures took about 80% of the revenue in China’s car industry. Jet engines, airliners and advanced chips stayed out of reach. China’s typical winner offered most of the quality for much less of the price: huge volume, thin margins. That is a follower’s business model. It is not a tooth.

Now apply it to India. On assembly, the numbers are remarkable. India made about 7% of the world’s iPhones four years ago; industry trackers expect 25–28% in 2026. Tata Electronics’ iPhone plants, including those it bought from Wistron and Pegatron, exported $26.3 billion of iPhones over FY22–FY26 under the incentive scheme, edging past Foxconn’s $25.6 billion.

Cameron’s line is the warning: the counted number is not always the one that counts. Local value addition, the share of the phone’s contents actually made in India, rose from 5–8% in 2020 to roughly a fifth. Assembly share has run ahead of content share.

The rule hiding in that chart is almost physical. Value per part is highest where the physics is hardest, and headcount is highest where the physics is easiest. India entered the iPhone on the flat floor of the smile, between the teeth. McGee is right that a phone stamped “Made in India” is, today, no less dependent on China’s supply chain. But the way out is not to climb the walls towards Apple’s brand. It is to grow a tooth.

What does a tooth look like in money? Aerospace castings give the cleanest answer. An ordinary industrial casting sells for about ₹1,200 a kilogram. A standard aerospace casting fetches about ₹30,000. A single-crystal turbine blade for an engine’s hot section, grown as one continuous crystal of superalloy, sells for about ₹1 crore a kilogram. The metal is broadly the same family. The knowledge is not. The firms at the top of that gradient earn margins near 30%; Foxconn’s parent earns a few per cent for assembling the world’s phones.

Climbing that gradient means looking wrong for years. One Indian maker of titanium and superalloy parts bought a second-hand vacuum furnace for about $2.5 million, against $80–90 million for a new one, and committed itself to aerospace grade before it had fully earned it. The commitment came first; the competence was forced to follow.

Thirteen kingdoms in a pocket

“The woollen coat, for example, which covers the day-labourer, as coarse and rough as it may appear, is the produce of the joint labour of a great multitude of workmen.”

— Adam Smith, The Wealth of Nations, 1776

In 1958 the free-market writer Leonard Read wrote an essay in the voice of an ordinary pencil. Its claim was startling: not one person on earth knew how to make it. The cedar, the graphite, the lacquer, the brass ferrule and the rubber came from different continents and different trades, coordinated by nobody. The iPhone is that pencil raised to the power of physics. It has about 1,285 components. Apple’s published supplier list, which covers 98% of its direct spending, runs to fewer than two hundred companies, and behind them stand thousands more. Apple designs the phone and owns the customer. It manufactures none of it.

The back of the phone already tells the story in eight words: “Designed by Apple in California. Assembled in China.” On a growing share of phones the last word is now India. The sentence is a smile curve in miniature. The design end and the brand end are Apple’s; the middle travels.

Figure 20 showed where the money sits in a Pro-class iPhone, whose parts and assembly cost roughly $400–560. One layer down, the phone is thirteen separate value chains, each with its own geography, its own physics and its own balance of power.

Read the table by its last two columns and a pattern appears. Power concentrates where the physics is hardest and the suppliers are fewest. TSMC is the only company that can make Apple’s main chip. Sony makes most of its image sensors. Samsung Display leads on its screens and Corning on its glass. Three companies make almost all the world’s memory, and Japanese firms such as Murata dominate the tiny ceramic capacitors, around a thousand of them in a modern smartphone. The camera is now the costliest system in the phone and still rising. India’s column is filled where the work is mechanical, electrochemical or manual, and empty everywhere physics is hardest.

How each supplier fares depends on two questions: how hard is it to replace, and how much of its revenue comes from Apple? The answers sort the ecosystem into five types.

The physics monopolists are hard to replace, and Apple is a minority of their sales: TSMC (Apple is about a fifth to a quarter of its revenue), Sony’s sensor business, Samsung Display, Corning, Murata. They set prices. Peter Thiel’s line that competition is for losers describes their position exactly. The specialists are also hard to replace but depend heavily on Apple: LG Innotek, Largan, Cirrus Logic (which has drawn the large majority of its revenue from Apple) and RF makers such as Skyworks. They are profitable and fragile; one redesign can erase a large share of their sales. The scale executors are individually replaceable and collectively indispensable: Foxconn, Luxshare, Goertek, Lens, Sunwoda. Their margins are thin, and their real return comes from using Apple-certified capability to win other customers, as Luxshare did when it went on to assemble Apple’s Vision Pro and to build for carmakers.

The fourth type is a warning. In 2009 Tim Cook set out what became Apple’s doctrine: the company believed it must “own and control the primary technologies behind the products” it makes. The insourcing casualties followed. Imagination lost the iPhone’s graphics in 2017; Dialog sold its power-chip team to Apple in 2018; Intel’s processors left the Mac in 2020; Apple’s own C1 modem and N1 wireless chip began replacing Qualcomm and Broadcom in 2025. The rule for any supplier is blunt: if Apple wants to control what you make, your Apple revenue is temporary. The fifth type is India’s: the geographic hedges, such as Tata, Motherson, Aequs, ATL’s Indian plant and Jyoti CNC, whose first asset is simply that they are not in China. They win share because of where they are and must keep it on yield and ramp speed.

Jeff Bezos once warned rivals that “your margin is my opportunity.” Apple applies the same rule to its own suppliers, and four shifts now decide where the money in this pocket moves. Apple keeps pulling strategic chips inside, which helps TSMC and hurts the merchant chipmakers. Cameras keep getting bigger and costlier, which helps Sony and LG Innotek and squeezes Apple’s margin. On-device AI needs more memory just as AI data centres bid up memory prices, which helps the three memory makers. And assembly keeps moving out of China, which helps India and Vietnam.

Clayton Christensen offered the deepest explanation of why the money moves the way it does. In The Innovator’s Solution he called it the law of conservation of attractive profits. When one stage of a product becomes good enough and modular, so that any competent firm can do it, profits drain away from it. They reappear in the adjacent stage where performance is still not good enough, where parts must be designed together and the work is still hard. Phone assembly, standard boards and enclosures have become modular; cameras, chips, memory and machine controllers have not. Profit lives where the problem is still hard. India entered at the stage that has just become easy, and its task is to reach a stage that is still difficult before that one, too, becomes easy.

For an investor, the map is also a ranking. The best seat is the physics monopolist; the most dangerous is the Apple-dependent specialist whose part Apple would like to own. Most of India’s listed suppliers sit, for now, among the geographic hedges, and the market is pricing them as if they will become something more. Warren Buffett described the businesses worth owning as economic castles protected by unbreachable moats. In Apple’s empire the castle is easy to locate: it is whatever Apple cannot build for itself and cannot buy from anyone else. India’s suppliers will be judged on whether, a decade from now, they have moved from the edge of the map towards that square.

Water and diamonds

“Nothing is more useful than water: but it will purchase scarce any thing … A diamond, on the contrary, has scarce any value in use; but a very great quantity of other goods may frequently be had in exchange for it.”

— Adam Smith, The Wealth of Nations, 1776

Smith’s paradox took a century to solve. Price is not set by how useful a thing is. It is set at the margin, by how hard it is to get one more. Water is essential and abundant, so it is cheap. Diamonds are trivial and scarce, so they are dear. Every era has its water and its diamonds, and fortunes are made by noticing when they swap.

A swap is under way now. Artificial intelligence is making thinking cheap, so bits deflate. Scarcity does not disappear; it concentrates in what cannot be copied: power, copper, transformers, machines and factories, so atoms inflate. And when any text, image or voice can be generated, what cannot be faked becomes precious, so trust appreciates.

Read the iPhone through that lens. Its design files are bits; they could be emailed anywhere in seconds. Its aluminium body, camera module and circuit boards are atoms, and the capacity to make them at Apple’s tolerances takes years to build. The reason someone pays a thousand dollars for it is trust. For thirty years India sold bits to the world, through software services. The repricing favours the country that learns to sell atoms.

Which atoms? Justus von Liebig, the German chemist, popularised in the 1840s the idea that a crop grows only as far as its scarcest nutrient allows. Picture a barrel with staves of unequal height: it holds water only up to the shortest one. Eliyahu Goldratt turned the idea into a management creed: an hour lost at a bottleneck is an hour lost for the whole system. India’s iPhone barrel has tall staves for assembly and labour, and short ones for circuit boards, camera and display modules, machine tools and the instruments that measure a micron.

There is an older version of the same lesson. In 1848, before he told San Francisco about the gold at Sutter’s Mill, Sam Brannan quietly bought up every pan and shovel he could find. He became California’s first millionaire without mining an ounce. The AI boom has the same shape: nine physical layers, from lithography machines to cooling skids, ending in a craftsman winding copper around grain-oriented steel for a power transformer. The supply chain of intelligence ends in a human hand. The iPhone is where that chain meets a pocket. In the iPhone rush, the shovels are the machines, the gauges and the hands.

Volume is a teacher

“Efficiency is the engine that powers human civilization.”

— Brian Potter, The Origins of Efficiency

Potter opens with penicillin. Fleming found it in 1928, but a vial still cost about $200 in 1943. By 1952 it cost $1.30. Nothing about the molecule had changed. What changed was how it was made: a better growth medium, a better mould strain, and thousands of small fixes to tanks and filters. A discovery becomes abundance only when someone learns to make it cheaply.

In 1936 an aircraft engineer named Theodore Wright noticed that every time cumulative output doubled, the labour needed per plane fell by roughly the same percentage. Boeing’s first B-17 bombers took more than 140,000 labour hours each; by plane number 6,000 it was under 20,000. Solar panels fell from $100 a watt in 1975 to $0.26 in 2021, about 23% cheaper with each doubling of output. Wright’s law is the nearest thing manufacturing has to gravity.

Two of Potter’s findings matter enormously for India. First, the skill lives in the process, not only in the worker. Boeing kept getting faster even as its veterans were drafted and replaced by novices, because jigs, fixtures and sequence sheets carried the knowledge. That is good news for a country training workers quickly. Second, assembly learns faster than machining. Rearranging people is easier than redesigning a machine, so assembly lines slide down their curves quickly while machining crawls. Read that as an investor. The step India mastered first is the step anyone can master quickly. The slow-learning steps are slow to copy too, which is exactly what makes a tooth a tooth.

Taiichi Ohno, who built the Toyota Production System, put the attitude in one line: “Having no problems is the biggest problem of all.” Every September, Apple hands its suppliers a fresh set of problems.

That is the twist that matters most. Potter shows that learning curves reset whenever the product changes. An apparel factory that switched styles saw costs jump and the curve start over. Intel built its “Copy Exactly” policy, making new fabs identical down to the paint, precisely to avoid that reset. Apple redesigns its phones every year, so every autumn the whole supply chain climbs a fresh curve. China’s real advantage is not that it is cheap. It is that its resets are small, because it has relearned the iPhone every year since 2007.

Volume alone is not enough, though. The cautionary pair is titanium and solar. Both got cheaper at about the same rate per doubling. But since 1975 solar’s cumulative output has grown roughly 300,000-fold while titanium’s grew five- to tenfold, because titanium is still made by the same awkward 1930s batch process. Same learning rate, wildly different number of doublings. Volume gives a country chances to learn. It does not guarantee that anyone takes them.

When Intel decided in 1996 to assemble and test microprocessors in Costa Rica, nothing about the country was suited to chips; the draw was taxes, tariffs and a welcome. Intel soon accounted for a fifth of Costa Rica’s exports. By 2014, when it moved that assembly to Asia, there were enough Costa Rican engineers to staff the engineering and design centre that took its place. In effect, Intel’s investment “gave the country a comparative advantage in electronics manufacturing it had not previously possessed.” Assembly can leave a residue. Whether it does depends on how many people the host country keeps when the line moves on.

Wang describes the same residue at a scale no other country has reached. By his count China has about 70 million manufacturing workers to America’s 12 million, and on the high-end lines, he says, they are “learning to solve three new problems a day before breakfast.” Follow one of them. A worker on Shenzhen’s original iPhone lines in 2008 might build Huawei phones a year later, start a drone company a few years after that, then move into electric-vehicle batteries. That is why Shenzhen became the hardware capital of the world. Capital cannot shortcut the path. Apple, worth trillions, spent about a decade on a car before abandoning it in 2024. Xiaomi, worth a small fraction of Apple, pledged $10 billion to cars in 2021, and its electric cars are now on Chinese roads. One company had the money. The other had the process knowledge, and the suppliers who carried it.

This is the asymmetry that competitiveness debates miss: tacit knowledge compounds. Every production run is a repetition, every quality failure a lesson in pattern recognition, every line rebalanced a piece of intuition that travels to the next product. The result is optionality in the workforce itself. The same hands assemble phones one year and battery packs the next; only the substrate changes. It pays three dividends. The first is iterative innovation, thousands of small process gains that add up to a cost and quality lead no laboratory breakthrough can match. The second is rapid scaling: a deep bench of experienced people means a new product ramps without a cold start. The third, and the most powerful, is industry creation. Consumer electronics led to electric cars and drones because the factory floor had become a platform for new sectors. A machine depreciates from the day it is installed. Process knowledge appreciates with every cycle. The West built supply chains. China built supply-chain capacity: the meta-skill of spinning up new chains on demand.

From a line to a loop

“Nothing is lost, nothing is created, everything is transformed.”

— Attributed to Antoine Lavoisier, 18th century

Twentieth-century manufacturing was a line. Raw materials came out of the ground, were fabricated into parts, assembled into products and shipped. Value moved in one direction, and the only question was who stood at which station.

Twenty-first-century manufacturing is a loop. Materials are designed before they are dug up. Fabrication and assembly run continuously into each other. Finished products are bundled with software and services into something a customer subscribes to. And used products come back, through recycling, as the next batch of materials. Every new node on the loop is a place where knowledge, not labour, sets the price.

Apple already lives on the loop. An iPhone is hardware bundled with an operating system and a subscription business, and Apple now makes many of its enclosures from recycled aluminium. For India the loop opens doors the line never had: materials science, where a decade of speciality chemicals has built real depth; recovery and refining of the metals inside old devices; and the software that runs factories, which is why IT firms such as HCLTech and Wipro turned up on Apple’s list of prospective Indian partners.

The mysteries of the trade

“The mysteries of the trade become no mysteries; but are as it were in the air.”

— Alfred Marshall, Principles of Economics, 1890

Marshall noticed something about the cutlery workshops of Sheffield and the textile towns of Lancashire. When enough firms in one trade sit near each other, skill stops belonging to any single firm. Children pick it up without being taught. A good idea in one shop is in the next one by Friday.

Kyle Chan, describes China’s modern version of this as a jigsaw puzzle. Every piece already in place makes the next one easier to fit. Batteries learned for phones became the base for electric cars. Motors learned for cars feed robots. Drone makers build lidar. Xiaomi now makes cars, and BYD makes its own chips. Each industry supplies, buys from and teaches the next.

And the puzzle was assembled patiently. Chan’s reading of China’s Five-Year Plans shows the same targets returning across decades: semiconductors, aviation, energy. High-end CNC machine tools got their own national megaproject in 2009 and became a Made in China 2025 priority. Deng Xiaoping had declared in 1978 that science and technology were the key to modernisation. The tactics changed. The direction did not.

India’s puzzle has real pieces: assembly at scale, a world-class auto-components base, cheap steel, a pharmaceutical industry the world depends on, and the largest pool of software engineers outside America. The gaps are just as clear: battery cells, display panels, and the machines that make everything else. Of those three, machines matter most, because they are the piece every other piece is cut by.

The taproot

“[Americans] call in the aid of machinery in almost every department of industry. Wherever it can be introduced as a substitute for manual labour, it is universally and willingly resorted to.”

— Joseph Whitworth, report to the British Parliament, 1854

In 1785 a Paris gunsmith named Honoré Blanc laid out fifty musket locks, took twenty-five of them apart, mixed the pieces in bins, and rebuilt working locks from whatever came to hand. Thomas Jefferson, then America’s minister in Paris, watched and wrote home urging America to copy it. Four years later revolutionary crowds wrecked Blanc’s workshop. The historian Ken Alder says France then suffered fifty years of “technical amnesia” and missed the Industrial Revolution unfolding across the Channel.

Stewart Brand tells this story in Maintenance: Of Everything, in a digression on how America learned to make machines make machines. The first lesson is the one France learned the hard way. Precision is not a discovery that stays discovered. It is a living practice, held in workshops and hands, and it can be lost in a decade.

Britain held on to it. In 1774 the ironmaster John Wilkinson patented a way of boring cannon from solid castings. A year later he bored the cylinders for James Watt’s steam engine. The gap between piston and cylinder fell from half an inch, the width of a little finger, to about a tenth of an inch, the thickness of an old shilling. That fivefold step is what made the steam engine practical. England had about 2,000 engines by 1800 and 10,000 by 1850. Precision moved from weapons into engines, and the engines moved everything else.

Watt’s own verdict on Wilkinson’s first cylinders survives in a letter: the bore “does not err the thickness of an old shilling at any part.” An interest in tolerance, Simon Winchester writes in The Perfectionists, began that day.

America’s turn came out of humiliation. In August 1814 a smaller British force routed some 6,000 to 7,000 Americans at Bladensburg and then burned Washington. The War Department resolved that its weapons would be built from interchangeable parts that soldiers could repair in the field. Under Colonel George Bomford the Ordnance Department spent more than $2 million over the following decades on that single goal. Brand’s judgement is blunt: only the federal government could have paid for it.

The decisive contract was small. In 1819 the War Department ordered 1,000 breech-loading rifles from a Maine inventor, John Hall, and in doing so paid for a new way of making rifles, not just for rifles. At Harpers Ferry Hall built his locks from 23 identically machined parts and checked them with 63 gauges arranged in three sets: one for the workers who made the parts, one for the inspectors, and a master set that ruled the other two. In 1852 a flood at Harpers Ferry soaked some 9,000 muskets made on the armory’s machines. They were taken apart, cleaned and reassembled at random. Every one worked. The historian Merritt Roe Smith concluded that Hall’s system “formed the taproot of modern industrialism.”

Notice what the War Department bought. India’s production-linked incentives (PLI) pay for output, for phones shipped. Hall’s contract paid for a process, for the capacity to make anything to a gauge. Notice, too, what set America moving. It was not ambition but a burned capital. Countries rarely commit to precision out of hope. They commit out of fear. India’s fear arrived in 2020, with a pandemic that froze Chinese supply and a deadly clash on the Himalayan border.

America’s twentieth-century version of the story has a name: Bill Knudsen. In 1921 he left Ford, and the next year took a job at General Motors paying a fraction of his old salary; asked what he wanted to be paid, he said anything they liked. What mattered was where he would finish, not where he started. In 1940 he left GM’s presidency to organise war production, and the arsenal that followed produced some 86,000 tanks, 286,000 warplanes and 2.5 million trucks. The lesson fits in one line: great industrial nations are forged under existential pressure, not built. India now faces three such pressures at once: a militarily superior China, a Western aerospace supply base that cannot keep up with its order books, and the drone revolution’s inversion of the economics of war.

Then the skill travelled. Armory mechanics went to Samuel Colt, whose Hartford factory ran about 400 special-purpose machine tools. A former Colt mechanic ran the Connecticut factory of Wheeler & Wilson, which made sewing machines with truly interchangeable parts, about 25,000 a year by 1860. Sewing-machine shops turned into bicycle shops; Albert Pope converted a rifle and sewing-machine works to make his Columbia bicycles, and by the mid-1890s America made close to a million bicycles a year. A Chicago toy maker, Western Wheel Works, learned to stamp hubs and sprockets from sheet steel. Then came Henry Ford. The product changed every generation. The skill compounded across all of them.

India’s version of that migration has already started, and it runs from cars to aircraft. Exports of aircraft, spacecraft and parts rose about fivefold, from $1.4 billion in FY20 to $7 billion in FY25. Boeing buys about $1.4 billion a year from more than 375 Indian suppliers, by one industry count; Airbus buys over $1.6 billion and is aiming for $2 billion before 2030. The pull is real: the industry has a backlog of more than 17,000 aircraft, about twelve years of current output, and its Western supply base is ageing. The firms answering are often car-parts makers. Bharat Forge now machines engine parts and landing gear; Sansera machines engine casings; Motherson makes Apple enclosures and aircraft parts. Aerospace suppliers must pass AS9100 and NADCAP audits and first-article inspections that can take years. That is Hall’s three sets of gauges, written as paperwork.

Capability climbs a qualification ladder. First a supplier proves one part. Then it proves a process and becomes qualified. Then, after years without defects, the customer lets it certify its own parts: in Hall’s language, the customer hands over the master gauge. At the top, the supplier becomes the sole source, the gauge itself. One Bengaluru precision maker took 21 years of zero-defect delivery to earn five self-certifications. Another Indian supplier has shipped 13,000 sets of flap-track beams for a single aircraft programme, all on time. No subsidy buys a rung. Only time and output do.

The same ladder is now visible in a third industry: India’s data-centre build-out. India generates about a fifth of the world’s data but hosts only 2–3% of its data-centre capacity, a gap that data-localisation rules are now closing. About 27% of the equipment spend (power, cooling, cable, fibre) can be made in India. Indian suppliers are entering not on price but by passing audits. One heat-exchange maker won a global order after the incumbent failed a weld audit; a pipe maker welds its spools in clean rooms because a speck of dust can kill a $40,000 GPU; Aeroflex is the exclusive Indian maker of cooling skids for Vertiv. When a $2 part can destroy a $2 million rack, procurement stops being purchasing and becomes risk transfer. One line belongs beside Hall’s gauges: qualification is a stock, not a flow. In Geoffrey West’s language, operators age like organisms while qualified suppliers grow like cities.

Brand’s history also contains the smile curve, a century before Stan Shih drew it. Isaac Singer’s sewing machines did not have interchangeable parts; they were hand-fitted. Singer won anyway, with rent-to-buy plans, trained instructors and a worldwide sales machine, and by the late 1880s the Singer company was selling well over half a million machines a year. Colt advertised interchangeability he had not fully achieved. Wheeler & Wilson had the better factory; Singer had the better market. The brand end of the smile won the profits. The precision end won something else: the mechanics, and through them a country’s next industry.

Companies capture the brand. Countries keep the craft.

Measurement made the last step possible. In 1896 a Swedish machinist, Carl Edvard Johansson, made a set of 103 steel gauge blocks that could be stacked to check about 20,000 dimensions in thousandths of a millimetre. Ford bought his American business in 1923. Its tool rooms kept 140 full sets at a constant 68°F so the steel would not swell, with master gauges accurate to two millionths of an inch. Ford himself said that without the Johansson blocks, “our tool rooms would be blind.” The maxim often credited to Lord Kelvin, that what you cannot measure you cannot improve, is a management cliché because it is a manufacturing fact. Here India’s gap is quiet but real: calibration labs, coordinate-measuring machines, metrology engineers. A country that cannot measure a micron cannot sell one.

One last detail in Brand’s chapter deserves underlining. Ford, he notes, was completely open about its production methods; its engineers had “no skeleton closets.” That openness is why Ford’s system spread across the world within a generation. Beijing in 2025 chose the opposite instinct: keep the engineers home, slow the machines. Openness spreads a method. Gates only delay it.

Brand sums up two centuries of the story in four words: “Tinier always means faster.” Each step down the staircase made the next machine possible. Which brings us to the machines themselves.

Roots, not branches

“The diversified corporation is a large tree. The trunk and major limbs are core products, the smaller branches are business units; the leaves, flowers, and fruit are end products. The root system that provides nourishment, sustenance, and stability is the core competence.”

— C. K. Prahalad and Gary Hamel, Harvard Business Review, 1990

Bharat Forge does not really sell forgings. It sells the ability to make a safety-critical metal part that must survive extreme loads without failing: a crankshaft under repeated combustion pressure, an artillery barrel under repeated firing, a landing-gear component under repeated impact, a turbine blade under repeated thermal cycling. That is one capability (metallurgy, forging process control and precision machining) sold into markets that share no customers, no cycles and no competitors. The moat is not any single product. It is that the capability is hard to copy, with decades of process knowledge, a zero-defect discipline and the certifications to prove it, while the markets it can be pointed at are many and mostly uncorrelated. Not every bet works; some of its electric-vehicle components did not. But that is what a platform looks like. It is a different animal from a conglomerate that owns unrelated businesses for portfolio reasons. The metallurgy is the asset. Carbines, turbine blades and landing gear are that one capability pointed at whatever the world needs now.

Baba Kalyani, describes the turn in the cycle plainly: “Every phase of industrial growth has its own demands. Some reward capacity. Some reward cost leadership. The phase ahead will reward capability, technology ownership, speed and the courage to build before demand fully unfolds.” And on defence: “When a nation can build its own carbines, its own artillery, its own armored platforms and its own mission-critical systems, it stands more securely on its own feet.” From roughly 1990 to 2020 the dominant story was that value lived in software, brands and financial engineering, and that physical things could be made wherever was cheapest. The world is now remembering what it forgot for thirty years. If a country cannot make the things that matter, its sovereignty depends on someone else’s goodwill.

Samvardhana Motherson runs the same play with a different root. Automotive was the industry forgiving enough, for fifty years, to let its underlying machine (design, qualify, manufacture, integrate, consolidate) become provably repeatable. Each new vertical (aerospace, consumer electronics, health and medical, logistics, rolling stock and semiconductors) is the same bet run again in a new industry, incubated inside the parent until it can ramp at scale and then allowed to stand on its own.

Laksh Vaaman Sehgal puts the car business simply: “If you look at the key real estate in the car… we own that real estate.” He also explains why the timing suits a layer player. With automation shrinking the labour arbitrage and logistics and currencies volatile, customers increasingly prefer local production, and “you can really be competitive in any location.”

Aequs shows the same pattern starting from the hardest customer of all. Its Belagavi cluster became, in 2009–10, the first Indian supplier approved by both Airbus and Boeing for aerospace processing. Fifteen years of that discipline are now being pointed at electronics: in FY 2025–26 its consumer revenue grew 84% to ₹1,840 million as consumer electronics moved from pilot to commercial scale, on 218 CNC machines dedicated to the business. By its own account, it was the first company qualified to make and ship two lines of mechanical enclosures for “a leading global consumer electronics company.”

Rajeev Kaul, states the transfer exactly: aerospace demands rigorous qualification, and consumer manufacturing “requires the same process discipline applied at significantly higher volumes.” The company still reported a loss of ₹113 crores on revenue of ₹1,230 crores, the cost of capacity built ahead of volume. Rajeev Kaul’s own sequence is the right way to read it: capability into scale, scale into cash, cash into returns.

Depth in one real capability, built over decades, is worth more than breadth built quickly. Apple’s qualification is the curriculum, but the companies worth owning are the ones that turn an Apple line into a root, and then point it at every high-precision, safety-critical market they can qualify for. A country works the same way. Its branches are industries; its root is what its people and machines can make.

Countries have roots too, and China’s are now feeding a third tree. The parts that let a bullet train or an electric car turn high-voltage power into motion are, almost one for one, the parts an AI data centre needs: silicon-carbide and IGBT power chips, high-power inverters and converters, batteries, liquid cooling, and high-voltage direct-current wiring. A car and a server rack share one problem. Both must push a great deal of electricity through a small space without losing it as heat.

The companies show the root at work. CRRC Times Electric learned to make high-power IGBT chips for China’s trains after its group bought Britain’s Dynex in 2008. It now leads in rail and grid power modules, ranks second only to BYD in power modules for China’s new-energy cars, and has opened a silicon-carbide line. Two Chinese firms, TanKeBlue and SICC, held about a third of the world’s silicon-carbide substrate market between them in 2024, a year of sharp price falls. Sanhua, a key Tesla supplier, says it is expanding into liquid-cooled servers; Google sent a procurement team to Envicool for data-centre liquid cooling this year. Battery makers led by CATL are steering capacity towards storage for AI data centres. And as Nvidia, Google and Microsoft move AI data centres to 800-volt direct current, Innoscience, a Chinese maker of gallium-nitride chips, has signed on to supply the power devices.

Nobody in Beijing wrote electric-car subsidies as an AI policy. But capacity built for one demand became the cheapest supply for the next, because the root was the same. This is the essay’s argument at national scale, and it is the one place where India’s order of building matters. India’s data-centre boom is arriving before its electric-car industry has scaled, so the power shelves, battery racks and cooling skids for its new halls will be imported unless the root is grown on purpose. This is the weakest stave: the power switch inside every motor drive. The same switch sits in a locomotive, an electric car’s inverter and a data centre’s power shelf. Build it once and India owns three trees. Import it and India rents all three.

True to itself

“Perfection is the child of time.”

— Bishop Joseph Hall, Works, 1625

One winter evening in the 1950s, a London precision engineer brought home a velvet-lined box of steel tiles and laid two of them on the dinner table, one on top of the other. His ten-year-old son could not pull them apart. The faces were so flat that they clung together; they could only be slid apart sideways. The tiles were Johansson gauge blocks, and the boy was Winchester, who grew up to write that history of how precision engineers made the modern world.

Winchester begins by separating two words most people use interchangeably. Accuracy is how close you come to the true value. Precision is how closely your attempts agree with one another. “Accuracy is true to the intention; precision is true to itself.” A rifleman whose six shots cluster tightly, but off the bull, is precise and inaccurate. A factory can be the same.

Precision also has a family tree. In 1789 the lockmaker Joseph Bramah could not make his famous locks fast enough, so he hired an 18-year-old blacksmith from the Woolwich Arsenal, Henry Maudslay, and skipped the customary seven-year apprenticeship. Maudslay built the machines to make the lock parts, then built an iron lathe with a slide rest and a lead screw, so the same settings gave the same part every time. It has been called the mother tool of the industrial age, and it worked to a ten-thousandth of an inch. His shop then trained the next generation, Joseph Whitworth and James Nasmyth among them. Whitworth went on to build a measuring machine that read a millionth of an inch and to standardise the screw thread. Winchester notes that the British Standard Whitworth thread is still used in workshops “from Carlisle to Calcutta.” India inherited the standard. It did not inherit the lineage that produced it.

Winchester adds a detail about flatness that reads like an industrial policy. A surface plate cannot be made flat by checking it against another plate, because a convex plate will happily hide a concave one’s errors. The old rule was that to grind one surface perfectly flat, you must grind three at the same time, each checked against the other two. No single party can certify its own truth. A buyer, a maker and an independent measurer are the three plates of a supply chain.

Then comes the most useful paradox. Henry Royce and Henry Ford were both born in 1863, and the companies that bear their names began a year apart, in 1903 and 1904. Royce, Winchester writes, was “captivated by perfection”; Ford was “consumed by production.” Yet precision mattered far more to Ford. A hand-built Rolls-Royce could be filed and fitted until it was right. A moving assembly line could not wait: every part had to fit the first time, or the line stopped. Precision, in Winchester’s phrase, is essential to the “unforgiving tyranny of a production line.” Apple asked for something neither Henry attempted: Royce’s finish at Ford’s volume, hundreds of millions of times a year. That is the bar India’s lines are being asked to clear.

Ford learned one more lesson the hard way. When he complained that SKF’s bearings were out of tolerance, Johansson’s gauge blocks showed the bearings were right and Ford’s own gauges were wrong. His factory had been precise only to itself. A supply chain needs a shared, external standard, which is why calibration labs and accredited measurement are the quiet infrastructure under every export.

Ohno put the same point in management language: “Where there is no standard, there can be no kaizen.” A factory cannot improve what it cannot measure against something fixed. Continuous improvement, the habit every Apple supplier is graded on, rests on a calibration lab most visitors never see.

Two failures in Winchester’s book show what happens when that infrastructure slips. On 4 November 2010 a Qantas A380 lost an engine over Indonesia, minutes after leaving Singapore. The cause was an oil pipe about 5 centimetres long, made at a Rolls-Royce plant in 2009, whose bore had been drilled off-centre, leaving part of its wall thinner than its 0.5-millimetre minimum. It passed inspection. After 8,500 hours in the air it cracked, oil sprayed into a hot cavity, and a turbine disc burst. Forty engines were pulled from service. Winchester’s verdict is blunt: it “just failed in real life.” Australia’s investigators blamed culture, warning that quality systems tend “to regress if not constantly monitored.”

The Hubble Space Telescope failed the other way. Its main mirror, launched in 1990, was among the most precisely polished objects ever made, and it was wrong. The instrument used to test it had been assembled 1.3 millimetres out of place, so the mirror was ground, perfectly, about 2.2 microns too flat at its edges. Technicians who doubted the reading had trusted the laser. It took a space mission in 1993 to fit corrective optics.

A line often attributed to John Ruskin has outlived his century because factories keep proving it: “Quality is never an accident; it is always the result of intelligent effort.” India has had its own warning. In December 2020 workers at Wistron’s iPhone plant near Bengaluru rioted over unpaid wages, and Apple put the supplier on probation after finding lapses in how it managed working hours and pay. Culture regresses fastest exactly where volume grows fastest.

Winchester ends his history not with a machine but with a balance. A mature industrial society, he argues, holds two ideas at once: reverence for machine precision and reverence for human craft. “Japan, I would argue, is just such a place.” At Seiko’s factory in Morioka the two sit on either side of a single wall. On one side, robots assemble about 25,000 quartz watches a day. On the other, a couple of dozen craftsmen at wooden benches build Grand Seiko mechanical watches by hand, to a hundredth of a millimetre, about a hundred a day.

The most instructive detail is a near-loss. In Winchester’s telling, Seiko dropped Grand Seiko in 1978 and let its ageing watchmakers go. When demand returned in the mid-1980s, the company found it had kept the names and addresses of every one of them. They came back and trained a new generation, who still work on that side of the wall. Tacit knowledge, once dismissed, survives only if someone kept the addresses. Apple’s engineers carried China’s first education in their heads. India’s second will depend on who it trains, and on whether it keeps track of them.

Machines that make machines

“Give me a place to stand, and I will move the earth.”

— Archimedes, as recorded by Pappus of Alexandria

Machine tools are called the mother machines because every other machine is made by one. Archimedes wanted a lever and a place to stand. An industrial economy’s place to stand is its machine tools.

Potter’s history of the gunmaker Beretta reads like a route map for India. Beretta began with craft gunsmiths, then bought English machine tools, then American special-purpose machines. When the M1 Garand demanded ten times the precision, it built its own machine tools, then added statistical quality control, then numerically controlled machines. Henry Ford walked the same road at speed. By 1914 Highland Park held 15,000 machine tools, Model T parts were held to a sixty-fourth of an inch, and the car’s price fell from $780 in 1911 to $360 in 1916. Ford replaced machines the moment better ones existed, once throwing a $180,000 custom piston machine into the yard. And Potter’s numbers hold good news for a newcomer: the smallest efficient machine-tool maker needs under 1% of its market, so the industry supports hundreds of mid-sized firms. Aircraft has room for two. Machine tools are one of the few teeth a country can grow without a single national champion.

States have helped before. Chang describes Prussia’s Peter Beuth, whose craft institute bought foreign machines, had them copied, then handed the originals to private firms. Frederick the Great gave every foreign weaver who settled in Silesia a free loom. By 1842 Silesia had nearly caught up with Britain.

Think about what it takes to turn 1,285 parts into a phone, a million times a day. A factory has to shape metal and glass, join the pieces, move them between steps, see every defect, prove the finished phone works, and remember each unit’s history so it can learn from failures. Each verb is a family of machines.

Apple owns much of this machinery itself; it sits in suppliers’ factories, on Apple’s balance sheet. Apple’s capex was $12.7 billion in FY25. By the author’s estimate the equipment pool tied to Apple’s devices runs $15–20 billion a year once suppliers’ own purchases are added. And because every September brings new geometry, the top of the stack gets rebuilt every year: new fixtures, new test programmes, new inspection recipes.

Relocation adds a second layer. Moving 15 points of global iPhone capacity to India means building that capacity twice: roughly $4–5 billion of one-time equipment over the next few years, plus about $1 billion a year of retooling. The same machines then serve AirPods, Watch and Mac, and the far larger Android volume India already makes.

This is where an old slogan earns a rewrite.

The template has been European machines, Indian labour. The opportunity is Indian machines.

Precision engineering in India today looks like speciality chemicals did a decade ago. The advantages rhyme. Deep engineering talent. Quality at a fraction of Western cost. Some of the world’s cheapest steel. Captive home demand, and export buyers actively seeking a non-Chinese source. Chemicals had China-plus-one. Precision engineering has China-plus-one and the iPhone as a reference customer. Apple has reportedly worked with about 35 Indian firms able to build machinery for its suppliers, including Jyoti CNC, Titan Engineering & Automation, Bharat Forge and Wipro PARI.

Underneath sits a frontier. It has been almost 75 years since numerical control was invented, about 50 since early computer-aided manufacturing, and 35 since metal 3D printing. We still cut metal with metal; lasers and electron beams are nowhere near replacing the cutter. Whoever multiplies the speed at which we arrange matter, in physics or in how a factory orchestrates its machines, does not just win a supplier slot. They change the cost of making everything.

A few Indian names are now on that frontier. Kaushik Mudda and Navin Jain started building robots in their hostel at Bengaluru’s RV College of Engineering around 2010. After graduating they built their first simple CNC machine with about ₹1.75 lakh of prize money from debating and robotics contests, and sold three-axis machines to small workshops from a rented garage. In 2018 they set a harder target: five-axis machines, a market held by German and Japanese makers with half a century of history, where one machine cost more than $500,000 and took most of a year to arrive. Mudda reckons the last new entrant before them was an American firm in the 1980s. It is Maudslay’s story again: Bramah’s locks needed parts no machine could make, so an 18-year-old built the machines.

Their company, Ethereal Machines, spent about four and a half years on research, much of it in basements, kept alive for four years by a single seed investor, Blume Ventures. Each machine has about 3,200 parts, and some surfaces need 600 hours of hand scraping, taking off half a micron per pass: Winchester’s flat plates, made by hand in Bengaluru. Nobody in India had built one, so there was no one to hire; the founders had to learn mechanics, electronics, software and the mathematics of motion at once. When the machine finally worked, in 2022, buyers still doubted it. Indian customers told them that only Germans and Japanese could make such machines.

So Ethereal stopped trying to sell machines and started selling what they make. A customer sends a drawing and places a first order of $10,000–20,000, instead of seeking approval for a half-million-dollar capital purchase. By May 2025 its fleet had grown from 7 machines to more than 60 running three shifts, revenue had risen fivefold in a year and the team had reached 300. By September 2026 it had 75 machines, with seven acres bought for a factory planned to hold 1,000. Customers include HAL, BEL and Collins Aerospace; for one, a 30-hour job fell to seven. When nobody will buy the machine, sell what it makes. Every part shipped becomes a reference, and every shift of uptime is a rung of the qualification ladder climbed in public.

The national arithmetic explains why one company matters. By Mudda’s estimate India buys about $2.2 billion of CNC machines a year and imports about $1.2 billion of them, with foreign makers dominating the four- and five-axis end where the hardest parts are cut.

America, China and Korea built manufacturing before they built services, while India went from farms to software and skipped the middle.

Ethereal is one of a wider shift. Prayank Swaroop, a partner at Accel, describes three generations of Indian manufacturing start-ups. The first were marketplaces such as Moglix, Udaan and OfBusiness, which matched buyers with suppliers that already existed. When they ran out of suppliers able to make sophisticated, high-value products, they had to build supply themselves, mostly by buying factories, the way Foxconn had to send Chinese engineers to bring its Indian plant up to speed. That was the second generation. The third generation creates its own intellectual property. The IP has to carry “a large business rather than a niche.”

The state is filling in the base of the stack. IIT-Madras’s Advanced Manufacturing Technology Development Centre, set up in 2016 with the Ministry of Heavy Industries, developed high-precision machine tools at about a quarter of the cost of machines built from imported components. Since 2022–23 it has worked with Indian firms on the motors, drives and controllers inside industrial robots. Its hardest problem is not engineering but habit: Indian factories are used to buying their systems from Germany and South Korea. Airbound, a Bengaluru maker of delivery drones, shows what a single rule change can do. It began with a $500 hackathon grant; once India allowed drones to fly beyond the pilot’s line of sight, it raised $37 million from Greenoaks and DoorDash.

Every thread in this essay arrives at the same bottleneck. All need a first customer willing to buy from a newcomer. Start-ups still make less than 1% of India’s manufacturing output. Apple has been that first customer for India’s phone assemblers. The most valuable thing it could do next is not to assemble more phones in India, but to let its Indian suppliers fill their lines with Indian machines.

The machines behind the machines

“Give us the tools, and we will finish the job.”

— Winston Churchill, February 1941

A country’s machine-tool capability is the closest thing economics has to a civilisational indicator. The machines that make machines set the ceiling on everything downstream: defence production, semiconductors, electric vehicles, aerospace. Every workshop of the world has had its own. Britain’s Industrial Revolution was not only steam; it was Maudslay’s screw-cutting lathe and Whitworth’s standard threads. America’s rise ran through the armories and Ford’s tool rooms. Japan’s post-war climb was inseparable from FANUC and Mazak. China’s dominance today rests on having absorbed machine-tool technology from German and Japanese partners through joint ventures, licences and reverse engineering from the 1980s to the 2000s, and then making the lower and middle tiers its own.

Look at who makes the world’s mother machines and the map is short. Of the ten largest CNC machine-tool builders by revenue, four are Japanese: Yamazaki Mazak, AMADA, Okuma and Makino. Germany has the largest, TRUMPF, and shares DMG MORI with Japan. South Korea, the United States, Switzerland and China have one each. India has none. Together the ten take in roughly $21 billion a year, about a twentieth of Apple’s annual revenue. It is a small industry with an enormous lever: nearly everything Apple sells passes, at some point, through one of their machines.

India started early. The government founded Hindustan Machine Tools in Bangalore in 1953. HMT did make machine tools, and its machine-tool units still serve industry and defence. But the name became famous for something else: wristwatches, made from 1961 with Japan’s Citizen. Seventy years on, India still imports most of its high-precision CNC machines, from Germany and Japan (DMG MORI, Yamazaki Mazak, Okuma), from Taiwan and, increasingly, from China. The watch unit closed in 2016. The machine-tool gap stayed open.

The most surprising thing about that list is how little of it is Chinese. After twenty years as Apple’s classroom, China has one builder in the top ten. China has cracked low- to mid-precision CNC machines in huge volumes, trained by the demand of Apple and Tesla, but not the high end; a good share of DMG MORI’s high-precision machines are exported to China. Chinese industry analysts themselves name CNC control systems, precision components and high-end materials as remaining chokepoints. Apple taught China to run machines by the tens of thousands. It did not teach China to build the best ones. The tuition loop has a ceiling, and the ceiling is the mother machine.

That ceiling is Ethereal’s opening, and its numbers are now those of an exporter. About 70% of its revenue reportedly comes from abroad, from customers in the United States, Israel and Germany: the export test, passed in the hardest market there is. Its Aura platform claims positional accuracy of ±4 microns; the company holds the AS9100D aerospace certification and checks parts on coordinate-measuring machines. A $13 million Series A in 2024 was followed in June 2026 by a $28.5 million Series B, led by Avataar with Peak XV, to build a 300,000 sq ft factory near Bengaluru and an Indian multi-axis CNC controller: the electronic brain of the machine, and a layer India has always imported. The road there was not glamorous. Mudda’s short book about the early years is titled 63 Rejections, for the number of noes before the first customer. In 2019, when the power failed and they could not afford a backup, the founders drove a car into the garage and kept working by its headlights. They went through about a dozen prototypes, stripping each one for the aluminium, steel, motors and drives to build the next. The economist Albert Hirschman called this the hiding hand: if founders could see the true odds in advance, few would ever begin. Manish Sabharwal, puts it more briefly: entrepreneurship is the art of staying alive long enough to get lucky.

The deeper bottleneck may be the machine shop itself. America has 16,876 machine shops; 83% employ fewer than 20 people and just nine employ more than 500. India’s base is the same shape: Mudda describes his competition as tens of thousands of small job shops running three to five machines each. East Asia’s history backs the logic. Scale and export discipline, not a thousand subsidised workshops, is what grows a tooth.

Put together, the pieces make an unusual business. Ethereal’s edge is not cheaper CNC machines. It is the full stack: it owns the machine, is building the controller, runs its floor on its own software and sells the output, machined parts, as a service rather than selling the machine. HMT never had that model. HMT thought like a foundry, counting machines shipped. Ethereal thinks more like a chip designer guarding its intellectual property, and then runs its own fab to prove the design, as the great integrated chipmakers once did. The fleet earns cash while it earns a track record, and every hour of cutting is data for the controller.

That last link is the one to watch. If the controller is real IP, with its own motion algorithms rather than an integration layer over bought motion-control chips, it is the moat. If it is not, Ethereal is a very good machine shop. An investor’s first diligence question about any Indian machine-tool company is the same: who wrote the code that moves the axes?

Tim Cook once explained why Apple had stayed in China, and it was not wages. China, he said in 2017, had moved past being a low-cost country long ago; the reason was skill, in huge quantity and in one place. In the United States, he said, he was not sure Apple could fill a room with tooling engineers, while in China it “could fill multiple football fields.” Tooling engineers are the people who design the jigs, fixtures and moulds that turn a drawing into a million identical parts. They are the scarcest input, and the one India most needs to multiply.

People are the other constraint. Mudda trains machine operators from India’s industrial training institutes and diploma colleges in four to six weeks, and says plainly that he is not importing talent from Germany or Japan to run his machines. The harder gap is in the middle. In his telling, India’s manufacturing skill skipped a generation: the best toolmakers are in their fifties, and the cohort after them went into software. That is Seiko’s address book again. India’s version of the recalled watchmakers is its 55-year-old toolmakers, and the clock is running on how long they can still teach.

There is also a quiet link back to Apple. Mudda deliberately avoids car parts, which were designed decades ago for cheap three-axis job shops making millions of identical pieces. He chases industries where designs change quickly and engineers shave weight by the gram: aerospace, medical devices and electronics. That is the September sawtooth again. The faster a product changes, the more it rewards flexible five-axis machining. Apple’s annual redesign trained China to run machines in volume. The next lesson in Apple’s supply chain could be taught by a machine built in India.

The brain of the machine

“Without a strong manufacturing industry, there will be no country and no nation.”

Made in China 2025, Beijing policy document, 2015

Every machine moves on one part that almost nobody sees: the servo drive. It takes a command, powers a motor, reads the motor’s true position from an encoder, and corrects the error, thousands of times a second, holding an axis to within microns. The mathematician Norbert Wiener built a new science, cybernetics, around the principle in 1948. Feedback, he wrote two years later, is “a method of controlling a system by reinserting into it the results of its past performance.” Without that loop there is no CNC machine, no robot arm, no EV line and no automated phone factory.

India assembles the box. It does not yet design what makes the box valuable. The layers tell the story. Assembling the circuit boards, thermal design and testing is the easy part, and India does it today. Beneath that sit the power stage of IGBT and MOSFET switches and their driver chips, then the analog layer that senses current and shapes the pulses. At the top is the moat: real-time motion firmware that synchronises several axes within microseconds over industrial networks such as EtherCAT and PROFINET, damps vibration and retunes itself as loads change. That firmware has been refined for decades inside Yaskawa’s Sigma, Siemens’ Sinamics and Mitsubishi’s Melservo platforms. Only five countries hold the full stack: Japan, Germany, the United States, Switzerland and China.

The stakes are concrete. Servo motors and drives are a market of about $17.3 billion worldwide in 2026, with the top five suppliers (among them Yaskawa, Mitsubishi Electric, Siemens and FANUC) holding about 45%, and Chinese entrants such as Inovance closing in. India’s own market was about $452 million in 2025, heading to roughly $643 million by 2034, and its high-performance content is overwhelmingly imported. Indian firms such as Darwin Motion make drives; Bharat Bijlee sells German KEB drives under a partnership; integrators work at scale. But the power switches, driver chips, firmware and network protocols are licensed or bought, and there is no national research programme for the controller yet. Every factory India automates pays this import bill first.

Firmware sounds like software, and software is what India does best. But motion firmware is tacit knowledge in compiled form: thousands of tuning decisions about vibration, heat and changing loads, learned on real machines over decades. It is Polanyi’s problem written in C. That is also why it may be India’s most natural tooth. The controller is the one place in the machine where the country’s bits meet its atoms. It is no accident that Ethereal’s Series B pays for an Indian CNC controller, or that China’s own analysts still list CNC control systems among their chokepoints.

The controller is also the stickiest layer in the machine. FANUC and Siemens dominate it worldwide, and their lead is not a chip. It is half a century of motion algorithms; thermal compensation that corrects for a spindle and frame warming through a shift; and tool-path optimisation that looks ahead through thousands of moves so the cutter never stutters. Around that sits a moat of habit. Every CAM program needs a post-processor written for each controller, every operator was trained on one, and every service engineer in the district knows it. A factory that switches controllers rewrites its programs and retrains its people. That is why an Indian controller is worth more than an Indian machine, and why it is harder to build.

Seeing all of this at once has been hard, until recently. A public living map of India’s climb up the hard-technology stack, IndiaBUILD, now tracks it. By October 2026 it had mapped 7,100 capabilities a modern industrial country needs, across eighteen sectors from aerospace and semiconductors to machine tools and medical devices.

The map’s most useful page lists chokepoints: missing capabilities that block many others downstream. It is Liebig’s barrel drawn for a whole country. At the top sits semiconductor metrology and inspection equipment, which blocks fourteen products downstream; Winchester’s Hubble mirror shows why measurement comes first. Next come chip-design toolchains and thin-film deposition. Then a gap with no Indian builder at all: power semiconductors such as IGBTs and thyristors, the same switches that sit in the power stage of every servo drive, every EV inverter and every grid converter. One missing part becomes many missing industries.

Beijing’s 2015 document was a list of gates to build. IndiaBUILD is a list of gaps to fill. The maxim often attributed to the American statistician W. Edwards Deming applies to nations as much as to factories: “In God we trust; all others must bring data.” Apple measures its suppliers to the micron. A country that measures itself to the capability, and is willing to show where it fails, has at least begun grinding the first of its three plates.

A machine tool that learned to walk

“If every instrument could accomplish its own work, obeying or anticipating the will of others … chief workmen would not want servants, nor masters slaves.”

— Aristotle

Aristotle imagined self-moving tools more than two thousand years before anyone could build one. Today’s version is the humanoid robot, and its economics are surprisingly old-fashioned. One widely cited estimate puts about 56% of the cost of Tesla’s Optimus robot in its actuators: the motorised joints that turn electricity into precise movement. An actuator is the servo loop of the previous section shrunk into a joint. It packs a frameless motor, a gear reducer, a screw or bearing to carry the load, an encoder to read position, a torque sensor and a drive.

List of who makes those parts is short. Strain-wave reducers come from Harmonic Drive Systems in Japan and from Leaderdrive and Green Harmonic in China. Planetary roller screws come from Rollvis in Switzerland, Ewellix (now part of Germany’s Schaeffler) and SKF in Sweden. Frameless motors come from Kollmorgen, maxon, TQ-RoboDrive and CubeMars; precision bearings from THK and IKO in Japan; encoders from Renishaw in Britain. Japan, China, Switzerland and Germany dominate.

Read the list again and it is the CNC supply chain. THK’s linear guides and IKO’s bearings carry machine-tool axes. Renishaw’s encoders and probes measure them. SKF and Schaeffler bearings turn their spindles. And every part on the list is itself made on precision machines: the flexible teeth of a strain-wave reducer, the threads of a roller screw and the races of a crossed-roller bearing are all ground to microns. A humanoid is a machine tool that learned to walk. Any country that wants to lead in robots, humanoids and automation has to build its CNC base first.

This is the quiet reason China’s Unitree is so far ahead. It builds much of its own actuator stack and sells robot dogs and humanoids at a fraction of Western prices, and it does so on top of the volume CNC base that Apple and Tesla spent two decades training. Two of reducer makers are already Chinese, beside Japan’s Harmonic Drive. No Indian name appears on the list. The robot is downstream of the lathe, and the lathe is downstream of the iPhone. India’s humanoid strategy, if it wants one, should start in its tool rooms.

Every motor in that chain hides a dependency smaller than a fingernail. The frameless motor in a robot joint, the servo on a machine axis, the traction motor in an electric car and the speaker and Taptic Engine in an iPhone all run on permanent magnets made with neodymium and, for heat resistance, dysprosium. These are elements, not ingredients; the periodic table does not respond to market forces. And China does not merely dominate the supply. It is the supply chain. In Dürr and Heilmann’s 2025 estimates, mining rare earths is worth about $2.3 billion a year, processing about $10 billion and magnet-making about $20 billion. Below that chokepoint of roughly $32 billion sit more than $1.5 trillion of end markets: electric vehicles, smartphones, PCs and wind turbines.

The leverage compounds at each stage. Mining is powerful. Processing, which separates ore into oxides through dirty, chemically intensive work, is more powerful. Magnet-making is checkmate: a country that mines and refines its own rare earths but cannot turn them into magnets still ships its elements to China and buys back the finished product. The West offshored the dirty stages and confused cleanliness with cleverness. China has pulled the lever more than once. After a 2010 clash near the Senkaku Islands it stopped rare-earth exports to Japan, which then relied on China for about 90% of its supply. On 4 April 2025 it put export licences on medium and heavy rare earths and the magnets made from them, and within weeks Indian two-wheeler makers were warning of stalled electric-scooter lines. Bajaj Auto spoke of “a dark cloud on the horizon.”

The responses are the lesson. Japan spent about ¥100 billion on recycling, substitutes, stockpiles and mines abroad, and in thirteen years cut its dependence on China from about 90% to about 60%. Apple signed a $500 million deal with MP Materials in July 2025 for recycled magnets made in Texas from 2027. India’s cabinet approved a ₹7,280 crore scheme in November 2025 for 6,000 tonnes a year of magnet capacity, in a market where today’s magnets come mostly from China. The scheme aims at the right stage: not the mine but the magnet. You can scale code to a billion users overnight. You cannot scale a magnet line that way.

Taking away, adding on

“By sculpture I mean that which is made by taking away. That which is made by adding on is like painting.”

— Michelangelo, letter to Benedetto Varchi, published 1549

Every machine in this essay so far works the way Michelangelo carved. It starts with more metal than it needs and takes the rest away. A lathe, a mill and a five-axis CNC are chisels with motors. Additive manufacturing reverses the gesture. A laser melts a thin bed of metal powder, one layer at a time, and the part grows upward out of nothing. Anna-Sofia Lesiv and Sachin Maini, who study how things get made, rank it beside interchangeable parts: “an innovation on par with Whitney’s interchangeable parts revolution.”

Its promise is that complexity becomes nearly free. A cutter can only shape what it can reach, so a complicated part has to be made in pieces and joined. A printer does not care how tangled the inside is. When GE built an experimental engine, the Advanced Turboprop, with about a third of it printed, its engineers folded more than 800 separate parts into 12. Hirschman would spot the second-order effect at once. Each of those 800 parts had a supplier, a purchase order and a qualification file. Printing does not just make a part. It deletes a layer of the supply chain, and the value moves to whoever controls the powder, the printer and the process recipe.

Apple has already started. Since 2025 the titanium cases of the Apple Watch Ultra 3 and Series 11, and the USB-C port enclosure of the iPhone Air, have been printed from 100% recycled titanium powder. Each machine fires six lasers at once and builds a case in more than 900 layers over about 20 hours, from powder 50 microns across: “like very fine sand,” in the words of J Manjunathaiah, Apple’s senior director of manufacturing design. The process needs about half the raw material of forging and machining, and Apple estimates it will save more than 400 tonnes of titanium in 2025 alone. It took years of experiments, because nobody had printed cosmetic metal parts at scale before.

Then comes the sentence that matters most for India: “Twenty hours and over 900 layers later, the cases are ready for final finishing.” Finishing usually means machining and polishing. Printing does not escape the taproot. Lesiv and Maini are blunt about its limits. A CNC mill can hold a thousandth of a millimetre, and a printer cannot. Printed metal carries tiny voids that can grow into cracks, so printed parts fail sooner unless they are treated and surface-machined. And industrial metal printers cost from hundreds of thousands to millions of dollars. The printer moves the precision problem rather than removing it: upstream into the powder, downstream into the machine tool and the metrology lab. It adds a station to the line. It does not retire the lathe.

Rocketry learned the same lesson in public. In March 2023 Relativity Space launched Terran 1, a rocket about 85% printed by mass. It cleared the hardest moments of the climb, but its upper stage failed and it never reached orbit. Within weeks the company retired it and redesigned its next rocket as a hybrid: conventional aluminium for the long, simple tank barrels, and printing only where it cuts complexity. “We can actually take the more simple, straight sections of the vehicle and build them traditionally,” its chief executive, Tim Ellis, explained. Print where complexity pays; cut where it does not. It is an engineering rule, and it is also a capital-allocation rule.

India has one result here worth knowing. On 30 May 2024 Agnikul Cosmos, a start-up incubated at IIT Madras, flew its Agnibaan SOrTeD rocket on Agnilet, a semi-cryogenic engine printed in Inconel as a single piece, with no assembled parts. ISRO called it the first controlled flight of a semi-cryogenic engine made by additive manufacturing, and the company says it can print an engine in about seven days. The pattern matches Ethereal’s: where the incumbents’ head start is shortest, an Indian team can stand at the frontier. Metal printing is only about 35 years old. Five-axis machining has half a century of German and Japanese tacit knowledge behind it.

Vishesh Rajaram of Speciale Invest, one of Agnikul’s early backers, explains why that opening exists with a children’s story. David did not beat Goliath because he was brave. He won because he had a sling, the catapult of his day, a weapon that changed the terms of the fight. Agnikul could take on the rocket industry because metal printing arrived: old rocketry science, new manufacturing, a combination unavailable forty years ago. “There is no catapult, there is no way to win the game.” His corollary keeps the order straight. Printing a rocket is science; producing rockets faster is economics, and economics is the point. For a country, the question is the same as for a start-up: what has just arrived that lets the small fight the large?

The lesson of Apple’s finishing line and Relativity’s pivot still holds. A printer changes what can be drawn. It does not change what must be measured. The Indian printing companies worth watching will be the ones that also own the finishing, the inspection and the qualification file.

Founded upon a rock

“Weapons do not cut it, fire does not burn it, water does not wet it, and the wind does not dry it. It is eternal, present everywhere, firm, unmoving, everlasting.”
— Bhagavad Gita, 2.23–24

India’s industrial future, as Anuj Gupta puts it, depends on the boring stuff. Technical education, supplier networks, ports, reliable electricity, predictable regulation, research partnerships and steady labour relations decide whether factories survive long after the ribbon is cut. India, still treats industrialisation as an event rather than a process. His shortest line could caption this whole essay: “Factories can be imported. Industrial strength cannot.” He offers West Bengal as the warning. Once among India’s industrial leaders, it watched capabilities built over generations erode through adversarial labour relations and institutional decline, the same technical amnesia Ken Alder found in revolutionary France.

Wang and Kroeber, writing together, make the same argument about China itself. The real China model, they argue, is not the list of ten sectors in Made in China 2025. It is the way infrastructure, energy and innovation combine and reinforce one another, and their advice to rivals is to think in ecosystems and build the infrastructure first, energy above all. That is Gupta’s boring stuff, seen from Beijing.

The boring stuff is now being mapped directly. One curated collection is called Industrial Staples: the machine tools, bearings, pumps, valves, motors, gearboxes, fasteners, cutting tools and bulk chemicals that every factory quietly runs on, and that India still substantially imports. Others cover the rare-earth chain (where the site notes that the prize is not the mine but every step after it), a sovereign-chemistry list of molecules India must be able to make for itself, and solar’s seven policy levers. That last one carries a startling number: India’s budget put about $5 million into research across all renewable technologies combined, while America spent $132 million on solar photovoltaics alone.

The “nobody’s building” list reads like the inventory of this essay: precision metrology and calibration, precision motion control, CNC machine-tool controllers, bearing-grade alloy steel, high-resolution encoders, power electronics, precision balls ground to under five microns, and the grinding machines that cut threads and bearing races. Each holds up several products. None has an Indian builder.

Take the precision ball-screw linear actuator, a cousin of the roller screws inside a humanoid’s joints. Seven Indian builders are attempting it. To make it, India needs 24 underlying capabilities, and IndiaBUILD rates 9 of them missing or barely emerging, 10 demonstrated or licensed, and only 5 in hand. The missing ones include high-strength bearing steel, balls sorted to under five microns, and the grinding machines that cut threads and races. The licensed ones include the CNC controller and the servo motors.

This is where the essay’s threads knot together. Apple can bring a factory to India. It cannot bring bearing steel, a sub-five-micron ball, a calibration lab or a controller that has been tuned on real machines for thirty years. Those are Gupta’s boring stuff, Liebig’s short staves and Polanyi’s tacit knowledge, all at once. Capabilities scale across sectors, Gupta notes, while factories serve only one. The same bearing steel goes into a machine tool, a robot joint, a wind turbine and an aircraft. The country that builds it once collects on it everywhere. The market is, for now, pricing the foundation as if it were optional. It rarely stays that way. The house stands or falls on what nobody photographs.

Catch and control

“If you wish for industrialisation, prepare to develop agriculture.”

— Michael Lipton

Joe Studwell spent years asking why a handful of East Asian economies became rich while their south-east Asian neighbours, often richer at the start, stalled. His answer in How Asia Works is a recipe in three steps, and the order matters. First, land: break up big estates into small family farms that are gardened intensively, which raises yields, puts money in rural pockets and creates a home market. Second, manufacturing, pushed hard towards exports, with every subsidy tied to performance abroad. Third, finance kept on a short leash and aimed at the first two steps, not at shopping malls and property.

Step two is where Apple sits, and how Korea ran it is the sharpest guide to what India is now attempting. Park Chung Hee, the general who ran Korea from 1961, did not trust businessmen and did not need to. He fed them cheap credit, licences and protection, then demanded exports in return. Firms reported their export numbers monthly, and the credit followed the numbers. Studwell’s summary is the one-line correction to every debate about industrial policy: the state “did not so much pick winners as weed out losers.” Subsidies were the bait. Exports were the hook. In his phrase, the successful developing state “catches and controls its entrepreneurs.”

Hyundai’s car business shows what that discipline produced. It began in 1968 bolting together Ford Cortinas from kits. When Ford refused further technology without an equity stake, the Chung family went shopping instead: an engine and transmission licence from Mitsubishi, Japan’s weakest car maker and so the only one willing to teach; a former boss of British Leyland to run the plant; six European engineers for design and testing. Hyundai produced its first truly in-house engine, the Alpha, in 1991, twenty-four years after the company was founded. Steel followed the same pattern. POSCO took Japanese engineering advice, had it checked by an Australian miner, then had both reviewed again. In Studwell’s words, it “listened to everyone, and trusted no one.”

Malaysia is the control experiment. Mahathir Mohamad admired Korea openly and copied its forms: a national car, Proton; a national steel plant, Perwaja. Proton’s Waja, which went into production in 2001 and which Studwell calls the first real Malaysian car, was respectable. Yet it marks a peak Malaysia never climbed past, because the discipline was missing. Proton was sheltered at home and never forced to win abroad. Well-connected tycoons grew rich on property, gaming and government concessions. When Mahathir handed what was then Malaysia’s biggest construction contract to Japanese firms that bid higher than local ones, the taxpayer paid, “but Malaysians learned nothing.” By 2003 Malaysia’s income per head was $4,160, against Korea’s $12,680, and the threefold gap has held.

Hosting a factory is not the same as learning from it.

Read India against that ledger and the iPhone looks different. Apple’s value to India is not prestige. It is the export benchmark Studwell says every catching-up state needs, because, like a properly supervised exam, “exports cannot in the long run be fiddled.” An Indian component maker that wins Apple business in Chennai has passed a test no ministry could set. The obvious use of PLI follows: pay on verified exports and verified local value added, and stop paying the firms that cannot pass.

Studwell wrote his India verdict in 2013, and it still stings. Two decades after the 1991 reforms, only about 3 million Indians worked in IT, a sliver of the labour force. Only 14% of workers were in manufacturing; Korea had drawn 30% into industry after twenty years of trying. Software made India famous. It was never going to employ India.

There is also a lever Japan used that India has barely touched. In the late 1950s an official at MITI, Japan’s trade ministry, told IBM it would be shut out of Japan unless it licensed its technology to local firms at a royalty of no more than 5%. IBM agreed, because the market was too good to lose. Between 1950 and 1963 Japan approved 2,487 contracts for foreign technology, each one screened for what it would teach. The Japanese scholar Masayuki Kondo, studying why Malaysia’s heavy spending on universities produced so little, put it plainly: “The main context for industrial technology development is firms, not public institutions.” India now has the bargaining position Japan had then: a market no hardware company can ignore. The question is whether it trades access for know-how, deal by deal, as Japan did.

And step one remains India’s missing foundation. Studwell traces Japan, Korea, Taiwan and China back to land reform and intensive small farms. Taiwan in 1960 had 79 agricultural extension workers per 100,000 people; India had 1.2. Rich farmers buy bicycles, radios, and later phones, and send healthy children to factories. India’s weakest farm belts still send their young people hundreds of miles south to assemble the phones, and a poor countryside buys few of them. Apple can teach India’s factories. It cannot fix India’s fields.

Walking upstream

“It is not because things are difficult that we do not dare; it is because we do not dare that they are difficult.”

— Seneca, Letters to Lucilius

Start with where India already stands, because it is further along than the headlines suggest. It is the world’s second-largest maker of steel, cement, mobile phones, trucks and buses. Usually it is a distant second, making between a sixth and a third of what China makes; buses are the exception, where India nearly matches China.

The iPhone is where that distance is closing fastest. India’s share of global iPhone output went at under 1% in 2017, to about 7% in 2022 and around a quarter in 2026, with 30–35% in sight by 2030. In 2025 India passed China as the largest exporter of smartphones to the United States, and smartphones became India’s single largest export product.

Hirschman also gave this climb its classic shape in 1958. Development, he argued in The Strategy of Economic Development, spreads through linkages. An industry pulls its suppliers into being through backward linkages, and its customers through forward ones. Seen this way, an iPhone assembled in India matters less for assembly’s roughly 3% share of the phone’s cost than for the purchase orders it creates. Every phone put together in Chennai is a standing order for an enclosure, a circuit board, a camera module and, behind them, the machines and materials that make those. Assembly is not the destination. It is the demand signal.

Jaishankar’s phrase for the same idea is plainer: “We need to stop looking for a China fix. Indian growth cannot be built on Chinese efficiency.” A serious manufacturing economy, he argued, has to create “a kind of domestic vendor chain.” That is Hirschman’s backward linkage in a minister’s words. For now, the iPhones assembled in Chennai and Bengaluru still run largely on Chinese efficiency: Chinese parts, Chinese machines and, until Beijing called them home, Chinese engineers.

Demand is the other half of the chain. India cannot leapfrog manufacturing by decree, only by demand. Its electronics policy now has three levers: production incentives for finished goods, the component schemes for parts, and the Design Linked Incentive for chips. Each alone is a subsidy. Together they can be a flywheel, if system companies come first. Build the makers of CCTV cameras, smart meters and cars, let them pull components into existence, and subsidies become training wheels rather than crutches. Chips follow the same logic. A foundry demands billions in concrete and silicon; design is pure leverage, with no factory and gross margins often near 60%. India already employs tens of thousands of chip designers, mostly working for foreign firms. The prize is pointing more of them at Indian product companies. China’s lesson is that scale is the ultimate subsidy and domestic demand the ultimate design win. A country cannot distribute what it does not first produce, and cannot produce what it does not first demand. Industrial ecosystems, not individual technologies, are the unit of competition.

But assembly and making are different things, and the gap between them is the whole story. About 99% of the phones sold in India are now assembled in India. Yet only about a fifth of the value inside a phone’s bill of materials is made here. The first mobile incentive scheme, launched in 2020, hoped to reach about 25% value addition and fell short. A government review in 2024 listed the reasons: components need heavy upfront capital, the machines turn over slowly, plants take one to two years to start producing, and technology transfer was limited. That last reason is Polanyi’s in all but name.

Estimates of value added vary with the method: some put phones at 22–23%, with an industry goal of 35–40%. Every count agrees on the direction of the gap. So does the trade ledger. In 2025–26 India bought about $131.6 billion of goods from China and sold it about $19.5 billion. Much of that deficit is components for the phones India assembles. It is the tuition invoice, paid in the open.

The new bet is the electronics components scheme, launched in 2025. It was oversubscribed so heavily that the government raised its budget by 75%, to ₹40,000 crore. Applicants pledged about ₹1.15 lakh crore of investment, roughly double the target; by August 2026, 106 of the 249 applications had been approved. The list reads like a map of the iPhone’s missing middle: phone enclosures from Foxconn’s Yuzhan, Tata Electronics and Motherson; lithium-ion cells from TDK’s ATL; copper-clad laminates from Wipro; multi-layer and HDI circuit boards, where India imports 85–90% of what it uses.

Look closely at the partnerships and you see Hyundai’s method. Dixon is making display modules with China’s HKC and camera modules through a stake in QTech’s Indian arm. Syrma is learning HDI circuit boards from Korea’s Shinhyup. The teachers are Chinese, Korean and Japanese; the student intends to keep the lessons. Whether it does will depend on whether those plants are built to export or only to fill a quota at home.

Solar shows the other way to climb. In 2021–22 Chinese modules made up about three-quarters of India’s installations. A production incentive in 2021 achieved little, because Indian firms did the easiest thing: they imported Chinese cells and assembled modules, the least capital-hungry step. So the government changed tools. From 2024 only approved domestic modules could be used across most of the market; from 2026, domestic cells; from 2028, domestic wafers and ingots. The rule walks upstream one step at a time, and roughly 90% of the chain should be local by 2030. India now has the world’s second-largest cell capacity, and domestic-content modules sell at about a 50% premium.

Michael Porter’s line, “the essence of strategy is choosing what not to do,” applies to industrial policy too. Solar chose a protected home market, which Studwell would recognise as the Malaysian route unless the cells and wafers it creates go on to win abroad. Phones chose export markets, the Korean route, with Apple as the examiner. Indian solar buyers are paying the tuition through that 50% premium. Apple’s customers in America and Europe are paying it for the phones.

The same logic is now reaching chips. India’s first semiconductor incentive attracted about $20 billion of investment, including Tata Electronics’ $11 billion fab for mature 28–110 nanometre chips and assembly-and-test plants from Micron, Kaynes and CG Power that are already producing. The second phase, worth about $13 billion and launched in 2026, pays for something new: semiconductor equipment, materials, speciality chemicals and gases. For the first time, the state is paying for the mother machines and the inputs that sit beneath them. India already holds roughly a fifth of the world’s chip-design workforce. The missing middle of the iPhone and the missing base of the chip are, in the end, the same gap.

The frontier shows how far that staircase now runs. The lithography machines that print today’s most advanced chips are made by one Dutch company, ASML. In Winchester’s account, each 2018-era machine arrived in pieces aboard three Boeing 747 freighters, weighed about 160 tonnes assembled and cost about $100 million. Gordon Moore himself said his famous law served less to describe the chip industry than to drive it. Apple’s September launch works the same way for its suppliers: the calendar is the master gauge. India’s first fab will work at 28–110 nanometres, several steps up the staircase from the frontier. Every country that reached the frontier started on those steps.

Competition, not shelter

“The indispensable feature of a market economy is not private property but competition.”

— Arthur Kroeber, China’s Economy

Kroeber’s most useful warning for India is tucked inside his discussion of innovation. Chinese policy, he observes, kept confusing innovation with autonomy. The Chinese term for indigenous innovation can just as fairly be translated as autonomous innovation, and a country can be self-reliant by making just-good-enough copies of foreign goods. Protected firms learn to win the home market and stop there.

India’s production-linked incentives face the same fork. A subsidy that rewards local output is easy to earn with local assembly. It is only working if the firms it backs can later sell to demanding foreign buyers without it. Jack Welch put the corporate version bluntly: “Control your own destiny or someone else will.” For a supplier, durable control comes from being better, not from being sheltered.

Potter adds a caution about subsidies and learning. A subsidy can push an industry down its learning curve only where the process can actually improve; no amount of volume made titanium cheap. And instability kills learning outright. America’s nuclear plants grew more expensive through the 1970s and 1980s because rules kept changing mid-construction, a textbook case of negative learning. The US Navy now schedules submarine building to keep its yards continuously busy for exactly this reason. For India, the cheapest industrial policy may simply be a stable one.

Chang would object to any reading of this as a case for dropping protection. Measured against the productivity gap, today’s developing countries protect far less than the rich countries once did. India’s average tariff fell from about 71% to 32% after the WTO, at roughly a fifteenth of American income; in Chang’s figures, America’s never fell below 32% between the Civil War and the Second World War, at a much smaller gap. So the real question is not protection or no protection. Look at Robert Walpole’s 1721 reform, the template Chang traces through Hamilton to post-war Japan, Korea and Taiwan: cheap imported inputs, refunds of duty for exporters, protected outputs, subsidies for exports. It protected in order to export. That is the same line Kroeber draws between autonomy and innovation, and it is the test for India’s PLI. Shelter that ends at the border is a crutch. Shelter that pays for the climb into export markets is a ladder.

Jaishankar makes the case in the language of strategy. “In the name of opening the economy and globalizing,” he warned, “we should not end up de-industrializing this country.” Letting foreign rivals carry their home subsidies onto a level playing field in India would, in his words, be “economic suicide”; every country must support its manufacturers. Chang would agree, and so would Walpole. The East Asian record adds one condition, the one that separated Korea from Malaysia: support has to be earned at the border.

Studwell adds a reason that rarely appears in industrial-policy debates: exports keep the financial system honest. Korea borrowed heavily through the 1970s and early 1980s; by 1985 its foreign loans equalled 53% of national income, yet debt service fell as a share of export earnings, from 28% in 1970 to about 20% in the early 1980s. In his phrase, export discipline was Korea’s financial “get-out-of-jail card.” A country that borrows to build export factories can repay. A country that borrows to build apartments has to hope.

India’s third step looks healthier than it did. Bad loans at Indian banks peaked near 11% in 2018 and fell to about 1.8% by March 2026, the lowest in decades. That is a repaired financial system, as China had after it moved bad loans off its banks around 2000. But one trend would worry Studwell: household borrowing now outruns corporate borrowing. Credit that buys phones and flats teaches nothing. Credit that buys machines does.

Russell Napier, expects the whole Western system to relearn Studwell’s lesson. The developed world, he argues, underinvested in fixed assets in the belief that China’s investment boom “would always provide the tangible products that are still the bedrock of any economic system.” His forecast is a new monetary order built around a non-Chinese bloc, with limits on the free movement of capital and banks steered to lend for factories rather than for financial engineering: credit “directed to fund fixed-asset investment and the creation of new income streams, rather than the leveraging of existing income streams.” For an investor, that is a map of who wins. Large companies built for the old order lose. Those at the front of a capital-spending boom that “could last a decade or more” gain. India’s machine builders would be among them.

Kroeber’s second lesson is about who competes. Charlie Munger’s rule applies: “Show me the incentive and I’ll show you the outcome.” China’s engine was not only Beijing. Local governments carried most of the spending, were judged on growth, and paid for roads and power by selling land. They raced each other for factories with tax breaks, bonded zones and labour supply.

Kroeber describes India as China’s inverse: decentralised on paper, centralised in practice, which is one reason it never ran a Shenzhen-style experiment. That is changing. Tamil Nadu, Karnataka and Uttar Pradesh now compete openly for electronics clusters, and that rivalry is the right engine. China’s mistakes are worth copying out too. Land-financed building left a mountain of local debt, and migrants housed in dormitories without their families stayed a few years and went home. In Kroeber’s phrase, China urbanised jobs far better than it urbanised people. A state that does both keeps its skills.

Babies and dwarfs

“Little else is requisite to carry a state to the highest degree of opulence from the lowest barbarism, but peace, easy taxes, and a tolerable administration of justice.”

— Adam Smith

Manish Sabharwal, who built TeamLease into one of India’s largest employers, has spent twenty-five years on a question that sounds like a riddle: why are some small firms babies and others dwarfs? Both are small. Babies grow. Dwarfs stay small. India, in his telling, has a great many dwarfs. Only about 38,000 companies sit outside the government’s definition of a micro, small or medium enterprise. No Indian factory employs more than about 75,000 people at one site; China’s largest have employed several times that. Economists call the gap the missing middle: the firms that should be growing from ten workers to fifty to five hundred, and are not.

His diagnosis has a memorable name: regulatory cholesterol. It is the slow build-up of rules that narrows the arteries through which firms grow. A study he championed counted about 26,000 provisions under which an Indian employer could be jailed; before the new labour codes, one section of the Factories Act alone generated some 700 of them. An employer juggles 23 separate government identification numbers. Below the laws sit circulars, guidelines, notifications and FAQs. In his phrase, edicts have become 25 times rules, and rules 25 times acts. For a large firm this is a thorn in the flesh: TeamLease alone keeps well over a hundred people on regulatory affairs. For a small one it is a dagger in the heart, because, as he puts it, for small firms the rule becomes “show me the person, I’ll show you the rule.”

Wang has a frame for why this happens. China, he argues, is an engineering state: its leaders were trained as engineers and treat bridges, factories and even society as things to be built. America is a lawyerly society, good at protecting individuals from the state and bad at building anything quickly. India inherited the lawyer’s state from Britain and produces engineers by the million. It is a lawyerly country with an engineering population. The cholesterol is what that mismatch looks like from a factory floor.

Here the Apple story meets a harder truth. Foxconn and Tata are giants; they can afford compliance departments, and India rolls out a red carpet for them. But Hirschman’s backward linkages run through small firms. The enclosure maker’s tool room, the plating shop, the fixture builder and the calibration lab are mostly dwarfs today. If cholesterol keeps them small, the demand signal sent upstream by every iPhone assembled in Chennai arrives at firms that cannot grow to answer it. Mass prosperity, Sabharwal argues, will not come from a few giants. It will come from millions of small firms becoming babies.

His numbers on the iPhone make the stakes concrete. In roughly two and a half years, by his estimate, the value India adds to each phone has risen from about $2.50 to about $12, as India’s share of global iPhone output climbed to about a quarter. China’s is about $40. The road from $12 to $40 is not more assembly. It is the supply chain moving, which means Indian dwarfs becoming babies.

He turns one assumption upside down. Many people believe skills create jobs. Sabharwal argues that jobs create skills. China, he points out, never ran a massive national skilling programme; it ran factories, and the factories did the teaching. Foxconn hired a couple of hundred thousand people in India without complaining of a skills shortage. That is the tuition loop seen from the worker’s side, and it explains why India’s skilling schemes have disappointed: the classroom was never the main place where industrial skill was learned.

He also offers a sharper way to think about poverty. There are no poor people, he says, only people in poor places, and six places decide a person’s income. Three are physical: the country, the state and the city. Three are conceptual: the sector, the firm and the skill. Seen through that lens, an iPhone job is a change of address on almost every count. A young woman who leaves a farm in one state for an assembly line near Chennai changes her state, her city, her sector, her firm and her skill at once. India still has about 45% of its workers on farms producing about 14% of its output, which is why he says the only way to help farmers is to have fewer farmers. It is Lipton’s and Studwell’s first step, seen from the other end.

Where the change happens matters too. China’s genius, Sabharwal says, was not one ruler in Beijing but some two hundred mayors competing for investment. In India, as he puts it, 29 chief ministers matter more than one prime minister, and a hundred mayors would matter more than 29 chief ministers. That is why Apple’s Indian supply chain has gathered in Tamil Nadu and Karnataka, states that compete for factories, and why Kroeber’s chart of who spends the money belongs in any account of Apple in India. Sabharwal’s definition of development is the sharpest sentence in this section: it is the move from deals to rules. In India, he jokes, if you follow a rule you feel you have missed a deal.

He also offers the most uncomfortable comparison for an investor. Over the past twenty-five years, by his reckoning, China’s stock market went roughly nowhere while India’s returned about 1,300%. Yet China moved some 300 million people off its farms, and India did not. In his phrase, autocracy delivered wage growth and democracy delivered shareholder growth. Markets grade only what they can see. India’s listed companies have passed. Its workers are still sitting the paper.

His answer is patient, and it echoes the long game. In Kashmir, where he was born, there are two words for property. A jagir is yours to do with as you like. An amanat is held in trust, and must be handed on in better condition than you received it. Jonas Salk asked the same question in a different language: are we being good ancestors? Every rule removed, Sabharwal says, is not a law you are changing but an entrepreneur you are unleashing. Its full effect will arrive long after the reformer has gone. In his words, we will never see the end of the sentences that we start.

Prepare for war

“Let him who desires peace prepare for war.”

— Vegetius, De Re Militari, c. 400 AD

The next decade will change the price of the thing that is treated as scarce. As robotics and AI fuse, a J-curve of automation is coming: from chores and labour-intensive assembly to complex industrial processes and precision engineering. For a century, the hardest part of competing in goods trade was training and managing people, which is why manufacturing clustered where a factory culture already existed. Robots that learn quickly from humans and from each other bypass that old bottleneck. Unit costs and barriers to entry fall together. The likely result is manufacturing that is more democratic and more local, displaced labour, rising productivity and falling prices. Laksh Vaaman Sehgal described the same shift from Motherson’s side: with automation, he said, the labour arbitrage “really becomes really minuscule.”

The bottleneck does not disappear. It moves: to robotics intellectual property, energy, natural resources and the capacity to process raw materials. Margins will sit with whoever controls those knowledge and resource chokepoints, which is why the actuators, controllers and magnets matter more, not less, in an automated world. China and America hold the strongest hands. India has two cards of its own: a young, large consumer market and a deep pool of engineering talent.

For India this is a single shot at industrial leapfrogging: an economy in which domestic production serves domestic consumption at scale. The outcome is unusually binary. If India owns efficient production systems and the resources behind them, it can build the most dynamic production-and-consumption economy of the century. If it does not, it stays inefficient with low purchasing power, or is swamped by goods made abroad, and industrialisation never happens at the scale it needs. That road ends in the middle-income trap, with the cost of redistribution rising and the ability to pay for it falling. The era of low-risk, slowly compounding growth is ending.

Without fighting a war, India is absorbing a supply-chain shock of the kind wars bring: engineers recalled, equipment slowed, magnets licensed. One response is to build as a war economy would, and the list writes itself. Strategic reserves of oil, gas and their critical derivatives. Stockpiles of industrial and critical minerals. More mining, refining and mineral processing. Capital goods: machine tools and industrial robots. Defence manufacturing at scale. Strategic infrastructure. And computing capacity for AI. The building itself pays dividends: growth, a workforce trained on hard projects, a state that learns to deliver at scale, greater security and more leverage at the negotiating table. India starts from a real base. Its defence production reached a record ₹1,50,590 crore in 2024–25, nearly double the level of five years earlier, with private firms growing fastest, and defence exports reached a record ₹23,622 crore.

History supports the logic that technology flows downstream from defence. Blanc’s interchangeable locks were made for the French army, American mass production was born in the federal armories at Springfield and Harpers Ferry, and precision only later reached sewing machines, bicycles and cars. The jet engine, GPS, the early internet and the first big market for integrated circuits all came out of military budgets. India went through the rearmaments before both world wars as a colony, supplying Britain’s needs rather than building an industrial base of its own, and through the Cold War it built slowly, without the industrial depth that made those cycles pay elsewhere. Andy Grove’s title for a different era fits the next one: only the paranoid survive.

The same history carries a warning. The Soviet Union built one of the largest military-industrial complexes of the twentieth century and starved the civilian economy that was supposed to pay for it. Defence spending compounds only under the conditions that make any industrial policy work: competition among suppliers, exports that are graded by foreign buyers, and dual-use technology that spills into civilian supply chains. The test for a defence build-out is the test this essay set for the iPhone. Does it make Indian suppliers better, or only busier? Build the capability, not just the arsenal.

The exam

The world is the curriculum. Markets are the exam. And this exam will not be graded on iPhone export headlines.

Kroeber’s numbers dispose of one comforting myth first: that cheap labour is the moat. Chinese factory pay rose from about $500 a year in 1994 to about $3,500 in 2008, and real wages doubled again by 2017. China kept gaining share anyway, because output per worker and capital per worker rose faster. He also names India among the countries that could not keep investment growing fast enough to graduate, while China’s investment rate climbed from 28% to 45% of GDP. Machines are capital. The question is whether India invests enough to put more of them behind each worker.

Kroeber borrows a McKinsey study of 81 value chains that names four requirements for moving up: a large market, access to know-how, investment at scale, and real competition. Those are the four questions on India’s paper.

Competition is the test that matters most, because it turns certification into spillover. China compounded because Apple-trained people moved to Huawei, Xiaomi and EV lines. The Indian supplier worth backing is the one whose Apple certification shows up in its non-Apple customers.

Foxconn is the warning. A supplier can win an enormous share of Apple’s volume and still watch its margins shrink. Assembly alone will not make India rich. The value sits in what gets rebuilt every year (tooling, test and process know-how) and in selling that capability to everyone else.

Studwell supplies the number behind that warning. In 2009 Hon Hai, Foxconn’s parent, earned a net margin of 3.5%; Samsung, which designs and brands its own products, earned 7%. Taiwan built the factories that make the world’s gadgets and remained one rung below the companies that own them. South-east Asia offers the harsher lesson. Malaysia, Thailand and the Philippines hosted foreign electronics plants for decades, and their export numbers looked superb. But the know-how stayed inside the foreign firms. Studwell calls the Philippines, once among Asia’s richest countries, an “authentic, technology-less” Third World state.

Markets have already started marking the paper. Some of India’s listed component and contract-manufacturing firms trade at roughly 50 to 70 times this year’s expected earnings. Those prices assume the middle bar in Figure 65 climbs towards the bottom one, that India’s firms grow teeth. If they stay assemblers, Hon Hai’s 3.5% margin is the more likely destination, and no multiple survives that.

Yet markets misprice the other end too. The most consequential capabilities are always underpriced while they are being built, because markets price current earnings, not process knowledge that will not show up in accounts for a decade. Firms climbing the complexity gradient are often cash-hungry, low-return and over-invested. Assemblers get rich multiples for volume; builders of teeth get doubted for patience. The patience requirement is the moat.

Vishesh Rajaram has spent nine years pricing exactly this kind of patience, and his rules translate directly. Capital and time are the only two axes, and the one question is whether the risk in a business falls as both are spent. Work backwards from the earnings a company must make in ten or fifteen years, not forwards from the idea. In deep tech, he agrees, you make money by killing what can go wrong: each retired technical risk is why the next round prices higher, because the buyer is purchasing a de-risked profile, not a bigger dream. Returns live in the gap between what is true and what has been verified. And in India, he says, the seed problem is solved; the Series A problem is not. That is the gap where a single cited investor among 113 machine-tool builders stops looking like an oversight and starts looking like an opportunity.

The production process is often worth more than the product. Ford’s Model T was not a radical car. What mattered was the moving assembly line, which after 1913 cut the time to build a chassis from about twelve and a half hours to about an hour and a half, and let the price fall from $850 to under $300. Smartphones existed before the iPhone; what let Apple ship one at high quality and enormous volume was its manufacturing precision and its grip on the supply chain. Investors spend their effort on brands, network effects and switching costs. Those are real moats. They tend to ignore the moat that comes from simply being better at making things. A firm that has mastered production can underprice rivals and keep its margins, fund the next round of improvements while competitors are still catching up, and accumulate organisational knowledge that cannot be bought. Read that way, the learning curve is not just a cost forecast. It is a map of competitive position. The best investments are often the firms furthest down the curve with the most runway left, even when their products look commoditised. And unlike most advantages, this one tends to strengthen with time rather than erode.

The Industrial Revolution ran this sorting for the first time. Before machines, scale was arithmetic: one more unit needed one more pair of hands. Afterwards, fixed costs could be spread across ever larger volumes, and scale became geometric. Industries with complex processes and heavy fixed investment (steel, railways and chemicals) consolidated into a few winners whose cost advantages compounded. Simple industries with trivial fixed costs, the restaurant or the jobbing workshop, stayed fragmented, their profits competed away. The same structure carries its own risk. Fixed costs that create dominance also create rigidity, and when a new process arrives the incumbent must cannibalise its plant or watch it become obsolete. India’s machine-shop base sits on the wrong side of this divide today, in shops of a few machines each. Ethereal’s fleet is an attempt to cross it.

Deming, whose quality methods Japan turned into a national advantage, had a line for every supplier waiting to be chosen: “It is not necessary to change. Survival is not mandatory.”

Chang closes his book with an old Chinese saying about losing larger, longer-term gains by chasing smaller, short-term ones. It describes Apple’s two decades in China better than any business book.

Potter ends on agency: no gain in efficiency is inevitable; each is the product of deliberate effort by thousands of workers, engineers and managers redesigning products and rearranging factories.

Studwell’s conclusion holds his most provocative line: “Poor states can only be successful by lying.” They must praise free markets in public while running the disciplined interventions that actually make countries rich. Chang would call that kicking away the ladder seen from below. India, with its talk of open markets and its PLI cheques, is already fluent in both languages. The exam will reveal which one it means.

Not every careful observer believes India will sit the exam at all. Asked which country he would write about next, Wang said he did not “quite yet believe that India and Indonesia are ready to challenge these two bigger powers.” He also offered a glib forecast for artificial intelligence: “maybe America gets a much better McKinsey, and China gets a much better Foxconn.” India’s risk is to win the McKinsey half of that bet again, as it won the offices of the last globalisation. The exam asks whether it can also build a better Foxconn, and the machines inside it.

Vishesh’s answer to the McKinsey risk is to run a different race. Arriving second in someone else’s race for general intelligence is a plan to finish second. India’s opening is small models that are excellent in one domain (aerospace, defence, automotive, drug discovery) and trained on what happens on its own factory floors. “Why do I need general intelligence? I am the general intelligence. I need specific intelligence.”

Winchester closes his book in Beijing. Outside the front door of China’s new national metrology laboratory stands a gift from Britain’s National Physical Laboratory: a sapling grown from Isaac Newton’s apple tree. It is a fitting emblem for a country that took its precision education from Apple. India now has the phones. It has yet to plant the tree.

List would recognise the stakes. Apple’s first education took twenty years and built an industrial superpower Apple now cannot leave. The second is moving faster, under pressure, in a democracy both sides need.

Which leaves the question worth sitting with: this time, who is the student, and who ends up teaching whom?
