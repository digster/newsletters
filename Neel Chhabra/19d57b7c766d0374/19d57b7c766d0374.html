The Long Game: On Building Conviction in Things That Take Decades to Be Obviously True

Neel Chhabra
Apr 4
 
READ IN APP
 

The most important thing Bill Knudsen ever did took place not in a factory but in a phone call.

It was early 1922. Knudsen had just resigned from Ford, walked away from a $50,000 annual salary and a 21 percent bonus, the most important production job in American industry, because Henry Ford had started undermining his directives and he refused to work in an organization that didn’t trust him. He had almost no savings. His wife knew their situation was precarious. She hugged him and said: “Now we can have some peace around this place.”

Alfred Sloan, the new executive vice president of General Motors, picked up the phone a few days later. He explained that he had no particular job in mind for Knudsen. He just needed someone who could help the operating units do a better job. “If you would like that job, we would like to have you here.”

“All right,” Knudsen said.

“How much shall we pay you, Mr. Knudsen?”

“Anything you like. I’m not here to set a figure.”

Sloan started him at six thousand dollars a year, twelve percent of what he had been making at Ford.

Knudsen didn’t care. It wasn’t where he started, but where he’d finish, that mattered.

Twenty years later, that same man would stand in the Oval Office as a lieutenant general, the only civilian in American history ever to hold that rank, overseeing the production of two-thirds of all Allied military equipment in the largest war in human history. 86,000 tanks. 286,000 warplanes. 2.5 million trucks. The arsenal that won the war and built the postwar world, assembled from almost nothing in five years, because one man with thirty years of embodied manufacturing knowledge agreed to work for a fraction of his market rate while the world caught up to what he already understood.

I have been thinking about this story for months. Not because it is inspiring, though it is, but because of the specific thing it illustrates about the relationship between time, knowledge, and value that I believe is the central investing insight of this moment in India.

The most consequential capabilities are always underpriced while they are being built, because the market prices current earnings, not the compounding value of accumulated process knowledge that won’t appear in a financial statement for a decade. Knudsen at six thousand dollars a year in 1922 was not six thousand dollars of value. He was thirty years of manufacturing knowledge that would become the arsenal of democracy. The market, in this case, Alfred Sloan, was buying a decade of future compounding for a fraction of its eventual value, because no financial model could capture what Knudsen actually was.

This is the lens through which I have been looking at India’s industrial moment.

Part One: The Forcing Function

Every great industrial nation was forged under existential pressure. Not built, forged. The distinction is not semantic.

Building implies a plan, a blueprint, a rational allocation of resources toward a predetermined outcome. Forging implies heat. Pressure. The transformation of raw material into something harder and more capable through the application of force that the material would never have sought voluntarily.

The United States was forged by two world wars. Britain by centuries of naval rivalry and colonial competition. Germany rebuilt from rubble into Europe’s industrial heart through rearmament. Japan converted wartime organizational discipline into postwar manufacturing supremacy through a process so systematic that it eventually overwhelmed the industries of the country that had defeated it. In every case, the mechanism was the same: existential necessity created the urgency, the urgency forced capability development that peacetime incentives could never have produced, and the capability, once built, compounded into something far larger than the specific emergency that created it.

India has missed every such forcing function in the modern era. Not through failure but through colonial arithmetic: the first two cycles found India supplying raw materials for someone else’s forge. The Cold War cycle found India attempting its own forge — DRDO, HAL, BEL, the nuclear and space programs, but never quite reaching escape velocity, always constrained by the dual pressure of democratic accountability on long-horizon spending and the chronic temptation of cheaper imports over the harder work of domestic capability building.

What changed? Three things, arriving simultaneously, with a fourth accelerant that most analysts are still processing.

The first: China. Not the economic China that was a partner, that China is gone. The strategic China that has matched or exceeded Indian capabilities in almost every critical military-industrial dimension while the Indian MIC stalled. The two-front threat this creates, a nuclear-armed adversary with a military-industrial base an order of magnitude larger, combined with a nuclear-armed western neighbor, is the most concentrated forcing function in India’s independent history.

The second: the global aerospace supply chain rupture. Spirit AeroSystems rescued by Boeing. Latécoère bleeding money, closing European facilities. Liberty Steel’s aerospace forging division idled because UK energy prices made the furnaces uneconomical. GKN closing plants. The median US aerospace manufacturing worker over fifty years old, with more than a third of skilled machinists approaching retirement and no replacement generation in the pipeline. This is not a cyclical disruption. It is a structural collapse of Western aerospace manufacturing capacity, driven by demographics, energy economics, and forty years of optimization that stripped out every redundancy in the name of efficiency. India, with its automotive manufacturing base, its engineering talent pool, its cost structure, and crucially its rapidly expanding position as one of the world’s largest aircraft buyers, is the only viable geography to absorb the displaced work.

The third: the drone revolution. A $500 commercial FPV drone destroyed a $3 million tank on a Ukrainian battlefield. Operation Spider’s Web launched 117 drones from cargo trucks and caused $7 billion in damage at a total cost of $234,000. Operation Sindoor made this visible and personal, those videos of objects bursting in Indian skies, intercepted by L70 guns firing proximity-fused ammunition, were a real-time advertisement for an entire counter-drone ecosystem broadcast to every defense ministry in the country simultaneously. The cost-exchange ratio of warfare inverted. The entire calculus of what a military needs to procure, and therefore what a defense-industrial complex needs to build, shifted in the span of three years.

The fourth accelerant is the one that connects them all: the dual-use logic that every previous military-industrial compounding cycle has expressed but that no one has adequately named in the Indian context.

The precision machining capability required to produce an aero-engine hot-section turbine blade — titanium alloys, nickel superalloys, five-axis CNC to tolerances of ±0.002mm, thirty-five times thinner than a human hair, is not separate from the capability required to produce UAV propulsion components. The materials science required to cast single-crystal turbine blades for commercial jet engines does not have a clean boundary excluding military jet engines or loitering munition warheads. The quality systems built to achieve aerospace certification from GE or Pratt & Whitney are the same quality systems that make a company credible to Indian defense procurement.

The internet was ARPANET before it was commerce. GPS was a targeting system before it was navigation. The jet engine powered bombers before it powered commercial aviation. Industrial capability at genuine depth is general-purpose. The forge doesn’t care what it’s making. And India is now being handed the conditions, simultaneously, to run the same experiment.

This is not a defense sector thesis. It is an industrialization thesis. The defense and commercial aerospace demand vectors are the twin forcing functions. The substrate being built is the general-purpose industrial capability that has historically produced the most durable competitive advantages in existence.

Part Two: The Substrate

The forcing function is necessary but not sufficient. The United States in 1940 did not build the arsenal of democracy from nothing. It converted a pre-existing base of industrial capability — machine tools, trained workforce, supplier networks, process discipline, quality systems, that the American economy had accumulated over forty years of competitive peacetime manufacturing. The wartime conversion was fast because the underlying capability was deep. Roosevelt could call Knudsen because the American economy had already produced him.

India’s equivalent substrate is more advanced than most market participants realize, less advanced than most promoters claim, and developing faster than any linear extrapolation of past trends would suggest.

The automotive manufacturing base is the most transferable foundation. Companies like Sansera Engineering have been precision machining engine and drivetrain components for two decades — developing the five-axis CNC expertise, the statistical process control systems, the materials handling knowledge for titanium and Inconel, and the customer-facing quality discipline that directly translates to aerospace work. Sansera’s Aerospace, Defense, and Semiconductor division grew from 6% to 24% of total order book in a single year. The conversion is not theoretical. It is happening.

The aerospace-specific substrate is more recent but more important. Dynamatic Technologies entered commercial aerospace in 2008 through an acquisition and has since delivered over 13,000 flap-track beam shipsets for the A320 family with a 100% on-time delivery record, earning a direct Tier-1 contract from Airbus for the A330 program and then, in the most significant milestone in Indian commercial aerospace history, a full door-system assembly contract for the A220. Door assemblies require system-level validation, tight tolerance stack-ups across multiple moving parts, and the kind of integration engineering that separates program partners from component vendors. This is not incremental capability. It is a category shift.

Aequs has built the most vertically integrated commercial aerospace operation in India — forging, machining, surface treatment, sub-assembly — with a Saab joint venture that made it the first Indian company to assemble commercial aircraft doors, and an Airbus “Detail Parts Partner” designation that gives it early access to new program sourcing that non-D2P suppliers cannot see.

PTC Industries has acquired, from distressed European sales, the EBCHR furnace capability that would cost $80-90 million to purchase new, building the closed-loop titanium processing capacity that India currently lacks entirely, at a fraction of replacement cost, in a country with abundant titanium reserves.

Azad Engineering is India’s only producer of titanium aerofoils and has been selected as the sole industry partner for end-to-end manufacturing of the ATGG turbojet engine for UAV applications.

Maini Precision Products has earned self-certification status from five global aerospace customers, meaning it makes a new part and places it directly on an aircraft without external quality review, after twenty-one years of zero-defect delivery.

Five self-certification customers. Twenty-one years. Zero defects. This is not a financial metric. It is Knudsen’s kind of credential. It is the accumulated trust that precedes the large contracts, the institutional recognition that a supplier has crossed from vendor to partner, from make-to-print executor to genuine program collaborator.

The substrate exists. It is real, qualified, and, crucially, self-reinforcing. Every program win creates the process experience that enables the next qualification. Every self-certification earns the trust that opens the next program tier. Every delivered shipset in the Tier-3 base builds the credibility that makes a Tier-1 contract conceivable. The compounding is slow and therefore invisible to investors with the wrong time horizon. It is also, for exactly that reason, underpriced.

Part Three: The Value Chain Hierarchy That Determines Everything

Not all positions in this ecosystem are equally valuable. The difference between the most defensible positions and the merely good ones is not a matter of degree. It is a matter of kind.

Understand the pricing structure of the aerospace value chain and you understand the entire investment thesis in a single data series:

Standard industrial castings: ₹1,000-1,200 per kilogram.
Standard aerospace castings: ₹10,000-30,000 per kilogram.
Single-crystal hot-section aerospace castings — the turbine blades that rotate at 15,000 RPM at 1,400 degrees Celsius in the core of a CFM LEAP engine: ₹1 crore per kilogram.

The difference is not the material. The same titanium and nickel alloys underlie all three. The difference is process knowledge, certification depth, and the barrier to replication that comes from operating at the absolute frontier of what materials science and manufacturing technology can currently achieve. Howmet Aerospace generates 30% EBITDA margins on its engine products segment despite manufacturing entirely in high-cost Western facilities. Precision Castparts was acquired by Berkshire Hathaway at a valuation reflecting sixty years of compounded position in the highest-complexity layer of the value chain.

The complexity gradient runs from engine hot-section rotating components at the apex — combustor parts, turbine blades, high-pressure turbine vanes, down through engine cold-section parts, structural components, airframe structures, and finally interiors. The pricing premium compresses at each step down. The certification barrier compresses. The switching cost compresses. The duration of competitive advantage compresses. The margin compresses.

In drones and counter-drones, the hierarchy runs differently but the principle is identical. The airframe is commoditized. Over half of drone component value concentrates in payloads, propulsion, and avionics… the most IP-intensive areas. In counter-drones, 60-70% of total system value sits in hard-kill effector systems, advanced radars, and AI-based sensor fusion and battle management software.

Astra Microwave’s GaN-based radar development. Data Patterns’ full design-to-integration capability with 35%+ EBITDA margins — the economics of a software business expressed through hardware. These are not manufacturing companies with good margins. They are IP companies with manufacturing operations, and the distinction determines everything about how to value them and what their long-term competitive trajectory looks like.

The investment implication in both value chains is the same principle expressed in different materials: position in the highest-complexity layer is durable because the barrier to entry is physical rather than regulatory, and it compounds rather than cycles because every year of production experience deepens the process knowledge that makes the position defensible.

But here is what most analysts miss: the companies building toward the apex of the complexity gradient look wrong in the near term. They are FCF negative. They have high working capital intensity — because aerospace programs require purchasing 500 castings to qualify 5, and the inventory sits on the balance sheet for years before the qualification converts to revenue. They are investing in capability before the demand is fully contracted. They are building for a position they want in 2035, not optimizing for the earnings call in 2025.

The market prices them as if the near-term wrongness is permanent. It is not.

Part Four: The Knudsen Gap and How It Closes

America in 1940 had Bill Knudsen. One human being who contained, in his memory and instincts and accumulated practice, the specific knowledge of how American industrial production worked at the deepest level, who could walk into an automobile factory and see, immediately, how to convert it to produce aircraft engines, where the bottlenecks would appear, what the tooling changes required.

India does not yet have its Knudsen. Not because the human capability is absent, India produces engineering talent at enormous scale, but because aerospace-specific production knowledge can only be acquired by doing aerospace work, and India has only recently begun doing aerospace work at the scale that generates genuine learning. The knowledge requires the work. The work requires the work before it.

This is the actual constraint. Not capital, which is available. Not demand, which is arriving from two directions simultaneously. Not political will, which is present and growing. The constraint is the time required to convert general engineering capability into the embodied, tacit, practice-grounded knowledge that makes a supplier irreplaceable, the knowledge that lives in the hands of Maini’s machinists who have been working titanium and Inconel for two decades, that lives in Dynamatic’s engineers who have built 13,000 flap-track beams without a single delivery failure, that will live, eventually, in whoever masters single-crystal turbine blade casting on Indian soil.

The Knudsen gap closes one program at a time. One qualification at a time. One self-certification at a time. One generation of engineers who have spent their careers building aerospace components rather than automotive components.

It closes faster than the pessimists believe because the forcing function is real and intensifying. When Airbus appoints its former Global Chief Procurement Officer to run India and South Asia — a CPO-level appointment that in an industry where supplier relationships are built over decades always precedes materially deeper integration, that is not a diplomatic gesture. It is a sourcing signal. When GE Aerospace sourcing from India grows 20x in five years, that is not an accident. It is a market responding to a specific structural reality: the Western supply chain cannot expand capacity at the required pace, the Chinese alternative is strategically untenable, and India is available.

Being available is the beginning. Being qualified is the middle. Being indispensable is the end. The Indian companies that are executing this journey correctly are moving through these stages on the only timeline that can be moved through: the real one, determined by the physics of industrial learning, not the aspirational one in an investor presentation.

Henry Kaiser knew this, in his own way. When he agreed to build a steel mill in California without knowing what a turbo-blower was, he was not pretending ignorance away. He was betting that the specific knowledge required to build the mill could be acquired in parallel with the building of it, because the commitment creates the urgency that generates the learning that fills the gap the commitment appeared to require. He finished five months ahead of U.S. Steel’s competing facility, which had an eleven-month head start. The commitment, made before the knowledge existed, created the conditions under which the knowledge could be acquired faster than any sequential approach would have allowed.

PTC Industries is running this logic explicitly. The acquisition of a 5,000-tonne EBCHR furnace for $2.5 million, a furnace that costs $80-90 million to purchase new, commits the company to becoming an aerospace-grade titanium processor before it fully is one. The commitment creates the urgency. The urgency creates the learning. The claim to have developed single-crystal turbine blade technology, if industrialized, places PTC in a group of perhaps five companies globally. The bet is that the commitment-before-readiness approach produces learning faster than the readiness-before-commitment approach would.

This is not recklessness. It is the specific kind of industrial audacity that every forge moment in history has required.

Part Five: The Investment Philosophy That This Demands

I want to be direct about what all of this means for how to invest, because the intellectual architecture is only worth building if it changes how you act.

The standard framework for evaluating manufacturing companies — EBITDA multiples, FCF yield, return on capital, is correct for mature supply chains in stable industries where the primary question is “how much will they earn this year?” It is wrong for formative supply chains at the beginning of a structural industrial shift where the primary question is “what position in the global ecosystem will this company occupy in fifteen years, and is the market pricing that correctly?”

These are not the same question. They produce not just different answers but different companies.

The companies that will generate exceptional returns from India’s industrial moment are not the ones with the best near-term earnings visibility. They are the ones building genuine position in the highest-complexity layers of the value chain — layers where pricing power is durable because the barrier to replication is physical, where switching costs are prohibitive because re-qualification timelines run to years, where the qualification barrier is high enough that new entrants cannot replicate your position without investing the same decades you already invested.

Three tests identify whether a company is building position or capturing tailwind.

The complexity test: what layer of the value chain does the company actually occupy, measured by the engineering intensity of what it produces, the certification depth required to qualify it, and the margin per unit of output? A company producing engine hot-section components at aerospace tolerances is building something that compounds. A company producing structural sheet metal panels is capturing something that cycles.

The trust test: has the company earned the institutional credibility that reflects genuine partnership rather than vendor status? Self-certification approvals. Sole-source program designations. Tier-1 system integration contracts. These are qualitative evidence of position that does not appear in financial statements but predicts long-term competitive trajectory with high accuracy. Maini’s five self-certification customers after twenty-one years of zero-defect delivery is not a marketing fact. It is a moat expressed in the language of institutional trust.

The duration test: is the management team building for the position it wants in twenty years, or optimizing for the earnings call? The companies building enduring position run FCF negative for years, invest in capability before the demand is contracted, qualify into programs where the revenue won’t materialize for half a decade. They look wrong in the near term. They are right in the long term. The market, calibrated for the near term, will misprice them longest and most consistently, which means they offer the largest return for investors with the right time horizon.

There is a fourth test that precedes all three, and it is the most important: the investor test. Do you have the time horizon, the conviction architecture, and the intellectual framework to hold a position that looks wrong for five years before it looks right? Can you distinguish between a company that is wrong and a company that requires patience? Can you sit with a negative FCF statement and see through it to the process knowledge being accumulated on the factory floor, the qualifications being earned, the trust being built that will eventually convert into revenue that no competitor can replicate?

If the answer is no, if your investment process requires quarterly validation, if your risk management framework demands FCF positivity before meaningful position sizing, if your clients demand the comfort of near-term earnings visibility, then this is not the right opportunity for you, and there is no shame in that. Different time horizons produce different opportunities, and the industrial formation thesis is explicitly a long-duration bet.

If the answer is yes, then you are looking at one of the most asymmetric opportunities available in Indian equities, precisely because the thesis requires a time horizon that most investors cannot or will not maintain. The asymmetry exists because of the patience requirement, not despite it. The patience requirement is the moat.

Part Six: What the Whole Thing Means

A nation’s industrial capability is a form of stored knowledge. Not abstract knowledge, the kind that lives in textbooks and strategy documents and policy recommendations, but embodied knowledge, the kind that lives in the hands and eyes and accumulated practice of people who have spent decades making things, failing, learning from the failure, and making things again. This knowledge is extraordinarily slow to build, extraordinarily difficult to transfer, and extraordinarily durable once built. It does not respond to incentive programs on the timescales that incentive programs typically anticipate. It does not appear in GDP statistics until it has been accumulating for a decade. It is invisible to most valuation frameworks until it suddenly is not.

Every great industrial nation built this knowledge under pressure. The pressure created the urgency. The urgency created the conditions under which the knowledge could accumulate faster than peacetime incentives would have produced. The accumulated knowledge then compounded, into civilian technological leadership, into export dominance, into the general-purpose manufacturing capability that turns raw materials and human energy into complex manufactured objects at scale, reliably, with quality.

India is now under the specific kind of pressure that has historically produced this kind of knowledge accumulation. The forcing functions are real: a Chinese military-industrial threat that is not going away, a commercial aerospace supply chain that has structurally failed and needs India to absorb its displaced work, a drone revolution that has inverted the economics of warfare and created urgent demand for capabilities India is only beginning to build. These forcing functions are arriving simultaneously, which has never happened to India before in its independent history.

The substrate is being built. One qualification, one self-certification, one delivered shipset, one single-crystal turbine blade casting at a time. Slower than the moment demands. Faster than the linear trend would suggest. Exactly as fast as the physics of industrial learning allows.

The market has not priced this correctly. It will, eventually, when the knowledge that is currently invisible in balance sheets and P&L statements becomes visible in the sustained high margins and durable competitive positions of the companies that built their capability before the demand arrived. The lag between the building and the pricing is where the alpha lives.

Bill Knudsen arrived at the White House in May 1940 and said: “Mr. President, I know how to make things.”

Twenty years earlier, he had accepted a salary of six thousand dollars — twelve percent of what he had been making, because it wasn’t where he started but where he’d finish that mattered.

The Indian companies that are building toward the apex of the aerospace and defense value chain are making the same calculation. They are accepting the near-term cost — negative FCF, high working capital, years of investment before revenue materializes, because it isn’t where they start but where they finish that matters.

The investors who understand this are not the ones watching the quarterly earnings. They are the ones watching the qualifications. The self-certifications. The sole-source designations. The CPO appointments. The program wins. The slow accumulation of the kind of institutional trust that takes twenty-one years of zero-defect delivery to earn, and that, once earned, cannot be replicated by a competitor in less time than it took to build.

That accumulation is happening right now, in factory floors in Bangalore and Belagavi and Lucknow and Hyderabad.

The forge is lit. The question is whether you can hold the position long enough to see what it becomes.
