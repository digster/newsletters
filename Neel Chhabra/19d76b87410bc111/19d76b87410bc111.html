The Landlord’s Decade

How India’s consumer market stopped being a battlefield and became a toll booth

Neel Chhabra
Apr 10
 
READ IN APP
 

"First the innovator, then the consolidator, then the toll-collector. The sequence has never varied." — Alfred Chandler, The Visible Hand

The landlord is thriving. The tenants are calling it growth.

That sentence contains everything. The rest of this essay is just the mechanism.

India’s D2C consumer landscape has, by most observable metrics, never been more vibrant. Challenger brands rising across every category… luggage, skincare, protein, apparel, footwear. Formalization compressing the unorganized sector from ~40% a decade ago toward 20%, and still falling. E-commerce growing. Quick commerce so fast that “10-minute delivery” has gone from marketing slogan to behavioral baseline. Legacy incumbents, spooked enough to write checks rather than fight… acquiring Minimalist (HUL), Yogabar (ITC), Plix (Marico), essentially paying for their own funeral arrangements in advance.

Every surface signal says: golden era.

Now look at the unit economics.

A D2C brand generating ₹100 of revenue on Blinkit or Zepto surrenders ₹25–30 before paying for ingredients, manufacturing, or working capital. Before the founder’s salary. Before the interest on the debt funding inventory sitting in a dark store they don’t own. The company that looks, from the outside, like a high-growth consumer brand is, from the inside, running a P&L that is a footnote in someone else’s advertising business. And the advertising business is the platform.

This is not a cyclical problem. It is not a “we’ll fix it at scale” problem. It is a structural problem… the kind that doesn’t announce itself loudly. It arrives slowly, then all at once, and by the time the market acknowledges it, the equity has already been extracted.

The anomaly isn’t that some D2C brands have bad margins. The anomaly is that the market hasn’t fully priced what it means that the platform take-rate is the margin, not a cost. When 25–30% of revenue flows to platform visibility before the business can do anything else with it, you haven’t built a consumer business. You’ve built a variable-cost supply chain for a media business.

This distinction is perhaps the most important structural fact in Indian consumer investing right now.

The Physics of Channel Formation

Every market has a gravitational arc. The channel that liberates ends up extracting.

Watch how it happens, because the sequence is precise enough to be a law.

A new distribution channel emerges. It promises things the old channel couldn’t: lower entry barriers, broader discovery, faster feedback loops, direct consumer relationships, escape from the tyranny of shelf space allocated by regional distributors who’ve been running the same routes for twenty years. Challenger brands rush in… grateful, margins relatively intact, growth accelerating. The channel is celebrated as democratizing infrastructure. The press calls it disruption. Investors call it opportunity. Founders call it freedom.

Then the aggregation logic kicks in.

Consumer attention, which was always the scarce resource, even when the constraint looked like distribution, begins concentrating on the platform with the best curation, the fastest delivery, the deepest catalog. The platform achieves liquidity. Liquidity attracts more brands. More brands create more consumer choice. More consumer choice requires better discovery infrastructure. Better discovery means algorithmic curation. Algorithmic curation creates winners and losers based on who optimizes for the algorithm. Optimizing for the algorithm costs money. The platform begins charging for visibility.

The liberation becomes a dependency. The dependency becomes a tax. The tax becomes a moat, on the platform’s side, not the brand’s.

Modern trade did this. Amazon did this. Meta’s advertising platform did this, the average cost of reaching 1,000 consumers on Facebook increased approximately 60% between 2018 and 2022 as competition for algorithmic placement intensified. And now, with extraordinary efficiency and speed, Blinkit, Zepto, and Swiggy Instamart are doing this. Quick commerce has compressed a decade-long arc into three years. The transition from liberation to extraction that took Amazon fifteen years is happening here in something closer to five.

The dark store isn’t the asset. The dark store is the antenna. It’s the instrument by which the platform generates a signal… consumer intent at the moment of maximum purchase readiness, that is worth dramatically more as an advertising surface than as logistics infrastructure. Amazon figured this out around 2012 and built what is now a ~$70 billion annual revenue advertising business running at margins north of 50%. Bezos didn’t plan it as a media play. He built a marketplace, built Prime, built logistics, built the flywheel, and then discovered that the attention he’d aggregated was worth more than the commerce it facilitated.

Blinkit’s contribution margin is already positive. The advertising revenue line is just becoming legible in Zomato’s earnings disclosures. The market is beginning to price this, but it’s pricing the early version… the logistics business finding its footing. It hasn’t yet priced what happens when the advertising flywheel reaches escape velocity, when the operating leverage of a media business nesting inside a logistics business becomes the dominant P&L story. For Zomato specifically, this is potentially the most important medium-term catalyst that equity markets haven’t yet modeled, because the margin dynamics of that transition look nothing like what current frameworks project.

The more uncomfortable question, the one that doesn’t fit neatly into any standard valuation framework, is whether Blinkit or Zepto eventually run the Amazon private label playbook.

Amazon used its unparalleled search placement data to identify which products convert best in which categories, then manufactured its own versions to compete directly with its own tenants, at preferential placement, with zero customer acquisition cost. If you were an investor in a marketplace tenant when Amazon launched Amazon Basics, the experience was instructive. You had built your growth on a platform with perfect information about your business, pricing leverage over your visibility, and every structural incentive to replicate what you did and cut you out.

India’s quick commerce platforms are acquiring exactly this information advantage… at SKU-level resolution: conversion rates, repeat purchase cycles, price elasticity by geography and consumer cohort, basket adjacency data. The tell will be subtle at first: a house brand appearing in “frequently bought together” rows, a private label SKU ranking above yours despite lower search volume, a quiet repricing on your category’s most-converted SKU. Watch for those signals. When Blinkit launches private labels, it won’t announce the strategy. It will simply start appearing where you used to be.

The brands that haven’t built demand-side leverage by then won’t just face margin pressure. They’ll face a competitor with perfect information and zero customer acquisition cost, because they paid it for them.

The Escape Geometry

Coca-Cola doesn’t pay for placement at D-Mart.

This sentence contains an entire theory of competitive strategy.

Coca-Cola has inverted the power relationship with its distribution channel so completely that the negotiation runs the other way: the retailer needs Coca-Cola more than Coca-Cola needs the retailer. Coca-Cola generates foot traffic. Coca-Cola anchors basket attachment. Coca-Cola absent from the shelf generates consumer complaints. The brand doesn’t exist to serve the retailer’s discovery infrastructure; the retailer exists, in part, because of the brand’s gravity.

That inversion… from tenant to landlord, from paying for placement to being placed because the channel needs you, is the only durable defense against distribution rent. And the mechanism by which it’s achieved is not advertising spend, not gross margins, not operational excellence. It is demand-side leverage: the brand so deeply embedded in consumer intent that the platform serves the brand, not itself, when the consumer types a search query.

There is a useful distinction here between share-of-search and share-of-wallet.

Share-of-wallet is a lagging indicator… it tells you what happened after the consumer opened the app and navigated to a category. Share-of-search is a leading indicator… it tells you what the consumer was already thinking about before the platform got a chance to steer them. A brand with high share-of-search has colonized the consumer’s mental model before any algorithmic intervention. The consumer comes to the platform with the brand already decided. The platform becomes the mechanism of transaction, not the mechanism of discovery.

When someone opens Blinkit wanting boAt, the platform serves the brand. When someone opens Blinkit wanting earbuds, the platform serves itself.

That distinction, which looks trivial at the transaction level, is, at scale, the entire game. It determines who accumulates terminal equity in the consumer relationship over the next two decades.

The brands that have built this kind of pull share a structural characteristic that doesn’t appear cleanly in their P&Ls: they used platforms as listening posts and distribution infrastructure while building audience ownership through content, community, and recurring behavioral rituals that live outside the platform’s algorithmic control. They have consumers who follow them, not just consumers who buy from them.

The consumer who follows is generating intent that the platform must serve. The consumer who merely buys is generating intent that the platform can redirect.

Building this kind of demand-side gravity requires a specific sequencing. The brand must earn psychological priority… must become the mental shorthand for a category or a moment, before the platform’s data advantage becomes fully weaponized. The window for doing this is shortening as platforms accumulate more information and more pricing leverage. For most categories, the useful window is probably five to seven years from the emergence of dominant quick commerce platforms. After that, the data asymmetry becomes too large, the algorithmic moats too entrenched, and the private label incentive too overwhelming.

Most D2C brands in India today are not in this window. They are in the renting phase, mistaking growth for compounding, accumulating operating history that is structurally indistinguishable from advertising revenue for the platform.

The ones that escape, and some will, will do so not by scaling faster but by converting faster. Converting buyers into followers, transactions into relationships, reach into gravity. Converting share-of-wallet into share-of-search before the landlord discovers what the tenants are worth.

The Moat Migration

The received wisdom about Indian consumer moats, in any analysis written before approximately 2018, follows a coherent and now-inadequate logic.

Three pillars. Brand, built through mass-media advertising spend that only scale could sustain. Distribution, built through decades of investing in a physical network that only incumbents could afford. Product, built through formulation advantages, supply chain investments, and proprietary manufacturing capability that takes time to replicate.

These pillars held, and they held well, for forty years, because the constraints were physical. Shelf space was finite. Distribution networks were expensive and slow to build. Consumer attention defaulted to whatever was most visible, because visibility was the scarce resource. Scale solved all three problems simultaneously.

Then the inversion came. Total and simultaneous.

Discovery became infinite. The virtual shelf of any e-commerce or quick commerce platform carries orders of magnitude more SKUs than any physical retail format ever built. A founder with a Shopify account and a Blinkit listing can reach more consumers today than a mid-sized FMCG company could reach in 2005 with a regional distributor network built over a decade. Distribution became abundant, not free, but no longer gatekept by physical infrastructure. And consumer attention, which was always the actual underlying asset that distribution moats were proxies for, became ferociously, genuinely scarce.

The strategic variable that determined forty years of consumer market outcomes… distribution reach, is no longer scarce. The variable implicitly assumed to be abundant… consumer attention, is now the binding constraint. And scale doesn’t buy attention the way it bought shelf space.

This should be catastrophic for incumbents. In many categories, it is. The challenger brands proliferating across FMCG, luggage, skincare, and apparel aren’t anomalies… they’re the structural prediction of this inversion. When the entry barriers were physical, only companies with large balance sheets could contest the category. When entry barriers became digital, any founder with a differentiated product and a coherent content strategy could contest the category.

But here is the part that most disruption narratives miss, and it’s what makes this question genuinely hard rather than just analytically complex.

The migration of moats from physical to digital to behavioral is not a replacement sequence. It’s an overlay.

The brands that built genuine psychological lock-in… not just distribution reach, but actual behavioral embedding, the kind where the consumer reaches for the brand before opening any app, didn’t lose their moat when distribution was democratized. Their moat was never distribution. It was the pre-app decision.

Colgate doesn’t worry about Blinkit’s private label toothpaste because the consumer has already decided on Colgate before the delivery app is relevant. The incumbent’s distribution advantage doesn’t transfer, but the behavioral embedding does.

What many incumbents are now discovering, stripped of distribution advantages that masked the absence of genuine positioning, is that what looked like brand equity was sometimes just the absence of competitive pressure. The shelf, it turns out, was doing more work than the brand.

The challengers that survive to become the next generation of incumbents are finding exactly these categories: where the old incumbents had distribution reach but not genuine positioning. They’re building something harder to displace than shelf presence. They’re building the answer the consumer gives before they open the app.

What the Market Doesn’t Understand About India’s Consumer Transition

India doesn’t climb the adoption ladder. It skips rungs.

The country never built the 7-Eleven ecosystem. Never developed the multi-store hypermarket format that dominated American and European mass-market consumption through the 1980s and 90s. It went from the kirana store… the 10x10 neighborhood shop that served Indian daily commerce for centuries… directly to e-commerce. And then, before e-commerce had stabilized as a behavioral baseline, leaped again to 10-minute delivery.

Global frameworks, calibrated on markets that transitioned incrementally through every intermediate format, consistently underestimate Indian consumer adoption velocity because they’re using the wrong model. Indian consumers don’t evaluate new formats against the last format they used. They evaluate them against the kirana, against the implicit benchmark of convenience, familiarity, and relationship that the neighborhood shop provided for generations.

Quick commerce doesn’t replace e-commerce. It replaces the walk to the corner store. That’s a much larger behavioral shift with much more durable adoption, and it explains why retention curves for Blinkit and Zepto look nothing like what category lifecycle models would predict.

The implication for brand-building is counterintuitive. If the mental model for quick commerce is the kirana, then the relationship dynamic that matters isn’t the algorithmically-mediated discovery that e-commerce optimized for. It’s the habitual trust the kirana earned over years. The consumer who ordered protein powder from Blinkit last week and reordered this week isn’t making a discovery decision. They’re running a habit. The brand that captures that habitual repurchase cycle… that becomes the default cognitive shorthand for a category, a moment, a consumer need, has built something that looks like the kirana’s relationship advantage embedded inside a digital platform’s scale.

Software recurring revenue is contractual. Consumer habit is psychological. You can break a contract with an audit. You can’t break a habit with a competitor’s ad spend.

The formalization trend compounds this. The consumer who shifts from loose atta to packaged atta hasn’t just made a purchase decision. They’ve made a statement about what kind of household they run. The identity stake is real, and identity stakes are extraordinarily resistant to price elasticity. The consumer defending a self-image doesn’t respond to discounts the way the price-sensitive consumer does. They respond to narrative, to community, to the brand that articulates why their choice matters.

This is why premiumization has proven stickier than skeptics modeled. The reversion rate is low not because the premium product is dramatically superior, but because regressing would require the consumer to consciously defeat their own internal story about who they are.

The brands that understand this are building emotional infrastructure, not just product features. They’re not selling protein; they’re selling the person the consumer is becoming. They’re not selling luggage; they’re selling the traveler the consumer believes themselves to be. The distinction is not marketing fluff. It’s the difference between a transaction and an identity anchor — and identity anchors survive algorithmic disruption in ways that transactional brands cannot.

The Compressed Lifecycle and Its Exceptions

The consensus view on consumer business lifecycles is directionally correct and structurally incomplete.

Average lifecycle compression is real. What took forty to fifty years to build…the distribution reach, brand recall, and category dominance that defined the great FMCG companies of the late twentieth century, will now max out at twenty to twenty-five years for most businesses operating in this new architecture. Technology cycles shorten execution advantages. Platform commoditization eliminates distribution as a sustainable differentiator. Attention fragmentation means brand recall accumulated over decades can be disrupted, given sufficient capital, in eighteen months by a challenger with a sharper content strategy.

But averages obscure the distribution. And the distribution is where the money is.

What’s shortening is the lifecycle for businesses whose moat was always execution-dependent… companies that won because they were faster, better-run, or more innovative during a specific window. These businesses were never building moats in the structural sense. They were running faster on a treadmill that the market mistook for a fortress. They had temporal advantages in a world where temporal advantages depreciate at an accelerating rate.

What may be lengthening, or at minimum, holding, is the lifecycle of businesses that have achieved genuine psychological lock-in: the brand that has become the default cognitive shorthand for a category, whose product has embedded itself into daily behavioral ritual so deeply that switching requires active effort rather than passive default. In a world of infinite discovery and scarce attention, the brand that already occupies the consumer’s mind before they open any app has a structural advantage that compounding strengthens, not weakens.

The outliers that go beyond twenty-five years won’t be the companies that executed most brilliantly during the platform transition. They’ll be the ones that became verbs. That own not just a product category but a behavioral category, the reference point against which every purchase decision in that category is evaluated. Not just the leading protein brand, but the brand consumers reach for reflexively when they think about protein, the way they reach for Band-Aid when they think about cuts.

These companies exist at the intersection of positioning durability and behavioral embedding. They are rare. They are worth finding. They are worth holding, at reasonable prices, for much longer than the compressed average lifecycle of their category peers would suggest. And they are worth slightly overpaying for, relative to what a conventional DCF suggests, because the conventional DCF is almost certainly undervaluing the behavioral lock-in that doesn’t appear cleanly in any observable financial metric until it’s too late to buy at a fair price.

This is the part of the Indian consumer market that the market hasn’t yet fully priced, because pricing it requires holding two things simultaneously: the structural compression of average business lifecycles and the structural extension of behavioral-embedding outliers. Most frameworks are calibrated for one or the other. The investors who hold both will own the companies that matter.

Coda: The Architecture of the Next Decade

Here is the one question the market hasn’t fully answered: what happens when the platforms’ data advantage becomes so complete that the information asymmetry between landlord and tenant is insurmountable?

When Blinkit knows, with better fidelity than any brand’s own data science team, which SKUs convert best at which price points in which cohorts, and has every structural incentive to extract that information into its own private label strategy, the shelf is no longer neutral territory. It is instrumented. It is watched. Every sale a brand makes is a data point the landlord collects on how to replace them.

History suggests this ends one way.

The brands that matter in 2040 will be the ones that made themselves the answer to a question consumers ask before opening any app. Not the brands that won the platform game… there is no winning the platform game for the tenant. The brands that exited the platform game, at the highest possible leverage point, by converting enough consumers into followers that the platform needs them more than they need the platform.

That conversion… from buyer to follower, from transaction to identity anchor, from share-of-wallet to share-of-mind, is not a marketing strategy. It’s a compounding mechanism. And in India, where 1.4 billion consumers are simultaneously formalizing, premiumizing, health-upgrading, and convenience-maximizing at a velocity that makes every historical adoption curve look conservative, the brands that compound that mechanism will build equity that no DCF will ever adequately capture.

The landlord is thriving. But a few tenants are building their own land.
