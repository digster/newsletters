In the summer of 2020, with the world locked indoors and equity markets convulsing, the most sophisticated capital allocators on earth — Facebook, Google, Intel, Qualcomm, KKR, Silver Lake, Mubadala, the Saudi sovereign fund, queued up over fourteen weeks to put more than US$20 billion into a company that, by every textbook definition, they should have been trying to disrupt.

The company was an Indian telecom operator. It owned towers. It dug trenches and buried fibre. It carried about US$20 billion of debt against US$30 billion of poured concrete and steel. It was the single most asset-heavy thing a balance sheet can be: a regulated utility that moves electromagnetic waves through the air for money.

The entire received wisdom of the prior fifteen years said this was the losing side of the board. The asset-light platforms had won. Apple and Google had captured the operating system. WhatsApp and Skype had hollowed out the voice-and-text revenue that telcos were built on. The carriers had been demoted to “dumb pipes” — commodity plumbing beneath the value, condemned to pour capital into networks that someone else would monetise.

And here was Silicon Valley, the apex predator of asset-light economics, paying to get inside the pipe.

Six years later the anomaly has sharpened into a paradox you can price. As this is written, Jio Platforms has filed to go public at a valuation banks peg around US$115–140 billion — a telecom company being underwritten not against Airtel’s multiple, but against Big Tech’s. Its closest rival, Bharti Airtel, has compounded from a near-death experience in 2013 into one of the most valuable companies in India. The third surviving operator owes the government roughly US$7–9 billion it cannot pay, kept alive by a state that has quietly become its largest shareholder.

Three operators. One left for dead, one reborn from the dead, one priced like it was never a telco at all. To understand how the same industry produced all three outcomes, and why the smartest money on earth wanted into the pipe, you have to stop looking at telecom as a business and start looking at it as a machine. A machine that moves exactly one thing.

The history of telecom is the history of that one thing being renamed. Minutes. Then bytes. Now tokens. Each rename destroyed the people who had built their entire company around the old word.

The companies are the vehicle. What we are actually after is a transferable way of seeing: how value migrates through any infrastructure industry, who captures it, and why owning the road is worthless unless you also own the toll.

The anomaly: why the smartest money paid to get inside the pipe

A clue is hiding in plain sight. The same investors who built their fortunes on owning none of the assets chose, for once, to own the heaviest asset in the room.

Start with the thing that should not make sense. In 2020, an asset-light platform’s entire religion is that infrastructure is a liability… something to be rented, abstracted, or pushed onto someone else’s balance sheet. Uber owns no cars. Airbnb owns no rooms. The world’s largest taxi company, the line goes, owns no taxis.

So when the high priests of that religion collectively wrote US$20 billion of cheques into towers and trenches, one of two things had to be true. Either they had lost their minds, or the religion had a footnote nobody had read aloud.

The footnote: asset-light only wins when someone else stays asset-heavy. Amazon Prime is a demand-side miracle that runs entirely on a supply-side machine — warehouses, trucks, planes, the most capital-hungry logistics network ever assembled. The asset-light story is what you tell investors. The asset-heavy reality is what makes the story true. Supply-side weight reinforces demand-side lightness; it does not replace it.

India in 2020 presented a once-a-generation arbitrage. A billion-plus people, the cheapest mobile data on earth, and a single operator that had already poured the concrete, built the towers, lit the fibre, captured 400 million users, and was now offering the platforms a seat on top of that infrastructure without making them pay to build it. You did not have to choose between asset-light and asset-heavy. You could buy a position above the asset and let the asset’s owner carry the weight.

That is the first reframe, and it sets up everything that follows. But the cleaner way to see the anomaly is to look at how the market is being asked to value the survivor today.

Look at the two Jio bars. They are the same company on the same day. The difference between them…roughly US$25 billion, larger than the entire equity value of the third-placed operator, is nothing but the shelf the market files Jio on. Telco shelf: discounted cash flows on a regulated utility with ARPU growth in the low teens. Platform shelf: a digital gateway to 524 million Indians with optionality on cloud, AI, advertising, and payments stacked on top of the connection.

The whole IPO is an argument about which shelf is correct. And the argument is not really about Jio. It is about whether the unit of value in this industry has quietly changed underneath everyone, whether the company is still selling minutes and bytes, or has started selling something the old valuation models cannot price.

To adjudicate that, we have to go down to the metal.

What a telecom network actually is

Strip away the brands, the ad jingles, the IPO decks. Underneath is a single physical bet: that you can move a unit of information from one human to another more cheaply than the next operator, and charge for the gap.

A mobile network is four things stacked on top of each other, and the economics of the whole industry fall out of how those four layers behave. Get the layers right and the rest of this essay is arithmetic.

Layer one: spectrum is the only input you cannot make

Everything in telecom begins with a slice of the electromagnetic field. Radio waves at specific frequencies are the medium; the right to transmit on them is granted, exclusively and temporarily, by the state. This is the bedrock fact of the entire industry and the source of its strangeness: the primary raw material is sold by the government, in lumpy auctions, at prices the government partly controls.

You cannot manufacture more spectrum. You cannot import it. You can only win it at auction, then sweat it. Low-frequency spectrum (700 MHz) travels far and penetrates walls — cheap coverage, thin capacity. High-frequency spectrum (3.3 GHz, 26 GHz) carries enormous data but barely reaches the next building — dense capacity, expensive coverage. An operator’s spectrum portfolio is its genome. Jio is the only Indian operator holding low-band, mid-band, and millimetre-wave across every circle, which is why it can run the same 5G network as a rural coverage blanket and an urban capacity firehose.

The mechanism that runs the whole industry:

Because spectrum is state-granted and finite, telecom is structurally an oligopoly with a regulator sitting at the head of the table. The number of viable national operators is capped by how the spectrum is sliced. Every major event in this story… the 1999 bailout, the 2012 licence cancellations, the 2019 AGR ruling, is the regulator’s hand moving the pieces. You cannot understand the competition without the referee.

Layers two and three: the capital is poured here, the profit is not

Towers and fibre are where the money goes in. A national network is hundreds of thousands of tower sites, each needing land, power, steel, and backhaul, plus roughly a million route-kilometres of buried glass connecting them. This is the “asset-heavy” that frightens platform investors: tens of billions of capital, depreciating, defending a service that the customer experiences as invisible.

And here Airtel made a move in the mid-2000s that reads, in hindsight, like a thesis statement for the whole industry: it spun its towers into a separate company, Indus Towers, and shared them with its competitors. A moat, voluntarily converted into shared infrastructure. The logic was ruthless. If the towers are a cost everyone must bear, owning them alone is not an advantage, it is just a heavier version of the same cost. Better to mutualise the cost, lower it for everyone including yourself, and redeploy your freed capital into the layers where differentiation is actually possible: spectrum and subscribers.

The first lesson of infrastructure economics: an asset that every competitor must replicate is not a moat. It is a tax that you can sometimes turn into a toll.

Layer four: the zero-marginal-cost bet that detonated the industry

Now the layer that matters most, and the one that explains why an entire generation of operators died. For most of telecom history, a network was built for voice — circuit-switched telephone calls, with data services bolted awkwardly on top. Voice was the product; data was the upsell. The whole cost structure, billing system, and engineering culture assumed a minute of talk was the unit being sold.

A pure data network inverts this. When everything is data, when a voice call is just a low-bandwidth stream of packets like any other, the marginal cost of carrying one more call, one more video, one more anything collapses toward zero. The expensive part is building the network once. After that, capacity is effectively a fixed asset you are desperate to fill. The optimal strategy flips from ration the scarce minutes and charge a premium to flood the network with usage and charge almost nothing per unit, because the per-unit cost is almost nothing.

This is the single most important diagram in the essay. The challenger pays more upfront… tens of billions to build an all-data network from nothing, but reaches a marginal-cost floor the incumbent’s curve can never touch, because the incumbent is dragging a legacy voice core behind it. At high volume, the challenger can profitably charge a price that is below the incumbent’s cost.

That is not a price war. That is a structural execution. The incumbent who matches the price bleeds at every unit; the incumbent who doesn’t loses the customer. Both roads lead to consolidation. The only question is how long the incumbents’ balance sheets can outlast the descent.

Hold this shape in your head. In 2016, a company that had spent a decade and US$30 billion building exactly this curve switched it on across India in a single night. Everything else is the consequence.

Anatomy of the value chain, and the great drain

Follow a single rupee of mobile spend through the system. Where it sticks, and where it leaks, tells you who has power.

An industry’s structure is written in its margin pools — the places along the chain where profit accumulates and resists competition. In telecom, the chain runs: state (spectrum) → tower companies → operators → device makers → app & content layer → the subscriber’s attention. The profit does not distribute evenly. It pools where supply is scarce and switching is hard, and it drains away where the offering is commoditised.

For two decades the most reliable pool was the operator itself. A licensed moat, near-zero churn, infrastructure too costly to replicate, and a pipe onto which new services layered at almost no marginal cost. By the 4G era, the incremental margin on adding one more customer to an existing network exceeded eighty percent. That is the economics of a tollbooth: build the road once, and every additional car is nearly pure profit.

Then two drains opened at once.

The first drain was downstream. WhatsApp ate SMS. Skype, then every video-call app, ate international voice. The operator still carried the bits, but the value of those bits, the part the customer was willing to pay a premium for, migrated up into the app layer where Google and Meta sat. The operator was left holding the cost of the pipe while someone else monetised what flowed through it. This is the “dumb pipe” thesis, and for a while it was simply true.

The second drain was upstream. In October 2019, India’s Supreme Court ruled that “adjusted gross revenue” — the base on which operators pay an 8% licence fee — included not just core telecom revenue but essentially all revenue: interest income, asset sales, everything. Two decades of disputed back-dues crystallised overnight into a liability exceeding US$18 billion across the sector. The state, which sells the raw material, had just retroactively raised the price of it. One operator never recovered.

So the operator’s pool was being drained from above by the regulator and from below by the platforms. The “dumb pipe” was real. The question that splits this industry into survivors and corpses is what each operator did about it. And the cleanest way to see the answer is through the most violent consolidation in the history of any major economy’s telecom sector.

The thirty-year war and the refusal of fear

One company has been declared dead four times and is, in 2026, more valuable than ever. Its survival is not luck. It is a single repeated decision, made under conditions that begged for the opposite.

The story starts in Ludhiana, Punjab — a refugee city of small factories that the locals called the Manchester of India. A trader named Sunil Mittal grew up there absorbing one lesson from the place itself: adversity creates opportunity, survival fuels ambition, and the balance sheet is the only truth that matters. He sold bicycle parts, then yarn, then steel sheets he carried to Bombay himself. At a Taiwan trade fair he found push-button telephones, exotic in a rotary-dial India, had them disassembled into parts to dodge import rules, and reassembled them at home. When the government auctioned mobile licences in 1991, he spent sixty days in London paying specialists to teach him an industry he knew nothing about, won over a French operator in a single meeting, and launched Airtel in 1995.

What followed was not a growth story. It was four executions, survived.

Near-death one: the licence-fee trap (1999)

The early industry was built on front-loaded licence fees and spectrum royalties that pushed the entire sector toward default by 1999. The whole cohort of operators was drowning in fixed payments before the market had matured. The government rescued it, replacing fixed fees with revenue-sharing, separating the regulator from the department that ran the state telco, and creating, for the first time, an investable business model. The lesson banked: in this industry, the referee can kill you or save you, and reading the referee is a core competence, not a side activity.

Near-death two: the first Reliance assault (2002)

Then the first Reliance attack. Mukesh Ambani’s Reliance Infocomm launched in December 2002 on a US$2 billion fibre backbone, pricing calls at a fraction of incumbents’ rates, the classic move of an aggressor with a deeper balance sheet: price below cost, acquire at industrial scale, suffocate everyone who cannot match without bankrupting themselves. Airtel’s stock fell from Rs 45 to Rs 19. Every analyst, every board member, every textbook said: match the price.

Mittal took his leadership team to a retreat in Kathmandu instead, and made a decision that is the philosophical core of this entire essay.

Sunil Mittal, on the 2002 Reliance price war

“Lie low, I said, and we will live to fight for another day. I revved up morale in the company by telling all colleagues: everyone expects us to lose, but if we don’t, we will make history.”

The decision was an act of structural refusal, not refusing competition, but refusing the terms on which it was offered. Reliance had chosen price as the battlefield. Mittal recognised three things almost no one around him did: price was a battlefield Reliance was funding; the customers acquired at that price were liabilities whose unit economics would eventually destroy whoever serviced them; and the only question worth answering was whether his balance sheet could outlast Reliance’s appetite to fund the war.

It could. Within twelve months the stock was back to Rs 80, within three years Rs 429. Reliance Infocomm, built to be Airtel’s executioner, bet on the wrong technology (CDMA), found its below-cost subscriber base impossible to serve profitably, and was absorbed into the corpse-pile of Indian telecom by 2010. The fibre backbone became a stranded asset.

Market share acquired below cost is not market share. It is a controlled bankruptcy, executed quarterly, that takes longer to show up in the financial statements than a sudden collapse would.

Near-death three: the Africa misadventure (2010–2014)

Then Airtel did the thing disciplined survivors are supposed to never do: it over-reached. The 2010 Zain acquisition — US$10.7 billion for operations across fifteen African countries, at 8x EBITDA — flipped Airtel from cash-rich to debt-heavy and nearly broke it. The India tower-sharing playbook did not translate; each African country had its own tax code, labour law, and protectionist wall. By January 2013, net profit had collapsed 87%, ARPU had fallen, and eight of thirteen members of the management board had resigned.

Mittal’s misread, in his own later account, was a boundary-condition error: India’s price cuts drove volume because a genuine middle class existed to absorb cheaper service. In most of Africa, lower prices simply let consumers spend less, the same units, less revenue. The model was correct; the substrate was different. The turnaround came from a HUL consumer-goods veteran, Gopal Vittal, brought in as CEO, who centralised customer management on the cloud, exited markets where Airtel couldn’t reach the top two, and cut operating costs 40% in four years.

Near-death four: the second Reliance juggernaut (2016)

And then, the big one. In September 2016, Jio launched — free voice, data so cheap it undercut everyone by more than a third, 100 million subscribers in 170 days. This time the aggressor had the right technology and a bottomless balance sheet behind it. Over half of India’s remaining operators failed or merged in the aftermath.

Airtel’s defence was the accumulated muscle of three prior survivals. It had upgraded to 4G across nearly 300 cities before Jio launched. It tracked subscriber defections in real time with retention offers. In 2018 it deliberately purged 49 million low-value subscribers — voluntarily shrinking to improve network quality and free 2G spectrum for 4G. And it leaned into the one place its thirty-year corporate relationships gave it an edge Jio could not buy quickly: enterprise and B2B fibre, which grew 25–30% a year through the pandemic as it absorbed the weakening third operator’s business base.

By 2026 Airtel led on active subscribers and on ARPU (Rs 257 vs Jio’s Rs 214), while Jio led on data consumed per user (42 GB vs 31 GB monthly). The dead company had not merely survived the second Reliance assault. It had come out the higher-quality operator on the metrics that monetise.

Strip the four episodes down and the same gear turns each time. Not the refusal of caution… Airtel is among the most cautious major operators on earth. The refusal of panic. The refusal to match a competitor’s pricing on terms the competitor designed. The refusal to defend customer cohorts that destroy value. The refusal, in 2010, to assume the India model would transfer to Africa without checking the boundary conditions, learned the hard way, but learned.

Survival in a capital-gated industry is not a function of who has the best technology. It is a function of who refuses to fight the war the aggressor is funding.

That is the transferable lens. Now meet the aggressor, and the entirely different theory of the world it represents.

The reset: from oil, to data, to the gateway

Read the Jio logo backwards and it spells “oil.” The joke is also the thesis. A company built on hydrocarbons made the largest private bet in Indian history that the next refinery would process information.

Reliance’s founding logic was backward integration: start with polyester yarn, then secure the supply chain — petrochemicals, refining, finally oil and gas exploration. Own every link from molecule to market. When the question became where does the next Reliance grow, the answer was a different feedstock entirely. “Data is the new oil,” Mukesh Ambani declared at Jio’s launch. The line was marketing. The structure beneath it was deadly serious.

Here is what made the bet specific rather than slogan. Every incumbent operator on earth had built a voice network and layered data on top. Jio built, from the first brick, a pure all-IP 4G data network — the lower cost curve from Figure 3, made real at national scale. Three consequences followed mechanically:

Because 4G does not support traditional circuit-switched telephony, voice calls became just data — the cheapest, lowest-bandwidth thing on the network. Jio could give voice away free forever and lose nothing, because it was never a separate product. Because everything was data, the network could be built with commodity hardware in ways a legacy voice network cannot. And because the marginal cost of carrying traffic approached zero, the optimal play was the one the cost curve demanded: spend enormously upfront, then serve the maximum number of people to spread that fixed investment across the widest possible base.

This is a Silicon Valley bet… a wager on zero marginal cost, executed with a refiner’s balance sheet and a refiner’s institutional muscle. The same team that had built the world’s largest refinery at Jamnagar knew how to acquire land, win licences, manage state authorities, and pour US$30 billion of infrastructure on schedule. The hard part of Jio was never the strategy. It was the execution, and execution was the legacy oil business’s deepest competence, redeployed.

The launch numbers are the stuff of case studies. 100 million customers in under 180 days. Data prices collapsing from roughly Rs 225 per GB to under Rs 10. Monthly data per user rising from under 0.2 GB to, today, over 42 GB. By FY26, Jio carries about 60% of India's wireless data traffic and serves 524 million customers. But the launch is not the interesting part, everyone knows the launch. The interesting part is what the financial machine did after the build.

This is the payoff of the zero-marginal-cost bet, in two lines. While the network was being built, almost all of EBITDA was being eaten by capital spending — in FY24, just 2.6% survived. Two years later, with the build largely done, 55% of EBITDA converts straight through. The fixed cost was poured; now every additional gigabyte rides the asset at near-zero incremental cost. The tollbooth is finished, and the cars have started arriving.

But notice what Jio did not do with its most expensive, most defensible asset. It did not hoard the 4G network as a private moat. In the move that most baffled industry observers, Jio opened its infrastructure and pursued a deliberately layered strategy — dominating some layers, commoditising others, and inviting partners into the ones it chose not to own. That decision only makes sense if you stop thinking about Jio as a telecom company at all, and start thinking about it as something that competes on a different axis entirely.

Which brings us to the structural shift underneath the whole story.

The industry turned on its side

Most analysts asked whether Jio was a good telecom company. Wrong question. Jio was not trying to win the telecom game. It was trying to change which game everyone else was forced to play.

For most of the twentieth century, industries were organised vertically. A few large firms each owned the whole stack — supply, production, distribution, because coordinating across separate companies was expensive. The cost of getting two firms to work together (writing contracts, enforcing them, moving information) was high enough that it was cheaper to own everything yourself. Transaction costs built the vertically integrated giant.

Digital technology demolished that logic. When firms can communicate cheaply, interoperate via standards, and coordinate without owning each other, the expensive glue of vertical integration dissolves. The stack breaks into horizontal layers, each with specialists competing to do one thing well, all loosely organised toward a shared outcome. The industry rotates ninety degrees… from a few firms each owning a vertical slice, to many firms each owning a horizontal layer.

The telecom industry had already been rotated once — by Apple and Google at the OS layer, by WhatsApp and Skype at the communications layer, which is exactly what relegated operators to “dumb pipes.” Most operators responded by either copying the platforms (and failing) or fighting price wars among themselves (and commoditising further). Both responses accepted the new layered map and tried to survive within it.

Jio did something categorically different. It did not try to win within the layered structure. It tried to author the structure itself, to set the rules by which every other player at every layer would create and capture value, and then sell defined positions in that structure to the most powerful possible partners.

This is the move that explains the 2020 investments. Jio did not go to Google and Meta and say “buy a stake in our telecom company.” It went to them with a map of the entire Indian digital economy and said: here is the structure we are building, here is the position you would occupy in it, and here is who gets commoditised if you are not in it. Intel and Qualcomm took the position that strengthened a “made-in-India” 5G stack — an alternative to Huawei. Google took the device and OS position, building a low-cost smartphone. Meta took the user-facing services position, with WhatsApp as the super-app shell. Each bought a layer. Together they ratified Jio’s map.

And here is the cruelty of the design, the part that makes it more than a supply-chain squeeze. The players who get commoditised are not crushed directly by the structure’s author. They are made to commoditise each other in response to the rules. When Jio made data nearly free, it did not personally bankrupt the other operators, it set conditions under which they had to fight each other on price until two of them disappeared. The structure does the work. The author just chooses the geometry.

Your market power today does not matter. What matters is the layer at which you play. If you are at the wrong layer, you are toast… and you will be toasted by your own peers, on terms someone else designed.

This reframes the entire Airtel–Jio rivalry. They are not really running the same race. Airtel is the master of competing within — the most disciplined operator at surviving and winning inside whatever structure exists, four times over. Jio is an attempt to compete with the structure… to be the company that decides what the structure is. Whether that attempt succeeds is the open question. First, the arithmetic of where they actually stand.

The duopoly arithmetic

Two giants generating tollbooth cash flows. One wounded third kept alive by the state. The numbers reveal two different theories of how to win the same market.

After the great consolidation, India’s mobile market is effectively a duopoly with a state-supported survivor and a state-owned laggard. The economics are now the inverse of the price-war years: a structure stable enough that operators can raise tariffs together, monetise 5G, and convert the network into cash. The FY26 scoreboard:

Read the table as two theories of victory. Airtel optimises the value of each customer: fewer total subscribers than Jio, but higher ARPU, higher margin, faster net adds in FY26, and a deliberate skew toward premium and enterprise. It runs the network as a tollbooth and prices the toll as high as the structure allows. Jio optimises the size and engagement of the base: the most customers, the most data per user, the lowest net debt, and a platform stacked on top of the connection. It runs the network as a gateway and monetises what passes through.

Neither is obviously right. Airtel’s higher ARPU and margin make it, today, the better pure-telecom business… arguably the highest-quality large operator on the planet. Jio’s larger base, deeper engagement, and platform optionality make it the better structure business, if the structure thesis pays off. The market is pricing both: Airtel as a premium operator, Jio as a platform. The IPO will test which framing the public market believes.

The wounded third, and the referee who became a shareholder

And then there is Vodafone Idea — the merged remains of two operators that, at the 2018 merger, served over 400 million subscribers and now serve roughly 190 million, bleeding share to both leaders every quarter. The October 2019 AGR ruling fell hardest here: liabilities the company simply cannot pay, even after the government reduced and reassessed them, even after a years-long moratorium. To keep a third private operator alive, because a true duopoly invites regulatory and political discomfort, the state converted dues into equity and became the company’s largest shareholder.

This is the final twist of the spectrum-is-state-granted logic. The referee who sells the raw material, who set the price that nearly killed the operator, has now become its owner to prevent it from dying. The same hand that moved the pieces is now holding one of them upright. Vodafone Idea would need cumulative tariff hikes around 45% across FY27–FY30 just to meet its statutory obligations, which is another way of saying its survival depends on the duopoly raising prices enough to drag it along. The two strong operators benefit from a weak third existing; they do not benefit from it dying and triggering a duopoly backlash. So the structure holds a wounded player upright on purpose.

In an industry where the state owns the raw material, the most important competitor is sometimes the one the state refuses to let fail.

The next currency: minutes to bytes to tokens

Every previous rename of the unit destroyed the operators built around the old word. The industry is mid-rename again. This time the new unit is a unit of intelligence, and the people best positioned to own it are the same ones the operators must rent it from.

Return to the spine of this essay. A telecom network moves one unit of value, and the unit keeps changing names. The operators who thrived selling minutes were nearly wiped out when the unit became bytes — because a byte network has a fundamentally different cost curve than a minute network, and the minute-sellers could not cross to the lower curve fast enough. We are now watching the third rename. The unit is becoming the token: the atomic unit of AI inference, the thing a large model consumes and produces every time it answers.

Jio’s group leadership has said it in exactly these words… the industry is moving from minutes to bytes to tokens, and Jio intends to be the lowest-cost provider of tokens, the same way it became the lowest-cost provider of bytes. The phrasing is not incidental. It is the same strategy, re-aimed at the new unit: pour enormous fixed capital into the infrastructure that produces the unit (now AI data centres and power, not towers and fibre), drive the marginal cost toward zero, flood the country with consumption, and capture the structure.

Both Indian giants have grasped the rename and are pouring concrete for it… literally, because AI tokens are produced in data centres that burn gigawatts of power. The token’s “tower” is a building full of GPUs next to a power plant.

Jio has announced a roughly US$110 billion plan spanning AI, data centres, and energy infrastructure… the same playbook as 2016, scaled up and re-aimed. It has lined up the AI equivalents of its 2020 partners: a tie-up giving its users access to a leading AI assistant, a partnership structure with a major US chipmaker for compute, and an enterprise-AI joint venture with a global social platform’s subsidiary. The structure-authoring move, run again, for tokens.

Airtel is competing within the new structure with its characteristic discipline. Its data-centre arm, Nxtra, has raised US$1 billion and targets a quarter of India’s data-centre market; in Vizag it is co-developing a roughly US$15 billion gigawatt-scale data centre with Google and the Adani group. True to thirty years of form, Airtel is not trying to author the AI structure, it is securing the highest-value position it can within it, partnering rather than declaring a new map.

This is the genuinely open question on which the whole valuation argument rests. In the byte era, the critical input was spectrum, and spectrum was sold by the Indian state — a referee the operators knew how to read, and whose pieces eventually consolidated into a profitable duopoly. In the token era, the critical input is the frontier model, and that is not sold by the Indian state. It is controlled by a handful of global firms with capital scale that makes thirty years of disciplined Indian cash generation look, for the first time, structurally insufficient.

The byte war was won by whoever could pour the most infrastructure inside a market the state had fenced off and would eventually rationalise. The token war may be won upstream of the operators entirely at the model layer, where neither Indian giant has a structural position and where the consolidation is not guaranteed to happen on Indian terms.

Jio’s bet was that whoever owns the cost curve owns the industry. The token era asks a harder question: what if you own the cost curve, but rent the thing that flows through it from someone you cannot consolidate?

Nobody knows the answer… not the founders, not the analysts writing the IPO notes, not the partners. Which is precisely why the Jio bar in Figure 1 has that US$25 billion of daylight in it. The platform premium is the market pricing a probability that the byte-era playbook transfers to tokens. The discount is the market pricing the chance it does not.

The world, seen through the pipe

The companies were the vehicle. Here is the cargo — six transferable ways of seeing that work on any infrastructure industry, and most others besides.

If you only remember that Airtel survived and Jio scaled, the essay has failed. The point was never the two companies. It was to extract a set of lenses you can carry to the next infrastructure war — energy, logistics, payments, cloud, data centres, ports, because they all run on the same physics.

One — In a state-gated industry, the referee is a player.

When the primary input is sold by the government — spectrum, mining rights, landing rights, banking licences, the industry is structurally an oligopoly with a regulator at the head of the table. Every major value migration is the referee’s hand moving pieces. Reading the referee is not a compliance function. It is a core competence, and the firms that treat it as central (Reliance, Airtel both) outlast the ones that treat it as overhead.

Two — An asset everyone must replicate is a tax, not a moat.

The towers were not a moat; they were a cost every competitor bore. Airtel’s insight… mutualise the shared cost, redeploy capital to the layers where differentiation is possible, generalises to any industry where the heavy asset is undifferentiated. Own the layer that is scarce and defensible; share or rent the layer that is merely expensive. Owning expensive-but-undifferentiated assets alone just gives you a heavier version of everyone else’s cost.

Three — The cost curve is destiny.

The single most predictive fact about the entire thirty-year war was the shape of the cost curve. Whoever builds the structurally lower marginal-cost curve can price below incumbents’ cost and survive there… turning a price war into an execution. Before forecasting competitive dynamics in any industry, draw the cost curves. The firm on the lower floor wins the long game even if it loses money for years getting there.

Four — Refuse the war the aggressor is funding.

Airtel’s repeated survival came from refusing to fight on the battlefield the better-capitalised aggressor chose. Matching a below-cost price war funded by a deeper balance sheet is a controlled bankruptcy. The correct response is to conserve cash, defend positive-unit-economics customers, and let the aggressor fund your eventual consolidation — if the aggressor’s technology bet is wrong. The judgement is in distinguishing the aggressor who will exhaust themselves (Reliance Infocomm, 2002) from the one who has the right curve and infinite fuel (Jio, 2016). Against the first, refusal wins. Against the second, refusal only survives, and you must find a different layer to win on. Airtel found enterprise and premium.

Five — Compete with the structure, or get sorted by it.

The deepest lesson. Most firms compete within an industry’s structure… better product, lower price, more scale. A rare few compete with the structure… redrawing the rules of value creation so that other players commoditise each other on terms the author designed. When such a firm enters your industry, your current market power is nearly irrelevant; what matters is whether you occupy a layer the new structure rewards or one it is built to drain. Ask of any industry: who is competing within, and who is trying to redraw the map? The map-drawers are rare, but when they appear they decide everyone else’s outcome.

Six — The unit of value gets renamed, and the rename is the danger.

Minutes, bytes, tokens. Each rename rewarded whoever built the new cost curve first and destroyed whoever was optimised for the old unit. This is the master pattern, and it is everywhere: film to pixels, retail shelf-space to search-ranking, advertising impressions to attention to intent. The strategic question for any incumbent is never “are we good at the current unit?” It is “what is the next unit, what cost curve does it run on, and are we structurally able to cross to it, or are we, like the minute-sellers, optimised for a word that is about to be retired?”

Watched closely enough, two telecom operators turn out to be a referendum on a question much larger than telecom: is survival a property of the survivor, or a property of the substrate that permits it? Airtel’s thirty years argue that disciplined refusal of fear is a genuine, transferable quality, that some firms simply know how to not die. Jio’s bet argues that the substrate decides, that whoever owns the cost curve of the next unit owns the future, and quality is downstream of position.

The token era is the first clean test of which is true. For thirty years the substrate — state-granted spectrum, guaranteed eventual consolidation, rewarded the quality of disciplined survival. The next decade asks whether that quality transfers to a substrate where the critical input is not granted by a readable referee but controlled by global firms operating at a scale that makes the entire Indian telecom war look like a rehearsal.

The difference between “the survivor was good” and “the substrate was kind” is the entire question. And for the first time in thirty years, we are about to find out which one was doing the work.

That is what you can see through the pipe. Not two companies. A working model of how value moves through any industry built on physical infrastructure and licensed scarcity, and a live, unresolved experiment, filed with a stock exchange in June 2026, on whether the rules that governed the last unit of value will govern the next one.

The world is the curriculum. This particular exam has not been graded yet.
