Quick commerce is the loudest growth story in Indian logistics, and the standard advice to a third-party network is simple: chase it. Bid for last-mile contracts from Blinkit, Instamart, Zepto, whoever will sign. Volume compounds, and volume is the game. For two years, every operator with spare fleet capacity has been trying to get inside that last mile.
Delhivery has spent those two years walking the other way. In the quarter to June 2026 it moved 322 million express shipments, up 55.2% year on year, and 542,000 tonnes of PTL freight. It is hunting volume in every corner of its business. And it has explicitly, repeatedly refused the two parts of quick commerce that touch the end consumer.
We have stayed away from 2 specific parts of quick commerce… We do not run dark stores for quick-commerce players… We also do not deliver from dark stores to consumers… We do not view last-mile delivery in quick commerce as a differentiated capability.
— Sahil Barua, MD & CEO · Q1 FY27 earnings call
That is a company saying no, on purpose, to the demand pool everyone else is bidding for, while saying yes to almost everything else. A refusal that specific is not caution. It is a claim about structure, and it deserves a mechanism rather than a shrug.
The consensus read treats “quick-commerce logistics” as a single category you either serve or don’t. It isn’t. It is two legs that happen to sit end to end, and they obey opposite economics.
The dark-store-to-doorstep leg is short-haul, high-frequency, and it is the platform’s product. The ten-minute number is the brand promise; nothing else the platform does matters if that leg is unreliable. The inbound leg… factories and sellers into mother warehouses, mother warehouses into hundreds of dark stores, is long-haul, multi-node, and invisible to the customer. Nobody has ever chosen a quick-commerce app because its replenishment routing was elegant.
Here is the causal chain. A third-party network prices its service to cover a fleet that serves many shippers. A platform running its own fleet only needs to cover routes it already controls, serving itself. At low density, the third party wins easily, its pooled utilisation beats anything a single principal can achieve on thin routes. But density is not static. As drops per square kilometre rise, the captive fleet’s cost per drop falls much faster than the shared network’s, because the captive fleet was carrying the utilisation penalty and the shared network was not.
At some point the curves cross. Past that point there is no reason to keep paying a third party a margin on the one leg that defines your customer experience, you can do it cheaper and control the quality yourself.
This is not a forecast. It already happened once, in an adjacent category, in food delivery, the outsourced share of last mile is now small. The platforms took it in-house as soon as their own density made that cheaper. Quick commerce is the same shape, running a few years behind.
Sahil Barua: “As long as quick commerce continues to grow, there will obviously be a significant challenge in getting goods to mother warehouses and dark stores on time. Ensuring that this happens reliably is where Delhivery will participate. We have stayed away from 2 specific parts of quick commerce, as I have pointed out before. We do not run dark stores for quick-commerce players... We also do not deliver from dark stores to consumers... Similarly, we do not view last-mile delivery in quick commerce as a differentiated capability. I think that is something the quick-commerce players will ultimately keep captive, and they will reduce rates over time. We have seen this happen, for example, in food delivery, where the outsourced percentage is fairly small. We think the same thing will happen, and we have stayed away.”
The inbound leg does not obey this. It gets better the more shippers share it… routing, sortation, and fleet utilisation all improve with aggregate volume that no single quick-commerce player generates alone. A platform internalising that leg would be building a shared-infrastructure business inside a single-tenant company, paying for network breadth it can never use outside its own four walls. There is no reason to own it, and there never will be.
Suppose Delhivery had instead announced an aggressive push for quick-commerce last-mile contracts. Would the same “disciplined operator” narrative explain that equally well? No, it would require an incompatible claim: that last-mile density doesn’t compound the way food delivery’s did, or that platforms will keep outsourcing past the point where owning is cheaper. Nothing in the disclosure supports either, and the food-delivery base rate argues hard against both. The explanation would look obviously wrong under different facts, which is what separates a mechanism from a story fitted after the event.
Better: the same boundary, run in reverse, explains cases it was not built for.
Run it once more and it reaches the newest thing on the disclosure. Delhivery Financial Services received its NBFC licence in July 2026, and management was unusually plain about what it does not intend to do with it.
Our intention is not to lend heavily from the Delhivery balance sheet at all… Fundamentally, we do not anticipate having to allocate significant capital ourselves for lending. — Sahil Barua, MD & CEO · Q1 FY27 earnings call
That is the identical boundary, applied to credit. Originate where you hold a pooled information advantage across the demand and supply side of thousands of fleet owners, a genuinely shared asset. Do not own the capital-intensive leg, because a dedicated lender can carry that balance sheet more cheaply for itself. The company is running the same test on a business that has nothing to do with parcels, which is roughly the definition of a rule rather than a rationalisation.
An explanation that only flatters its subject is not an explanation. This one cuts both ways: any enterprise account whose volume with Delhivery, in a single dense corridor, grows large enough to make dedicated ownership cheaper than shared-network pricing is a candidate to walk. That is a live pressure point, not a permanent moat, and it is checkable.
Meanwhile the quarter itself showed what carrying a wide network costs when the environment turns. Revenue grew 27.8%. Adjusted EBITDA margin fell from 3.3% to 2.6% on buffer labour, statutory minimum-wage revisions across four states, and a one-month contractual lag on fuel pass-through. The network kept widening — 82,768 partner agents against 52,225 a year earlier, while the P&L tightened.
None of that falsifies the mechanism… margin pressure from wages and fuel is orthogonal to which legs a network should own. But it is the reason the refusal matters. A company under near-term margin pressure that still declines the loudest available volume is either disciplined or wrong, and the difference is entirely whether the boundary it drew is real.
If a large quick-commerce platform in a dense Indian metro, one already past the density where owning is cheaper, signs and renews a meaningful third-party last-mile contract at stable rates, the mechanism is wrong. Not “early”, wrong. The whole claim rests on internalisation being an economic gradient rather than a strategic preference, and a renewal at density would show it is a preference after all.
A network business survives on the legs that get cheaper with pooled volume across many principals, and dies on the legs that get cheaper with dedicated volume from one, so the moment any single customer’s density crosses the ownership threshold, the third party should withdraw before the customer forces it out at a worse price.
Which is exactly what Delhivery is doing with quick commerce today, several years before it would have been made to.
