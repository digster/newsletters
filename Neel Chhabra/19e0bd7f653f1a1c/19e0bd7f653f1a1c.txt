The Rupture
When intelligence becomes abundant, scarcity becomes sacred. 
Six weeks. Three transactions. Five billion dollars.
In March 2026, the Aditya Birla Group, one of India’s oldest and most diversified conglomerates, a company that makes cement and sells fashion and runs a telecom network, paid $1.78 billion for a cricket team. Not for a stadium. Not for broadcast rights. Not for a player roster. For the right to steward a franchise: an idea, a color scheme, a tribal attachment accumulated across seventeen years of collective heartbreak and one championship. The very next month, the Mittal family, the world’s largest steel producer paid $1.65 billion for another cricket franchise, one that has never won a title in the last eight years and competes in a city of 4 million people in the Rajasthani desert. That same week, a venture capitalist, Josh Kushner in New York who built his career betting on artificial intelligence announced a new permanent capital vehicle, designed to hold assets forever, not flip them in a decade, and made a baseball team its inaugural investment, describing it as something with “qualities that cannot be replicated by technology.”
A venture capitalist. A man who has spent fifteen years betting that technology replicates everything. Announcing, in 2026, at the precise moment AI can generate a film, compose a symphony, write a legal brief, and diagnose a scan, that there exist things technology cannot touch. And choosing to structure permanent capital around them.
Abundance doesn’t diminish scarcity. It concentrates it, compressing all the world’s irreplaceable things into a narrower and narrower category that commands an ever-larger share of human attention, emotion, and capital.
The smartest money in the world just figured this out. They bought the things that cannot be made. They structured the ownership to last forever.
This is not a sports story. It is not a private equity story. It is a story about what the smartest capital in the world has figured out, simultaneously, about the nature of value in an age of abundance, and it requires going back to a Saturday afternoon in southeast London in 1886 to understand why they are right.
The Afternoon That Built Everything
In 1886, a group of blacksmiths and lathe operators at the Royal Arsenal in Woolwich, southeast London, received an unusual gift from industrial capitalism: their Saturday afternoons.
These men made weapons… cannon shells, gun carriages, the physical materiel of British imperial power. Their labor was total, relentless, defined by the kind of physical demand that leaves no margin for anything else. But the creeping efficiencies of the Industrial Revolution had quietly liberated something. Not much. Just Saturday afternoons. And human beings, it turns out, cannot tolerate unstructured time. So they pooled their shillings, bought a leather ball, and started kicking it around.
They had pivoted, without knowing it, from producing munitions to producing entertainment.
That club is now known as Arsenal F.C. Its current market valuation is approximately £2 billion. The weapons factory closed in 1967. The football club it produced has employed thousands, generated billions in economic activity, and created in millions of human beings the specific, unreasonable feeling that a result in north London on a Saturday evening is among the most emotionally significant events of their week.
Arsenal did not start as a business. It started as a few hours of surplus time that had to go somewhere.
This story is not a charming footnote. It is the operating manual for the $6 trillion industry that grew from the same impulse… for the extraction architecture that governs it, for the businesses within it that have escaped that architecture entirely, and for what artificial intelligence is about to do to the whole structure. But first you need to understand the conservation law that nobody names: the paradox baked into the structure of progress itself.
Every major productivity wave in the last five centuries has produced the same thing. Not abundance as its terminal state. Abundance as the engine that creates a new and fiercer scarcity.
The Pattern We Keep Failing to See
The error we make every time a major productivity wave arrives is consistent and predictable: we look at what the technology replaces, not at what the replacement creates.
Think about what a market actually is… a mechanism for discovering where genuine scarcity lies. Prices are not arbitrary social constructs; they are physical signals. When the cost of producing something collapses toward zero, the price follows it down. The question is never whether value migrates. It always does. The question is where it goes next.
The Industrial Revolution was supposed to produce mass unemployment. Instead it produced the weekend, which produced modern sports and music halls and seaside holidays and eventually the entire consumer entertainment complex that is now the defining economic category of the global middle class. The washing machine was supposed to make domestic labor redundant. Instead it gave women back hours that, accumulated across a generation, contributed to the largest expansion of the professional labor force in American history. The ATM was supposed to eliminate bank tellers. Instead it reduced the cost of running a branch sufficiently that banks opened more of them, and bank teller employment increased for two decades after ATMs were introduced.
The pattern is not that automation destroys work. The pattern is that automation frees human energy from one category of activity, and that energy flows immediately toward new categories that nobody predicted in advance, and that everyone insists on predicting anyway.
Nobody in 1890 predicted “social media manager.” Nobody in 1950 predicted “UX designer.” Nobody in 1970 predicted “personal trainer” as a distinct professional category. These jobs emerged from entirely new economic space that productivity opened, space that didn’t exist until the surplus did.
Think of global productivity as a champagne tower. Each new wave — steam, electricity, computing, internet, AI, is a new bottle poured at the top. The champagne fills the first tier: basic survival. Once full, it spills to the second: material comfort. Once full, to the third: experiential abundance. The Arsenal blacksmiths were the first drop of champagne reaching that third tier. The $6 trillion global leisure economy that exists today is that tier, still filling. AI is another bottle at the top, a large one, and it is about to pour a fourth tier into existence.
We are now inside another iteration. The technology is artificial intelligence. The scale is larger. The speed is faster. The anxiety is, by historical standards, exactly proportional to the size of the transformation coming. And the destination, judged by five centuries of consistent data, is a world in which human labor concentrates in the activities humans find most meaningful, because the activities they find routine have been handled by machines.
Two Kinds of Scarcity
Not all scarcity works the same way. Confusing them leads to dramatically different conclusions about where value accumulates.
The first kind is structural scarcity — things whose supply is fixed or genuinely shrinking relative to a rising tide of buyers. Land in the best locations. Trophies of civilisational heritage. The rarest objects, the oldest names, the most irreproducible experiences. These assets appreciate as a simple function of wealth concentration. The number of such things stays roughly constant. The number of people wealthy enough to want them, and the magnitude of their wealth, keeps compounding. The Top 100 wealthiest people’s combined fortune has grown roughly 10x in nominal terms since 2000. Sports franchise values that grew 2,000% in the same period are not a coincidence. They are arithmetic.
The second kind is relational scarcity — things that become valuable not because their supply is fixed in absolute terms, but because something adjacent has just become cheap. When modularity and commoditization cause attractive profits to disappear at one stage in a value chain, the opportunity to earn attractive profits emerges at an adjacent stage. Value doesn’t die. It relocates to wherever the new bottleneck forms. Google didn’t profit from browsing; it made browsing cheap and ubiquitous so that attention became the scarce asset it could monetize.
Federico da Montefeltro, the Duke of Urbino, understood this instinctively in the 15th century. His bookseller noted that in Montefeltro’s library, no printed volume had been admitted… printed books would have been ashamed in such company. The printing press was the defining technology of his era. He responded not by ignoring it, but by recognizing exactly what it had just made scarce: handwritten codices. He employed thirty to forty scribes for over a decade, producing the most irreproducible library in Europe. The result: a collection that still commands awe six centuries later.
He was building the anti-replicable thing in an age of increasing replication. This is not nostalgia. It is strategy. The interesting play, in any era, in any market, is to catch relational scarcity before the market prices it as scarcity, when it still looks like just another thing.
The $6 Trillion Structure
The leisure economy is the most direct expression of both scarcity types simultaneously. Built from productivity surplus, it now generates $6 trillion in annual consumer spend and constitutes the defining consumption category of the modern middle class.
Travel and hospitality ($1.9T) is the largest and the clearest demonstration of what happens when aggregators colonize discovery. Booking.com, Expedia, and Airbnb now intermediate the majority of online hotel bookings globally. The consequence: hotels earn volatile EBITDA margins of 5-25% while the platforms sitting between them and their customers earn 25-35%.
Food and dining ($1.5T) is the segment most visibly broken by platform economics. Restaurants earn 5-10% EBITDA on a good year. Food delivery platforms take 18-30% of gross order value. A restaurant earning 8% EBITDA that routes 40% of its orders through delivery apps charging 25% commission is, at the margin, working partly for the platform. This is not a negotiating failure. It is a structural consequence.
Entertainment and media ($900B) is bifurcated in a way that illuminates everything. Streaming platforms earn 20-30% operating margins by owning content at scale. But the sub-sector has a counter-example: live event entertainment, where the rights holder owns an unsubstitutable moment and the platforms queue to pay access fees. The Premier League. The IPL. Taylor Swift’s Eras Tour, which grossed over $1 billion in a single touring cycle by the simple mechanism of being something that cannot be experienced any other way.
Gaming ($220B, growing at 10%+ annually) is the purest version of the leisure economy’s identity architecture. In-game cosmetic economies earn margins approaching 80% on items with zero instrumental utility, selling in the tens of millions because the player has become a particular kind of person, and the skin is the visible signal of that to their peer group.
Look at what is booming across all of these in real time. Women’s sports, which had essentially no commercial existence twenty years ago, are attracting billion-dollar broadcast deals. Formula 1, contracting through the 2010s, exploded after a Netflix documentary gave it narrative access to a new generation. Video gaming generates more revenue than film and music combined. None of this is accidental. All of it is the third tier filling in your lifetime.
The Extraction Architecture
The global leisure industry runs on a two-tier extraction architecture so consistent across every sub-sector that it amounts to a structural law.
Tier one is the operator layer. Hotels, restaurants, gyms, airlines, theme parks… the businesses that actually deliver the experience. Capital-intensive, labor-intensive, fully exposed to the cost of physical delivery. A bad review halves their bookings. A kitchen fire closes them for a month.
Tier two is the aggregator layer. Booking.com, Zomato, Spotify, YouTube, Airbnb, Steam, Ticketmaster. These businesses do not deliver experiences. They own the moment five minutes before the experience, when a human being is in mild uncertainty about what to do, where to go, what to watch, where to stay. They own the desire before the object of desire has been chosen. That moment, captured at global scale, earns 20-35% EBITDA. The aggregator earns more than the operator’s margin on the same transaction. The operator did all the work.
What is genuinely scarce, and therefore genuinely valuable, is not the experience itself but the access point to the human wanting of it. The aggregator owns the want. Everything that flows through it pays a toll. [ https://open.substack.com/pub/neelchhabra/p/the-faucet-economy-why-control-of?r=14cab&utm_campaign=post-expanded-share&utm_medium=web ]
The only escape from the extraction architecture is to own something the aggregator cannot intermediate: IP that is live, scarce, and unsubstitutable. The BCCI cricket rights work because 300 million Indians will reorganize their evening around a live match, and no algorithm can offer them a cheaper alternative. Disney’s mythology is so embedded in global culture that parents choosing a streaming service for their child do not make that choice on interface quality, they make it on whether the service contains Disney. These operators have reversed the power relationship: the aggregators compete to pay them access fees, rather than the reverse. This is worth, practically, the difference between 10-20% and 30-50% EBITDA margins.
The Paradox at the Center
Josef Pieper wrote Leisure: The Basis of Culture in 1948, in bombed-out postwar Germany, as a deliberate act of resistance against the total work society, the world organized entirely around productive output, where rest is merely the recuperation required to return to production. For Pieper, genuine leisure was not the absence of work. It was a fundamentally different mode of being: receptive, contemplative, present without agenda. An end in itself. Aristotle was earlier and more systematic: skholē is not the reward for labor. It is the point of it. You work so that you can be free.
Here is the paradox that the $6 trillion leisure industry has spent seventy years carefully not naming.
Genuine leisure would destroy the business model.
Not reduce it. Not complicate it. Destroy it. A person who has genuinely arrived at what Pieper means, at receptive stillness, at the absence of the imperative to produce or optimize or document or improve, does not need another subscription. Does not need another destination. Does not need to be notified, gamified, upsold, or reminded that a premium tier exists. They have arrived somewhere the industry cannot follow, because the industry’s entire architecture is built on departure, not arrival.
So what the industry sells, with growing craft and psychological precision, is the feeling of approaching leisure while engineering, at a structural level, the conditions that make actual arrival impossible. Every notification that interrupts your vacation is not a product failure. Every resort that schedules activities so tightly that stillness never has room to appear is not a design flaw. Every fitness app that has converted rest into a gap in your streak… a streak conditioned, through variable reward schedules and social comparison, to feel like a possession worth protecting, is working exactly as designed.
The product is not leisure. The product is the permanent, productive approach toward leisure. The industry sells the journey and quietly ensures you never arrive.
This is the deepest insight available about a $6 trillion economy. The surface level is the margin structures and the aggregator dynamics and the rights-holder premiums. Beneath the surface is this: the business model requires that the gap between wanting leisure and having it remains permanently open. And the businesses that understand this most precisely, not as a moral failing, but as a structural feature, have built something the conventional margin analysis cannot explain, because what they are selling is not an experience at all.
Four Tiers of Competition
For most of consumer history, businesses competed on three levels. Commodities competed on price. Services competed on quality. Experiences competed on memory.
Pine and Gilmore [ https://substack.com/redirect/fd14d954-a983-421d-8ba4-569519f451ae?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] identified the shift to experiences as the frontier of value creation in 1998. They were right. They undershot.
Above experiences is a fourth tier that the prior three cannot explain: identity. Not what the product makes you feel. Who the product makes you become.
A commodity is fungible. A service is substitutable. An experience is comparable… you can rank them, choose the better one next time. But identity is the only consumption that creates its own demand, because once a product has successfully colonized your self-concept, discontinuing it doesn’t feel like an economic decision. It feels like an amputation.
When you have become a golfer, not someone who plays golf, but someone for whom golf is woven into how you understand yourself, stopping playing is not a lifestyle adjustment. It is an identity crisis. When you have become embedded in a community with its own celebrities, its own rituals, its own social grammar, and its implicit claim about the kind of person you are, discontinuing is not cancelling a subscription. It is becoming a slightly lesser version of yourself.
Call this an existential switching cost. It operates at the level of self-concept. No rational calculation overcomes it. You cannot spreadsheet your way out of who you are.
The businesses with the highest long-run returns in leisure have, almost without exception, built at the fourth tier. They are not in the product business. They are in the identity business wearing a product costume. The product is the occasion. The identity is what they are actually selling.
The Identity Architecture: Three Cases
Marvel does not sell movies. It took Disney paying $4.24 billion for it in 2009, and the subsequent $30 billion in global MCU box office, to demonstrate it at the scale financial analysis requires.
The MCU is a mythology — a shared narrative universe so internally consistent, so dense with callbacks and continuity and references only initiates can decode, that consuming it is an act of membership rather than entertainment. When you see a Marvel film on opening weekend, having watched the trailer breakdowns, followed the casting announcements, participated in fan theory communities for months, you are not being entertained. You are performing your belonging to a community that understands things other people don’t. The film is the ritual occasion. The identity is the product.
This is precisely why “Marvel fatigue” discourse missed the mechanism entirely. Critics argued the films had declined in quality, mostly true, and predicted audience abandonment. What happened was demographic separation: casual consumers attrited, because they were buying experiences and the experiences got worse. The core community remained, because they were not buying experiences. They were maintaining an identity. You can stop watching films. You cannot stop being a Marvel person without becoming someone who used to be one, which is a different, slightly diminished self-narrative that costs something to accept.
Nike. Nike has been running the same architecture for fifty years. The shoe business is competitive: rubber, foam, textile, dozens of capable competitors. Nike’s gross margin runs around 45%. The products are not categorically better than Adidas or New Balance. What Nike has is what fifty years of capital and creativity built: the self-image of someone who tries.
“Just Do It” does not describe the shoe. It describes the person wearing it. The athlete who runs six miles before work and the person who has been meaning to start exercising for three years are addressed by the same three words, because the aspiration and the achievement are both legible as the same identity. Nike sells the self-image of trying. The shoe is the membership card. Membership, unlike a shoe, does not wear out.
Four Seasons. The room is not categorically superior to an equivalent luxury competitor, the amenities are comparable, the service professionally similar. What is incomparable is what staying there signals, to others and more importantly to yourself, about who you are and what your relationship to your own resources means.
The Four Seasons sells the version of yourself who belongs in a room like that. Once you have purchased that self-image, the alternative… discovering you are the kind of person who stays somewhere else, costs something beyond the room rate.
The structural law all three demonstrate: the capital-light, high-ROIC businesses in leisure are uniformly identity businesses. Disney earns returns that look like software because the parks and films are not the product, the mythology is, and the parks and films are ritual access points to it. The park could burn and the mythology would survive. The mythology could dissolve and no renovation would save the business.
Three Leagues, One Architecture
The clearest proof of the identity architecture operating at civilisational scale exists in sports leagues, and three leagues, wildly different in sport, history, and geography, demonstrate the same underlying structure.
The NFL and the product of drama. The National Football League did not become America’s dominant entertainment property because American football is objectively the best sport. It became dominant through specific institutional design that produces a particular kind of irreplaceable cultural experience.
A sports league is not selling athletic performance. It is selling dramatic uncertainty at scale. The value proposition is not “watch the best players compete” — you can get that from a highlight reel. The value proposition is: experience, alongside your entire community, a moment whose outcome is genuinely unknown. That collective, live, uncertain experience generates the mythology, the shared memory, the identity attachment that makes a league irreplaceable.
The NFL’s mechanisms to protect this drama… revenue sharing that prevents rich teams from permanently dominating, a draft system routing the best new talent to the weakest teams, look like economic communism. They are product design decisions. The product is drama. Drama requires uncertainty. Uncertainty requires competitive balance. Competitive balance requires redistribution. The owners voluntarily suppress their individual competitive advantage to protect the product they are all collectively selling. A century of this compounding has produced something with no analog in the technology world: not just market share, but calendar share. The Super Bowl is the most-watched annual event in America not because it is the best television but because watching it together is part of what it means to be American. You cannot replicate it in a decade. You cannot replicate it with abundant intelligence. You can only wait, which is to say, you cannot replicate it at all.
The IPL and redirected meaning. The Indian Premier League is the most extraordinary case study in the deliberate construction of irreplaceable cultural attachment that has ever occurred in sport, because it happened faster than anyone thought possible.
The IPL was not built from nothing. It was built on top of something that already had enormous depth: Indian cricket’s century-long relationship with the subcontinent, a religious attachment forged through colonial history, national identity, and the singular experience of watching a billion people care about the same eleven men simultaneously. The sport was already identity infrastructure. What the IPL’s architects did was not create meaning but redirect existing meaning, take the extraordinary attachment Indians had to cricket and give it a new vessel: city-based franchise competition, condensed to three hours, playable at night, designed for television, layered with Bollywood glamour.
The T20 format is itself a product design insight. Test cricket — five days, stately pace, designed for a leisure class that no longer exists, is the game’s highest form. But, Test cricket gave way to T20 when primetime became the primary distribution medium. One hundred and twenty balls per side. Three hours total. Every ball carrying consequence. The format compresses cricket’s strategic depth into an entertainment atom precisely sized for the modern evening. But the format is not the moat. Formats can be copied. The IPL’s moat is the specific meaning accumulated around its franchises in their specific cities through seventeen years of championships and collapses.
Now notice the demographic asymmetry markets haven’t fully priced: the NFL’s audience is constrained by American demographics, which are relatively stable. The IPL’s audience is constrained by Indian demographics, which are not. The per-match broadcast rights gap between the two leagues will close, not because the IPL’s product improves but because the purchasing power of the people watching it keeps rising.
Formula One and the accidental mythology. Formula One’s moat is the combination of engineering complexity with cultural layering that took seven decades to accumulate. The sport exists at the intersection of three things that have no business being together: the dangerous, obsessional precision of engineering competition; the luxury and glamour of Monaco; and the gladiatorial romanticism of drivers who spend ninety minutes at six Gs making thousands of micro-decisions at speeds that don’t permit a second thought. You could build a racing series with equivalent complexity. You could build one with equivalent glamour. You cannot build the specific combination… assembled over seven decades, with specific team rivalries and driver eras that defined generations. What Liberty Media understood when they acquired Formula One in 2017 was not that the sport needed a different product. It needed a different distribution strategy, one that treated accumulated meaning as inventory to be unlocked for audiences that had never been given access to it. The technology was the mechanism of legibility, not the source of the value. The value was always there.
Three leagues, three different sports, three different histories, one architecture. In each case, the value being unlocked was not created by the operator. It was already there, accumulated through decades of human attention, sitting in a structure that hadn’t yet figured out how to express it commercially. The operators were discoverers, not creators, people who recognized arrangement value the market hadn’t yet priced. The discovery is always the highest-return moment in the lifecycle of an irreplaceable asset.
The Participation Economy: You Are the Product. You Just Paid to Be.
If the leagues demonstrate identity architecture at institutional scale, Hyrox demonstrates it at the individual scale, and the inversion it reveals is more radical than anything the leagues have done.
Hyrox Bengaluru charged ₹9,000 a ticket and made ₹8 crore in a single weekend. Seventy-five percent came from participants. Twenty-five percent from sponsors. That ratio is a confession that something fundamental has changed.
Every entertainment business ever built — IPL, multiplex, concert, comedy show, ran on the opposite structure. Sponsors and advertisers paid the majority; the audience paid a fraction. The passive viewer was the product being sold to brands. The logic was simple: aggregate eyeballs, charge for access to them.
Hyrox inverted this. The participants are the audience. They paid ₹9,000 for the privilege of being watched… by cameras, by timing systems, by each other, and eventually by everyone on their Instagram feed. The sponsor is almost secondary. They’re buying adjacency to a self-actualization moment, which turns out to be worth more per impression than adjacency to someone watching a cricketer hit a six.
This shift isn’t fitness-specific. Run clubs in Delhi, Bandra, and Koramangala. Weekend treks billed as vacations. Cohort-based courses where the cohort is the product. Pottery studios. Supper clubs. Pickleball and padel courts booked weeks in advance. What these have in common isn’t sweat or community or wellness. What they have in common is that they sell becoming, not watching.
This matters because of something that happened to credentials. For two generations, the Indian middle class used educational and professional achievements as the primary technology for signaling who they were. Which college. Which company. Which MBA. But credentials inflated. When every second LinkedIn profile has the same tier-1 degree and the same consulting stint, the signal degrades. People needed new identity infrastructure… ways of communicating character that couldn’t simply be purchased with money or replicated with effort alone.
Physical and experiential accomplishment filled the void. You cannot buy a Hyrox finish time. You cannot fake a Himalayan summit. You can only earn them. That non-purchasability is what makes them valuable signals. The ₹9,000 entry fee is not an entertainment expense. It is a capital allocation toward a specific self.
The smartphone made this economy structurally viable. Every participation experience now generates an artifact… a finish time, a summit photo, a pottery bowl on a shelf, that compounds its social value over time. The Hyrox bib picture circulates for weeks. A concert photo is just a photo. A marathon finish photo is evidence of character. The artifact has positive ROI in social capital, which means the participation experience has a duration that access experiences don’t.
In the long run, who actually captures the rent? Not the event organizers — event businesses are operationally intensive, geographically constrained, and relentlessly commoditizable. The second Hyrox-competitor enters Bengaluru, the pricing pressure begins. Not the venue operators. The rent accrues to whoever owns the longitudinal identity record, the archive of becoming. Strava knows your entire athletic biography. That’s a social graph with physical roots. Stickier than a digital network because the underlying experience is embodied. More durable than a fitness app because it’s anchored to real events, real timing chips, real humans who saw you finish.
The deepest shift isn’t economic. It’s ontological. For decades, the Indian middle class was defined by what it consumed… what school, what car, what neighborhood. The participation economy is a bet that the next generation will be defined by what it does, and more specifically, by what it becomes. That bet, if it lands, will be worth considerably more than ₹8 crore.
The Atoms-Bits Inversion
As bits deflate, atoms inflate.
AI can produce a thousand movie scripts in an afternoon, ten thousand songs in a day, a million images in an hour… all competent, all technically proficient, all forgettable. The marginal cost of content creation is approaching zero. This does not disrupt the leisure economy. It sorts it.
As digital becomes the substrate — ubiquitous, ambient, forgettable… physical presence becomes the luxury good. The status symbol. The thing people pay for not despite its scarcity but because of it. A $300,000 Super Bowl package is not absurd excess. It is the rational market clearing price for something that cannot be streamed, downloaded, or replicated: being physically present at the most-watched event in America. Live sports, concerts, conferences, these aren’t competing with streaming. They are in a completely different category. One is information. The other is experience. You cannot torrent an experience.
In an infinite sea of synthetic content, the value of authentic cultural provenance grows exponentially, not linearly. The gap doesn’t merely widen, it compounds. Because the more content exists, the harder it is to cut through the noise. And the only things that cut through are those with genuine cultural weight behind them.
Think of AI content as currency inflation. If a government prints an infinite amount of new cash, each bill becomes effectively worthless. The feed floods. The signal drowns in noise. But in that same hyperinflationary economy, gold — original, authentic IP with established cultural resonance, acts as a store of value. The more paper money that floods the system, the more valuable and stable the gold becomes, because it cannot be printed on demand.
The companies positioned as gold reserves — Disney, Nintendo, the major studios with libraries stretching back decades, the sports leagues whose rights appreciate with each cycle, are the central banks of culture, holding reserves that become more valuable precisely as everyone else prints increasingly worthless paper.
Don’t think of Disney as an entertainment company that needs to constantly produce new hits. Think of it as a cultural bank with a vault full of appreciating assets — Mickey, Star Wars, Marvel, Pixar classics, that become more valuable as AI makes competing content worthless. The counter-positioning is perfect: AI lowers the cost of creating new content to near-zero, which makes established content with proven cultural resonance the only thing worth paying premium prices for.
The Scarcity That Cannot Be Automated
René Girard named the central property of the fourth tier: mimetic desire. Our sense of self is constructed almost entirely in relation to other humans. We want what others want not because we independently evaluated it but because the wanting of it by others signals its value to us. This is the mechanism by which social beings navigate the world, and it means that as material scarcity decreases, as AI handles more of the logistics of survival and production, what we increasingly compete for is not goods or services or even experiences. It is attention, status, belonging, and the sense that our specific existence matters to the specific humans whose regard we care about.
This is the scarcity of human intention. It is, structurally and permanently, the one scarcity that cannot be automated.
You can automate content production. You cannot automate the feeling that a specific person wrote something specifically for you, with you in mind, because they thought you would find it beautiful. You can automate medical diagnostics. You cannot automate the experience of a doctor genuinely present with you in your fear, who sees you rather than your scan. You can automate education delivery at scale. You cannot automate the moment a teacher finds your specific confusion interesting and stays because they want to see you understand.
These are not soft claims. They are economic predictions about where value accumulates as AI handles everything else. The demand for real human attention grows precisely as the supply of automated attention becomes overwhelming, in the same way that demand for artisan goods expanded when industrial production made identical goods cheap. Scarcity commands premium. Human intention, as the last irreducibly scarce input in an economy of abundance, will command an extraordinary one.
The highest-value human work of the future is not helping other humans produce. It is helping other humans feel… feel seen, understood, that their specific existence registers to another consciousness as worth attending to.
India: The Undiscovered Geometry
Every structural argument in this essay applies to India with amplified force, because India is living through the same transition the developed world lived through over a century, compressed into a single generation. The leisure economy here is not mature and monetized. It is early and forming, structurally underpenetrated, dramatically undermonetized relative to the passion it contains, and positioned at the beginning of an income inflection that will compound for the next fifteen years.
The screen poverty thesis. India has approximately 10,000 multiplex screens for 1.4 billion people — 7 screens per million. China has 57. The US has 140. This is not a cultural preference; it is a supply constraint that has suppressed cinema attendance for decades. PVR Inox, India’s dominant multiplex operator post-merger, is the clearest listed proxy for this thesis. The market prices it as a cyclical exhibitor facing structural disruption from OTT, which is the correct description of the wrong thesis. The OTT threat is real for casual, solo viewing. It does not threaten the social viewing behavior that drives Indian cinema’s cultural function, which is closer to what a stadium does than what a television does.
More importantly: PVR’s actual business is not admission fees. It is the food and beverage margin — 40%+ gross on the captive consumer who has already committed to spending two hours in a physical box. The admission is the distribution mechanism. The F&B is the product. Understood correctly, PVR is a social experience infrastructure operator with a recurring captive consumption model, priced as a disruption victim.
The sports monetization gap. The IPL’s per-match viewership exceeds the Super Bowl’s US domestic audience. India watches each of 74 IPL matches with Super Bowl intensity. The BCCI controls rights to a property that is, on a per-viewer-per-hour basis, the most valuable entertainment IP on earth. And yet the total monetization of Indian cricket — broadcast rights, merchandise, betting, fantasy sports, live attendance, is a fraction of what an equivalent property generates in the US, UK, or Brazil.
The monetization infrastructure hasn’t caught up with the passion. It is catching up rapidly: IPL rights went from ₹16,000 crore per year in 2017 to ₹48,000 crore across five years from 2023, a near-3x increase in six years. That trajectory does not reverse. It accelerates as income growth moves more of the mass market past the willingness-to-pay threshold and digital payment infrastructure makes micro-transactions frictionless.
The catalog undervaluation: Saregama [ https://substack.com/redirect/5f4395bd-307b-4b32-92ad-12186d2327d5?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o ] as the India gold reserve. Western private equity has spent the last decade buying music catalogs at 20-30x annual royalties because the mathematics is simple: a catalog is a perpetual royalty engine that appreciates as streaming penetration grows. The same framework applied to Indian catalogs produces an opportunity that has not yet been systematically arbitraged.
Saregama owns master rights to over 5 million songs — approximately 50% of all the music ever recorded in India, spanning from early HMV recordings in the 1930s through the Golden Era of Hindi cinema to contemporary releases. The voices of Lata Mangeshkar in 1965. The compositions of R.D. Burman in 1975. These assets are finite, immortal, and currently being repriced by the forces of digitalization and artificial intelligence.
Master rights. Perpetual copyright. Zero marginal cost of reproduction. Three facts that make Saregama structurally unlike anything else on the Indian exchange. A song recorded in 1960 has infinite shelf life and zero marginal cost of reproduction. Once the initial recording cost is amortized, which happened decades ago, every subsequent stream, every sync in a film or advertisement, is pure gross profit minus platform fees. Unlike a factory that depreciates or a patent that expires, copyright in India lasts 60 years after the death of the author. For a corporate owner like Saregama, the rights are effectively perpetual in terms of economic relevance.
The AI dynamic makes this moat deeper, not shallower. The proliferation of synthetic music accelerates the flight to authenticity. As feeds fill with algorithmically generated audio, human-curated playlists featuring real songs from established artists become more valuable. Saregama doesn’t compete with AI. AI makes Saregama’s moat deeper.
The bear thesis — “AI will eventually create music equally resonant”  fails at the mechanism. The issue is not technical quality. It is temporal priority. “Roop Tera Mastana” is not valuable because it is musically sophisticated. It is valuable because it was released in 1972 and has accumulated over fifty years of cultural capital, in the memories of everyone who heard it as a child, in the nostalgia of everyone who heard their parents play it, in the cultural substrate of three generations of Indian listeners. By the time AI creates something equally resonant, we are in a different era with a new generation carrying their own nostalgia for whatever became established during their formative years. AI cannot manufacture the passage of time. It cannot create memories in people who weren’t born yet. It cannot make you remember something from a childhood that hasn’t happened.
When Saregama pulled its catalog from Meta’s platforms in a licensing dispute, Meta came back to the table. This proved that an Indian social platform cannot function without the Saregama catalog, which is the definition of a chokepoint. The platform that needs the content more than the content needs the platform loses the negotiation, every time.
The most underappreciated dimension of the Saregama thesis is not the catalog. It is the Pocket Aces acquisition, and what it reveals about where the company is actually going. Saregama acquired a majority stake in Pocket Aces — a digital entertainment company controlling FilterCopy, Dice Media, and Gobble, channels with over 120 million followers. This transforms Saregama from a B2B IP owner monetizing a legacy catalog through platform licensing into a B2C media powerhouse with a direct engagement pipe to Gen Z. In an algorithmic world, the influencer is the gatekeeper. Owning the gatekeeper ensures priority placement. Content is king. Distribution is god. Engagement is truth. Saregama is building the full stack.
India’s streaming market currently sits at 1-2% paid penetration. Global averages are 40-50%. A paid subscriber is worth approximately 10x a free user in licensing revenue per stream. Even moving from 1% to 5% paid penetration, a fraction of the global average, would create a non-linear explosion in Saregama’s licensing revenue from the same underlying catalog, with zero additional content investment. The company is positioned as a call option on the Indian consumer’s willingness to pay. The land under the metropolis doesn’t need to do anything. It just needs to be there as the city grows around it.
The Steelman
The bear case deserves its full weight before the conclusion.
The strongest version of the bear argument is not that AI generates substitutes for entertainment content, that is true but incomplete. The strongest version is this: identity formation itself is being disrupted. TikTok has demonstrated that communities organize around algorithmically curated content faster than they organize around legacy institutions. Gen Z builds identity in months around new things, not decades around old ones. If the switching cost argument depends on identity being slow to form and hard to dissolve, and if that premise fails for a generation that has trained itself to form and dissolve identities fluidly, then the moat disappears, and the entire architecture collapses.
This is the best version of the bear case. Hold it seriously for a moment.
Now apply the stress test. You have abundant intelligence, unlimited capital, and genuine intent. Build the next NFL. Build the next IPL. Build the next Glastonbury. The infrastructure is achievable. The talent is acquirable. The production quality is matchable. What you cannot do is make it matter. Mattering is not a product quality. It is the residue of shared experience accumulated across time by a community that decided, collectively, to invest its identity in this thing. You cannot accelerate the accumulation of that meaning. You can only wait, and participating in the waiting requires having started a very long time ago.
The TikTok counter also fails at the second order. Yes, communities form faster around algorithmic content. But they dissolve faster too. The identity architecture of a Marvel or an IPL franchise is not just about switching costs at any single moment, it is about generational transfer. Children inherit their parents’ teams. The IPL franchise loyalties being built in households today are being handed to the next generation before the first generation has finished caring. Algorithmic identity is fluid; inherited identity is structural. TikTok trends last months. Arsenal has lasted 140 years.
The deflationary wave makes this clearer, not murkier. When algorithmically generated content floods every screen and every feed, the human need for meaning and distinction does not diminish. It intensifies. The contrast between infinite generated content and the singular, irreplaceable experience of genuine belonging clarifies what was always true: the leisure economy’s highest-value product was never the content. It was the community organized around the content. The shared language, the generational inheritance, the rituals that make an institution feel like home. These are the irreproducibles. The flood makes them precious.
The Arsenal Conclusion
Johan Huizinga’s insight, in Homo Ludens, was that play is not something civilization produces as a byproduct of surplus. Play is what civilization is made of. The rituals, the contests, the shared performances of meaning, these are not leisure activities that humans invented once they had enough to eat. They are the mechanism by which human beings construct the shared reality that makes collective life possible at all. Civilization is not what we build during our productive hours. It is what we play at together in our leisure ones.
The blacksmiths of Woolwich did not know they were building anything. They knew only that they had a Saturday afternoon, and something in them, some irreducibly human compulsion toward play, toward belonging, toward the shared construction of something that mattered, required filling it with something alive. Two billion people now watch football. A meaningful fraction of them feel, with a specificity they could not fully explain, that the players playing it are theirs — their club, their colors, their belonging to something that exists beyond them and yet is somehow, fundamentally, about them.
That feeling is what was created in Woolwich in 1886 with a handful of spare shillings and a leather ball. It is worth, at last count, approximately £6.7 billion in annual Premier League broadcast rights. That is the champagne tower, mid-pour.
The weapons factory always closes. The Saturday afternoon always remains. And the identity that forms around what people do with that afternoon turns out to be worth more than the weapons factory ever was. Not eventually. Structurally.
Because it is the only product that compounds rather than depreciates with use. The only consumption that creates rather than satisfies its own demand. The only scarcity that grows more valuable as everything else becomes abundant.
This is simultaneously the investment thesis and its limit. The thesis: identify the businesses that own genuine belonging… the cultural institutions, iconic franchises, irreplaceable venues, catalog assets, and platform positions that have been accumulating meaning in continuous chains of human custody since before the current competitive landscape existed, and own them before the AI-driven deflationary wave clarifies just how scarce they are. The market has not yet fully priced the bifurcation. The businesses that will compound through the coming decade of content deflation are not the ones with the best algorithm. They are the ones that cannot be algorithmically replaced, because what they are selling is not content. It is the record of human time and attention that no algorithm can generate, because generating it requires the one thing AI cannot provide: actual decades of actual human beings actually caring.
In India, that thesis has a second dimension that makes it more compelling still. India is not just underpenetrated in the monetization of its leisure economy. It is early in the formation of the identity architecture that the global thesis depends on. The IPL franchise loyalties that will produce existential switching costs three generations from now are being built right now, in households across the country, as children inherit their parents’ teams. Saregama’s century of recorded Indian music sits on a balance sheet that most institutional investors have not yet priced through the catalog lens that Western PE has applied to equivalent assets abroad. The witnessing machines, the multiplex chains, the live event infrastructure, the premium sports venues, are being built into a market with demand that far exceeds current supply, at the beginning of an income curve that will not stop compounding for a generation.
Booking Holdings earns 25% margins because it owns the desire before it becomes a booking. Disney earns software returns because its mythology is the product and the parks are merely where the mythology becomes physical. Hyrox inverted the entire model: the audience pays to be the product. Every one of these facts is the same fact, seen from a different angle.
The game in leisure is not product quality. It is not operational excellence. It is not aggregator market share or IP rights or take-rate dynamics, though all of those are proxies for the real thing.
The real thing is this: whoever owns the desire owns the economics. And desire, at the level that actually compounds, is not a want for a product. It is a want to be a particular kind of person.
The leisure industry is the most profitable solution to a problem it requires you to keep having. The problem is the question every human being wakes up with in a world of sufficient material abundance: who am I, and does it matter?
The industry cannot answer that question. But it has become extraordinarily good at selling the feeling that the next subscription, the next destination, the next experience, the next identity costume… just slightly better than the last one, will.
The scarcest asset in an age of abundance is not what you can buy. It is what you can make someone want to become.
Run the stress test on the things that pass it. The demand is infinite. The supply of genuinely irreplaceable things that can meet it is fixed by history and time.
That asymmetry is the investment. The rest will compound on its own.
