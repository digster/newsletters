“The whole of the advantages and disadvantages of the different employments of labour and stock must, in the same neighbourhood, be either perfectly equal or continually tending to equality.”
— Adam Smith, The Wealth of Nations, Book I, Chapter X, 1776
Smith’s “stock” meant capital. His “neighbourhood” meant anywhere that capital could reach. Two and a half centuries later the neighbourhood is a cement dealer’s counter in small-town India, and the law still holds.
Smith gave us the law. A German forester gave us the gauge. In 1849 Martin Faustmann asked a plain question: is this land worth more as a working forest than it costs to buy bare? Divide the first value by the second. Above one, plant trees. Below one, don’t. Mark Spitznagel, in The Dao of Capital, carries the Faustmann ratio out of the forest and into the stock market as market capitalisation over net worth. Economists know the same idea as James Tobin’s q. When the ratio sits above one, building beats buying, and capital should pour in until the gap closes. When it sits at nine, the invitation stops being polite.
Nature got there first. A bee does not wander from flower to flower at random. Eric Charnov’s marginal value theorem (1976) describes how a forager stays wherever the energy gained per unit of effort beats the average on offer, and leaves the moment it doesn’t. Flowers advertise their nectar with colour and scent. Listed companies advertise theirs with return on capital: quarterly, audited, and free to anyone with a broadband connection.
A high market cap relative to capital employed is a signal. Signals get read by whoever is hungry enough, and large enough, to act on them.
Paints. Jewellery. Wires and cables. In under three years the Aditya Birla Group alone has walked into all three, while JSW, Adani and Reliance work the same flower beds. The popular reading calls this empire-building: promoters chasing size, a conglomerate discount on the way. That reading gets the motive wrong. These entries are arithmetic, and arithmetic tells you where the bees fly next.
Graham and Dodd wrote the warning into Security Analysis in 1934, and Marathon Asset Management built a whole philosophy on it decades later:
“A business which sells at a premium does so because it earns a large return upon its capital; this large return attracts competition; and generally speaking, it is not likely to continue indefinitely.”
— Benjamin Graham & David Dodd, Security Analysis, 1934
A shorter version: “high returns tend to attract capital, just as low returns repel it.” The capital cycle is just Smith’s law with a timetable attached.
Start with the ratio Faustmann would recognise. Market cap divided by capital employed (equity plus debt) is a rough, public-filings cousin of it; counting debt makes it the stricter of the two. Asian Paints is valued at ₹2.31 lakh crore on about ₹25,300 crore of capital: roughly 9x. Pidilite runs at 13x. Page Industries, which makes Jockey innerwear under licence, runs at 23x.
Each of those multiples has two parts. One part is genuine scarcity: a patent, a process that took twenty years to learn, a network that gets better with every user. The other part can be rebuilt with money: factories, a dealer network, a brand assembled from decades of advertising. Warren Buffett, writing in his 2007 letter about businesses whose advantage has to be renewed again and again, put it this way:
“A moat that must be continuously rebuilt will eventually be no moat at all.”
The second part is exactly that kind of moat. It holds because nobody with a large enough balance sheet has yet decided to rebuild it. The moment someone does, the multiple stops being a measure of the incumbent’s strength and becomes a quote: here is what the market will pay for this position, and here is what it costs to build one.
When I wrote about barriers to entry in [my series on competitive strategy](https://substack.com/redirect/a6ad2821-597c-4f4c-9d85-29e252f084ee?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o), the line I kept returning to was Charlie Munger’s: “We buy barriers. Building them is tough… If you’re buying something at a huge discount to its replacement value and it is hard to replace, you have a big advantage.” Turn that sentence inside out and you have the conglomerate’s rule. If the market values something at a huge premium to replacement value, and it is easy to replace, you build.
Peter Thiel’s split, which opened [the first essay in that series](https://substack.com/redirect/874efd71-c5be-4454-a4b9-3987f49fe2fe?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o), sharpens the point. A business creates X of value and captures Y% of it, and the two move independently. A 9x multiple on a can of paint is mostly a bet on Y, and Y is what entrants come for.
Read the circles as a bee would. Grasim committed ₹10,000 crore to Birla Opus to contest a category whose leader alone is worth ₹2.31 lakh crore. The group committed ₹5,000 crore to Indriya against Titan's ₹4.07 lakh crore. UltraTech committed ₹1,800 crore to Ultravolt against Polycab's ₹1.21 lakh crore. When Ultravolt launched last month, about ₹13,500 crore of market value wiped off listed cable makers. That is 7.6 times the plant's entire budget. The market priced the threat at a multiple of the threat's cost. That ratio is the nectar.
For three decades Asian Paints was the textbook Indian compounder. Return on capital employed sat at 42% in FY15 and was still 38% in FY24. The usual explanation was brand, and brand was part of it. The bigger part was a system: a demand-forecasting engine, a tinting machine in tens of thousands of shops, and dealers who had learned that stocking a rival cost more than it paid.
Birla Opus went on sale in March 2024 with something no paint entrant had brought before: 1,332 million litres of annual capacity across six plants, about 40% of the industry’s existing capacity, added in one go. Kumar Mangalam Birla framed the venture as a contradiction on purpose:
“Birla Opus should be seen as a scale start-up incubated by the Aditya Birla Group. It uniquely combines the agility, energy, and frugality of a start-up with the muscle, reliability, and brand strength of a storied and dynamic conglomerate.”
— Kumar Mangalam Birla, Chairman, Aditya Birla Group
Then Opus went after the counter rather than the consumer. It gave tinting machines away free where dealers normally pay ₹1.5–2 lakh for one, offered dealers margins of 12–16% against about 4% from Asian Paints, and stretched credit from five days to twenty.
Charlie Munger’s line, “Show me the incentive and I will show you the outcome,” rarely gets a cleaner test. A Kolkata dealer told, that Asian Paints made up 70% of his paint sales in 2023 and 30% in 2024. Asian Paints’ total share has fallen from 59% to 52% in the twelve months to March 2025. Asian Paints CEO Amit Syngle called it what it was:
“In a market which is already slow, the intensity of competitive action has been much more. I think it is a double-whammy.”
— Amit Syngle, MD & CEO, Asian Paints
The most revealing document in the whole episode is a regulatory one. In July 2025 the Competition Commission of India ordered an investigation after finding a prima facie case of abuse of dominance. Grasim’s complaint alleged that Asian Paints threatened dealers who stocked Opus with credit cuts, inflated targets and lost perks, and told them to refuse Opus tinting machines. The allegations are still allegations. But notice what they assume. If the moat had lived in the consumer’s attachment to the brand, nobody would have needed to police the dealer. The moat lived at the counter, and a counter can be rented.
Botanists have a name for what Opus did. Some bumblebees, faced with a long tubular flower, don’t crawl in through the front where the pollen waits. They bite a hole at the base and drink the nectar from the side. Ecologists call it nectar robbing. The flower’s defences (its shape, its colour, its scent) all face forward, toward the visitor it expects. Asian Paints’ defences faced the homeowner: the advertising, the colour cards, the brand. Opus came in through the side, at the dealer, where the nectar was actually stored.
The JSW Group took the other door. JSW Paints launched in 2019, stayed small, and in June 2025 agreed to buy Akzo Nobel India, the Dulux business, for close to ₹13,000 crore including the open offer. “Coming together with Dulux takes us into the top four,” said Parth Jindal. Tobin’s q runs in both directions: when rebuilding is too slow, you buy someone whose market price sits closer to its rebuild cost.
Here is where the investor should look twice at the attacker rather than the defender. Grasim does not break out the paint loss, but Birla Opus is still weighing on group profits, and Grasim’s MD set out the order of priorities on the Q4 FY26 call:
“The priority is to become the number two player in decorative paints, achieve INR10,000 crores in revenue.”
— Himanshu Kapania
Profit comes third. Until it arrives, the paint losses sit inside Grasim’s reported earnings. The system they pay for (10% share, 50,000 dealers, about a quarter of today’s industry capacity) does not show up until utilisation does. Spitznagel has a word for this path, borrowed from the Austrian economists: the Umweg, or roundabout.
“Rather than pursue the direct route of immediate gain, we will seek the difficult and roundabout route of immediate loss, an intermediate step which begets an advantage for greater potential gain.”
— Mark Spitznagel
A single-category incumbent, reporting every quarter on one P&L, finds the roundabout hard to take. A conglomerate can bury three years of losses inside a cement cash machine. Reported multiple high, normalised multiple low. The cost of a system arrives on the P&L years before the system does.
On 27 February 2025 UltraTech said it would spend ₹1,800 crore over two years on a wires and cables plant near Bharuch. Polycab, KEI and RR Kabel each fell by double digits during the day. On 3 September 2026 UltraTech launched the Ultravolt brand, by its own count the group’s fourth new business in three years, and the stocks fell again. The stated aim is to be among the top two players within five years. Dilip Gaur, the director running Ultravolt, described the posture: “We are venturing at pan India scale rather than testing the market region by region.” Kumar Mangalam Birla made the category’s case:
“Wires and cables are becoming central to India’s next phase of development. They sit at the intersection of three mega trends.”
Kumar Mangalam Birla, on the Ultravolt launch, the trends: urbanisation, electrification, digitisation
The trends are real, but they belong to everyone. The details of the launch say more. Ultravolt went out through 5,000 UltraTech Building Solutions outlets, with a target of one lakh retailers, 1,600 electricians signed up before launch and 40,000 to be trained in the coming year. The plant is the easy part. The electrician is the asset, because the homeowner almost never picks the wire. The electrician does, and the electrician is paid in margin, credit and training.
The phrase “wires and cables” hides two businesses, and the hiding matters. House wires are chosen by an electrician and sold over a counter: a distribution game. High-voltage and extra-high-voltage cables are chosen by a utility’s engineers after type tests, approvals and years of project references: a qualification game. An entrant with a cement dealer network can buy its way into the first. It cannot buy its way into the second on any timetable a quarterly investor cares about. Whether the market can tell these two businesses apart when it sells cable stocks is the trade.
Before reading the launch-day sell-off as a verdict, run the arithmetic the market skipped. By Centrum estimate, UltraTech had spent about ₹900 crore of the ₹1,800 crore by launch. At the 4.5x asset turns that estimate assumes, the finished plant could earn about ₹8,000 crore of revenue by FY29. Now suppose a tenth of that came out of each of the three largest players. Their FY28–29 revenue would shrink by only 2–5%. The other 70%, roughly ₹5,600 crore, has to come from somewhere else: from market growth, and from the 20–25% of the wire market still held by unorganised makers and small regional brands.
That changes who pays. In paints, Asian Paints held more than half the organised market, so an entrant's share had to come mostly out of the leader. Wires are fragmented. A forager works the flowers with the weakest guard first, and the weakest guard here belongs to the unbranded wire-maker selling on credit and relationships, not to Polycab. The leaders also have somewhere to retreat: HT and EHV cables, specialised cables, infrastructure projects and exports, all qualification games an entrant cannot buy quickly.
The arithmetic has one blind spot. It counts revenue and ignores margin. Opus did most of its damage to Asian Paints through the dealer, forcing every incumbent to pay more for the same shelf; Asian Paints' ROCE fell twelve points while its share fell about seven. If Ultravolt's dealer incentives reset the price of an electrician's loyalty across the trade, the leaders pay in margin even where they keep the customer. So the question in wires is not how much revenue UltraTech takes. It is whether it reprices the counter. If the mechanism is right, the first damage will show up in the unorganised share, and in the leaders' margins before their revenue.
Indriya, the group’s jewellery brand, launched in July 2024 with ₹5,000 crore and a stated aim of a top-three position within five years. By June 2026 it had 81 stores in 40 cities and a target of 200–250. Its CEO, Sandeep Kohli, describes the posture plainly:
“We have adopted a mindset of abundance instead of constraint. We are also very disciplined on how to use our resources.”
— Sandeep Kohli, CEO, Indriya
Jewellery is the case that tests the mechanism, because the money is the same and the gatekeeper is different. Titan trades at about 9x capital employed, so the nectar is there. But Tanishq’s moat was never a factory. Tanishq was itself a Tata entrant, and it spent years making gold purity checkable with karatmeters in its stores, so that a family could trust a corporate brand over the jeweller their grandmother used. That trust built up one wedding at a time.
A painter will switch brands for a better margin by Monday. A mother buying her daughter’s wedding set does not switch jewellers for a discount. She switches for a reason she can explain to her sister. That reason can be built, but it accrues at the speed of wedding seasons, not plant commissioning. In the language of [the third essay in my competitive strategy series](https://substack.com/redirect/30c7323e-b5ae-4bf3-a0a0-2b16f816d679?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o), paints and wires carry financial switching costs, and a rival can refund those. Jewellery carries relational switching costs, and no rival can. Indriya says 45–55% of its sales now come through old-gold exchange, which is a fair sign that customers are starting to hand over the thing they trust most. Expect progress, and expect it slower than paints.
Jeff Bezos put the idea in five words: “Your margin is my opportunity.” In 2016, it was found that AmazonBasics accounted for about a third of online battery sales among the top ten brands. Batteries had decades of brand advertising behind them and almost nothing that Amazon’s own search page could not replicate. Amazon owned the shelf, and the shelf picked the brand.
Reliance ran the same play on soft drinks. It revived Campa, a cola that had all but vanished from shelves after Coca-Cola returned to India in 1993, and pushed it through more than 5,000 distributors. Campa sold ₹4,700 crore in FY26, which made it India’s fourth-largest carbonated soft drink player. “We intend to become one of the largest manufacturers of cold drinks in the country,” said Ashutosh Goyal, CFO of Reliance Consumer Products. In both cases the entrant already owned the path to the buyer. The category was just one more thing to send down it.
India already has the extreme version of this story, and it explains why balance sheets beat brands in a price war. Game theorists call it the long purse. Lester Telser showed in 1966 that a rival with deeper pockets can rationally fight at a loss, because the outcome of a price war is set by who can afford to keep losing, not by who runs the better business. John Maynard Smith formalised the same logic in animals as the war of attrition: the contest goes to whoever can hold out longest, not whoever is strongest.
Reliance Jio launched in September 2016 with free voice calls and free data until the end of that year, then extended the offer. Data fell from about ₹250 per GB to under ₹10. A crowded field of roughly a dozen operators became three private players within a few years. Jio had no technology its rivals could not buy. It had a purse its rivals could not match. Paints, wires and jewellery are milder versions of the same contest, and the same purse is open.
Now the pattern can be stated precisely. Entry happens where three conditions meet:
A high q. The market pays many times capital employed, so the gap between buying the position and building it is large.
A moat made of replicable things. Capacity, distribution and ad spend, with little that a patent office or a utility’s approval list would recognise.
A brand chosen by an intermediary the entrant already reaches. A painter, electrician, plumber or carpenter whose loyalty is priced in margin points, and who already walks into the group’s cement dealer every week.
Kautilya would have recognised the third condition. In the Arthashastra‘s mandala theory of statecraft, the decisive figure is often not the enemy but the madhyama, the middle king whose territory borders both rivals and who can tip a war by choosing a side. In paints and wires the madhyama is the dealer and the tradesperson. Whoever wins the middle king wins the war, and the middle king can be won with terms.
The third condition is the accelerant, and it is the part the empire-building story misses. Each new category spreads the cost of the same relationships. The cement counter becomes a hub, and every tradesperson who visits it becomes a spoke that can carry another product.
Michael Porter wrote that “fit locks out imitators by creating a chain that is as strong as its strongest link.” For thirty years that described Asian Paints: forecasting, tinting, dealer credit and painter programmes all reinforcing one another. Here is the inversion. The conglomerate’s fit runs across categories, through one counter and one set of tradespeople, and a single-category incumbent cannot copy it without becoming a conglomerate itself. The entrant arrives with the better fit.
Military theorists of Napoleon’s era, Antoine-Henri Jomini above all, called this advantage interior lines. An army at the centre can shift troops to any front faster than enemies strung around the edge can reinforce theirs. The cement counter is the conglomerate’s interior line. Paints, wires, and whatever comes next are fronts reached from one centre. Each incumbent defends a single front from the outside.
English history has a sharper version of the same lesson, and it starts with a fence rather than a factory. Between 1604 and 1914, Parliament passed more than 5,200 enclosure bills, turning some 6.8 million acres, about a fifth of England, from common land into private property. The mills that followed did not out-compete the commons. The commons were simply gone, and the people who had lived off them had nothing left to sell but their labour. That is the uncomfortable founding fact of industrial capitalism: markets are often made by removing the alternative to them. The fence came first. The factory came second.
Read the paint war through that lens. If the allegations in the CCI case hold, Asian Paints’ deepest moat was a fence around the dealer’s shelf: stock us, or lose your credit, your targets and your perks. Birla Opus did not gain its share mainly by making better paint. It brought a bigger field with a wider fence of its own, the cement counter and the tradespeople who walk through it. The contest in these categories is over who owns the fence. The factory is the easy part.
Fernand Braudel, who spent decades in price records, merchants’ ledgers and shipping papers, gives the fence its place in a larger structure. He saw economic life as three floors, each moving at its own speed. At the bottom is material life: the household, the barter, the informal trade that never reaches a ledger. In the middle is the market economy: the shopkeeper, the merchant, transparent prices and modest margins, the world Smith described and the models assume. On top is what Braudel called capitalism proper, the deliberate escape from the market’s discipline. The Medici bank was not one lender among many; it was the papacy’s banker, and much of Europe’s long-distance trade cleared through its branches. The Dutch East India Company was not a merchant; it was a state-licensed monopoly with its own army. The capitalist’s defining move is not exchange but position: not to serve the market, but to become a node the market has to route through.
The incumbents in this essay looked like top-floor businesses, and their multiples priced them that way. They were really the best tenants on the middle floor. The conglomerates live upstairs.
There is one more asymmetry. After enclosure, capital could opt out of any particular market: wait, move elsewhere, or refuse the terms on offer. Labour could not opt out of markets altogether. The side that can walk away, given enough time, takes more of the value, not because it is more productive but because it can walk away. The same asymmetry runs through these categories. A conglomerate chooses which category to enter, when, and on whose terms. Asian Paints cannot decide to stop being a paint company. Like the labourer after enclosure, it now negotiates inside an option set that someone else narrowed.
Bruce Greenwald’s test, which I leaned on in my competitive strategy series, says there are only three real competitive advantages: privileged supply (technology, know-how, inputs), captive demand, and scale. Run the three categories through it and the pattern is hard to miss.
An explanation is only useful if it would look wrong under different facts, so swap the facts and see what breaks.
Swap the dealer for trust as the gatekeeper and the mechanism predicts a slower entry. That is what jewellery shows: real progress, no 10% share in two years. Swap a replicable moat for a qualification moat and it predicts that entrants stay out. That is, so far, what EHV cables and specialty chemicals show. Swap a high q for a low one and it predicts nobody bothers. All three categories entered so far had a leader trading near 9–10x capital employed. The leaders in tiles and sanitaryware trade at about 6x and 4.6x, and neither category has drawn an entry on the scale of Opus or Ultravolt. Each swap changes the forecast. That is what separates a mechanism from a story told after the fact.
Run the three conditions over the map and four candidates fall out, in rough order of likelihood.
Switches, fans, MCBs and lighting. UltraTech is training 40,000 electricians to recommend its wire. The same electrician fits the switchboard, the MCB box and often the ceiling fan. These products are harder to make than wire, but they reach the buyer the same way. Havells, at 6.6x after a 30% fall in a year, is already pricing in some of this, and the influencer it depends on is now on someone else’s payroll. The wire is the wedge. The switchboard is the follow-through.
Pipes and fittings. Astral at 8.5x and Supreme at 6.8x sell products a plumber chooses, through hardware and sanitary shops that a building-materials counter already supplies. The main raw material is PVC resin, which a conglomerate with petrochemical capacity already makes. The tradesperson, the counter and the input are all in place. Only the decision is missing.
Waterproofing and construction chemicals, then adhesives. Pidilite carries the richest nectar among the tradesperson-picked names at 13x, which is exactly why the order matters. Waterproofing is chosen by applicators and contractors and bundled with paint jobs, so paint companies are already in it. Fevicol is different. A brand that became the word for the category has a consumer and carpenter habit no dealer margin easily breaks. Expect the attack on Pidilite’s edges long before its core.
Innerwear, as a watch rather than a call. Page at 23x is the richest flower here, but the shopper picks the brand, the shelf is a fragmented multi-brand outlet, and a conglomerate brand (the Aditya Birla group’s Van Heusen innerwear, launched in 2016) has been in the category for a decade. High q alone attracts bees. It does not guarantee they land.
What is not next: tiles and sanitaryware (too little nectar), EHV cables and specialty chemicals (guarded by qualification), and any category where the end customer verifies quality personally and slowly.
For incumbents, the lesson is that a high ROCE in a replicable category carries a hidden liability: the cost of defending it once someone notices. Underwrite the normalised return after a rival arrives, not the return before one did.
For the attackers’ parents, the lesson runs the other way. The losses come first and the system comes later, so the reported multiple overstates what you are paying for a business that has already bought its share.
For everyone, there are two leading indicators cheaper than any model. The first is dealer margins and credit days in a category: when they jump, a bee has landed. The second is supply-side question: how much capacity is being added, as a share of the industry? Birla Opus alone added about 40% to decorative paint capacity, before JSW bought Dulux. This is where returns are decided, long before demand forecasts notice. In Lewis Carroll’s Red Queen race, “it takes all the running you can do, to keep in the same place.” Asian Paints is running hard and its ROCE is still twelve points lower.
None of this makes the conglomerates villains, and the steelman deserves its due. Dealers now earn more, homeowners have more choice, and data in India got about 25 times cheaper. Entry is Smith’s law doing its job, and the enclosures, for all their cruelty, came before living standards that eventually rose for nearly everyone. But the steelman argues about outcomes. It concedes the mechanism. Whoever owns the fence sets the terms, and that is what the multiple was really pricing.
Three facts would prove this wrong. If Ultravolt wins meaningful utility-grade cable orders within three years at house-wire speed, qualification moats are weaker than assumed. If Indriya reaches a 10% share of organised jewellery within three years, trust can be bought as fast as dealers. And if no conglomerate enters switches and fans, pipes, or construction chemicals by 2029 while those categories keep trading at today’s multiples of capital employed, the hub logic is wrong and the bees were never as rational as this essay says.
A moat built from capital is a lease, and q is the rent. When the rent gets high enough, someone with a bigger balance sheet and the same tradesperson on speed dial moves in.
Which leaves the question worth taking to every high-multiple stock in your portfolio. If a rival were willing to lose ₹1,000 crore a year for five years to take this company’s position, would the moat still be standing? Make a list of the companies where the answer is yes. It is shorter than the index thinks.
Then ask the question Braudel’s floors pose of every name on that list: which floor does this business actually live on? The top floor owns the route. Everything below it collects the ferryman’s modest toll, and tolls, sooner or later, get competed away.
