Nothing is more useful than water: but it will purchase scarce any thing; scarce any thing can be had in exchange for it.
— Adam Smith, The Wealth of Nations, 1776
Smith went on: a diamond has almost no use, yet it buys a great deal. The puzzle sat unsolved for nearly a century. Then, in the 1870s, economists found the answer, and it is simple enough to carry in your pocket.
Price is not set by how useful a thing is. It is set at the margin, by how hard it is to get one more unit. Water keeps you alive, but the next glass is easy to find. A diamond does nothing, but the next one is hard to dig out. Usefulness is a property of the thing. Price is a property of its supply.
This idea explains the next decade better than any forecast.
Every era has its water and its diamonds. The money is made by noticing when they swap places.
Ours is a swap of unusual size. Information has become nearly free, so scarcity has migrated into the physical world and the social world. It is happening four times over.
Cognition: AI collapses the cost of cognition, and its demand lands on electrons, copper and land.
Media: Content abundance collapses the cost of media, and its value migrates to trusted voices.
Money: Debt monetisation makes money itself cheaper, and value migrates to gold, hard assets and real businesses.
Efficiency: Globalisation’s efficiency is unwinding, and value migrates to secure geography and redundant supply.
The last forty years rewarded whoever optimised for efficiency. The next twenty reward whoever controls scarce physical capacity and scarce trust.
In 1984, at the first Hackers Conference in California, Stewart Brand said a line that became a slogan: information wants to be free. The slogan dropped the other half of what he said.
information wants to be expensive, because it’s so valuable
— Stewart Brand
Brand’s point was that the two pull against each other. The cost of copying and moving information falls toward zero, while the value of the right information stays high. For forty years the first force won. Storage, bandwidth and computing got cheaper every year.
AI adds the last piece: the cost of thinking. A first draft, a summary, a working piece of code, a legal first pass. Each now costs a fraction of what it did three years ago. Cognition is becoming water.
It has happened before. In the 1850s aluminium was a luxury metal; Napoleon III is said to have kept aluminium cutlery for his most honoured guests while the rest dined off gold. Then, in 1886, the Hall–Héroult process arrived, and within a generation aluminium was kitchen foil. The metal did not become less useful. It became easy to get one more.
This is uncomfortable arithmetic for anyone who sells thinking by the hour. India’s services exports were built on reliable cognition at scale. That is the layer that deflates. Not all of it: engineering work tied to physical products is harder to automate. But the direction is set.
Deflation is not disappearance. Volume rises, the price of each unit falls, and the margin moves somewhere else. The useful question is where it moves.
History answers with a pattern. When ATMs arrived, banks were expected to lay off their tellers. Instead, cheaper branches meant more branches, and teller jobs grew for two decades. In [The Infinite Game of the Leisure Economy](https://substack.com/redirect/03294f47-bbb3-4124-b212-73d3092b070a?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o), I followed the same current into sport and leisure, where the world’s richest buyers are now paying for the few things that cannot be manufactured. Abundance doesn’t diminish scarcity. It concentrates it.
In 2011 Marc Andreessen wrote that software is eating the world. He was right. Now AI is making software itself cheap. When an AI agent can rebuild a product's code in weeks, code stops being a moat. The moat moves to what cannot be copied: plants, machines, permits, and data that exists only because something physical was put in the field. Elon Musk has a five-word version: the factory is the product. The richest software companies already act on this. Microsoft has signed up to buy the output of a reactor being restarted at Three Mile Island. Amazon designs its own chips. The asset-light era may prove the exception, not the rule. Software ate the world. Now the world is sending software the bill, in power, chips and land.
It is wholly a confusion of ideas to suppose that the economical use of fuel is equivalent to a diminished consumption. The very contrary is the truth.
— William Stanley Jevons, The Coal Question,1865
Jevons noticed something that looked like a mistake. James Watt’s engines used far less coal per unit of work. Britain should have burned less coal. It burned much more. Efficient engines made coal-powered work so cheap that people found a thousand new uses for it.
AI is running the same experiment. Each new generation of chips does more thinking per watt. Total watts keep climbing, because the appetite for cheap thinking has no obvious ceiling. The bits deflate, and the bill arrives in atoms: electricity, copper, transformers, cooling, land.
It helps to see the whole machine before hunting for its weakest part. Every power system does four jobs. It makes electricity, moves it, stores it and sells it. On the far side of the socket, where electricity becomes intelligence, the same four jobs repeat: chips make the thinking, networks move it, memory stores it, and someone sells it, token by token. Anyone who has studied money will know the shape. A monetary system mints, pays, banks and trades. Money, power and intelligence are three versions of one machine, and the output of the second now feeds the third.
Storage is the job changing fastest. A wire moves electricity across distance; a battery moves it across hours. That matters more each year, because sunshine arrives at noon and demand peaks at dusk. In 2025 Germany logged 576 hours when power prices fell below zero, and Spain 556. The market was paying generators to stop. In the same year, battery packs for grid storage fell 45% in price, to about $70 per kilowatt-hour. Cheap storage turns a glut at noon into a margin at dusk.
The frame also shows where money tends to collect. The firms that earn most usually hold two neighbouring jobs: a power plant built beside the data centre it feeds, a battery owner who also sells to households, a chip designed together with its memory. And selling is where new markets form. Grain got an exchange in Chicago in 1848, and futures contracts soon after. Oil got benchmark prices. Power now trades hour by hour. Compute, one of the largest items in corporate budgets, still changes hands mostly through private deals. That will not last.
Now follow the demand down the chain and look for the layer that cannot keep up. It is not the power plants, and it is not the copper mines, though both matter. It is the grid equipment in between: transformers, switchgear, the boxes that move power from where it is made to where it is used.
A data centre can decide it needs power in months. A transformer factory takes years to build. That gap is Smith’s margin, cast in steel. When the next unit is hard to get, the price of every unit rises.
Agronomists found this law long before investors did. The law of the minimum, made famous by Justus von Liebig in the 1840s, says a crop grows only as far as its scarcest nutrient allows, however much of everything else you pour on. The usual picture is a barrel with staves cut to different heights. It holds water only up to the shortest stave.
Right now the shortest stave in the AI build-out is the grid. Every other stave can be lengthened quickly. That one cannot.
The scarcity is in building the grid, not in using it. A grid is mostly fixed cost: poles, wires, substations. A large, steady customer spreads that cost over more units. One [US study](https://substack.com/redirect/fd0c8859-b473-40f3-bbeb-bcd643033179?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o) found that between 2015 and 2024, a doubling of data-centre capacity lowered residential power prices by about 3.5%, holding other demand fixed. The equipment is scarce and dear to build. Once built, the network gets cheaper the more it is shared.
You can hear it in management’s own words. CG Power’s standalone order backlog stood at ₹17,333 crore at the end of June 2026, 45% higher than a year earlier. Transformers & Rectifiers and GE Vernova T&D both say they are choosing orders for margin, not volume.
But a scarcity price carries its own clock. Voltamp’s board says it plainly in its latest annual report: with industry capacity coming, margins “are expected to get normalized going forward.” The same people earning the rent can see the date it ends. An investor who buys the margin without watching the capacity is buying a diamond just as the mines reopen.
There is an older name for this position. In 1848, when word of gold on the American River reached San Francisco, the story goes that a shopkeeper called Sam Brannan bought up every pan and shovel he could find, then walked the streets announcing the strike. Most of the miners who followed earned little more than they would have at home. Brannan is often called California’s first millionaire. In 1853 a dry-goods merchant named Levi Strauss set up shop to supply the same miners. The riveted work trousers his firm patented two decades later are still sold today.
The miners were betting on the outcome. The shopkeepers were paid by the activity. That is the difference between an AI application and the transformer behind it. The application has to win. The transformer only needs the building to happen.
The building is happening on a scale that is hard to picture. Alphabet, Amazon, Meta, Microsoft and Oracle spent close to half a trillion dollars on capital projects in 2025, and on trend will spend around three-quarters of a trillion in 2026. One company’s capital spending is another company’s order book. Every dollar of it lands, sooner or later, on a chipmaker, a transformer factory, a cable plant or a construction crew.
And today’s shovel-seller has an advantage Brannan never had. A transformer maker sells into grid expansion, renewable plants and data centres at once. A cable maker sells to cities, solar parks and server halls. A precision-engineering shop machines parts for aircraft, defence and chip equipment. They are not riding one rush. They are supplying several at the same time, which is exactly what makes demand durable.
It helps to picture any build-out in three layers. At the bottom is the foundation: power equipment, cables, transformers, bearings, materials, the things every trend needs. In the middle is the workshop: capital goods, precision engineering, automation, defence electronics, the skill that turns plans into steel. At the top is the shop window: data-centre operators, renewable developers, defence primes, the names everyone can see. The window gets the attention and the crowded valuations. The foundation and the workshop get less of both, which is usually where the mispricing lives. And AI is only one customer. McKinsey puts the world's infrastructure needs at about [$106 trillion through 2040](https://substack.com/redirect/3ecf0143-267a-4d1b-930b-33c827c69926?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o), most of it in transport, energy and digital networks. India happens to have an unusually deep bench of listed companies in the bottom two layers, many of them mid-sized and thinly represented in the big indices.
Every nation … ought to endeavor to possess within itself all the essentials of national supply.
— Alexander Hamilton, Report on Manufactures, 1791
Fifteen years after Smith, America’s first Treasury Secretary wrote the rebuttal. Smith argued from the shopper’s side of the counter: let each nation make what it makes best, and the invisible hand lowers every price. Hamilton argued from the factory floor: a nation that cannot make its own essentials is not fully free. Neither man was arguing against capitalism. They were arguing about whom it serves first, the consumer or the country.
Every era picks a side, and the economist Dani Rodrik gives the choice a shape. A nation can pursue deep global integration, keep its own policy room, and answer to mass democracy. It can have any two of the three, never all three at once.
The gold standard chose integration and sovereignty, and got away with it because few people could vote. Bretton Woods chose democracy and sovereignty, and kept capital on a leash. The dollar system after 1980 chose integration and democracy, and asked nations to hand much of their policy room to markets and to Washington. Inside each regime one country played Hamilton while the rest played Smith: America behind its tariff walls before 1914, Japan and Germany under Bretton Woods, China inside the dollar system. Each started small. Each ended by challenging the power that ran the system.
Now the dollar system is squeezed from both sides. Its guarantor carries debts near wartime levels and a thinner industrial base. Voters in many countries are angry about inequality. Answering both needs more sovereignty, and in Rodrik’s triangle that means less hyper-globalisation. This is not a crisis within the system, the kind a rate cut fixes. It is a crisis of the system, the kind that rewrites the rules. Karl Polanyi called the pattern a double movement: markets pull free of society, and society, in self-defence, pulls them back.
India took Smith's road after 1991, and it bought real things: low inflation, clean balance sheets, companies that prize return on capital. It paid in scale. Services exports now nearly match goods exports, rare for a country at India's income, while manufacturing's share of the economy has slipped rather than risen over two decades. In 2025–26 India bought $131.6 billion of goods from China and sold it $19.5 billion. And over the last decade, borrowing shifted from companies to households, the least productive rung on the ladder of debt. Borrowing to build a factory creates capacity. Borrowing to buy a phone mostly does not.
A Hamiltonian turn for India would look like this: credit steered toward production over consumption, public-sector investment leading until private capital follows, a currency kept on the cheap side, tariffs that protect scale, fixed assets favoured over financial ones. It has a cost worth seeing clearly. Scale is bought with return on capital, and guided credit is hard on the shareholders of anything that lives off free-flowing money. But a country that must choose between a higher return on equity and a factory it can defend is not choosing between two numbers.
Two and a half thousand years ago, a student asked Confucius what a state needs. Enough food, the teacher said, enough weapons, and the confidence of the people. Which would you give up first? The weapons. And then? The food. Everyone dies eventually, Confucius reasoned. But without trust, nothing holds.
if the people have no faith in their rulers, there is no standing for the state.
— Confucius
Look at what governments are buying today, and the list is the same three things. Energy security, which is food’s modern cousin. Weapons, as Europe rearms and India writes lists of what must be made at home. And confidence, which is what capital controls, gold reserves and payment rules are really about.
Nations rarely build this kind of capacity by choice. In [The Long Game](https://substack.com/redirect/993b52c8-e7bb-433b-87af-dcb6f466cccc?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o), I called it a forcing function: every great industrial power was forged under pressure, and the capability built in the emergency outlived the emergency. When Bill Knudsen joined General Motors in 1922 after leaving Ford, he accepted a salary about an eighth of what Ford had paid him. Two decades later his manufacturing knowledge helped run America’s wartime production. Capability like that is always underpriced while it is being built, because the market prices this year’s earnings, not the knowledge piling up underneath.
In 1848 the Communist Manifesto described capitalism with a famous line: all that is solid melts into air. For forty years of globalisation, that was the right description. Factories became supply contracts, and supply chains became spreadsheets.
Now the air is condensing back into solids: fabs, grids, stockpiles, shipyards. Efficiency is being traded for resilience, and the state has returned as the largest allocator of capital. When the buyer is a government, reading policy becomes part of reading markets.
For roughly forty years, the winning move in business was to own less. Make things wherever labour was cheapest. Hold as little inventory as possible. Rent the factory, outsource the fleet, return the capital. It worked beautifully, as long as the world stayed calm. Then a pandemic stopped the ships, a war repriced Europe’s gas, and a shortage of cheap chips idled car plants on three continents. The question in boardrooms changed from “where is it cheapest?” to “where is it safe?”
Efficiency can be bought with a spreadsheet. Resilience cannot. You cannot download a transformer, a machine tool or a trained welder. Each one has to be built, in a place, over years.
Put every change of this kind on one page and a pattern shows. The state is back inside the price. It holds the pen on money, it decides what gets built, it wants security more than efficiency, and the market is following it from the few to the many and from the screen to the plant floor.
There is a second change, quieter and more important. Once a nation decides that chips, grids and computing power are matters of security, spending on them stops being an ordinary business decision. The return on investing still matters. But the cost of not investing, of waking up in ten years dependent on a rival for the machinery of the age, starts to matter just as much. Confucius would recognise the trade: a state will give up a great deal before it gives up its standing.
Independence is not declared. It is assembled, one link at a time: secure the minerals, build the factories that use them, own the grids and networks that connect them, grow the know-how that improves them. Only then does a nation gain the thing it actually wanted, room to say no. And each link, once held, makes the next one cheaper to keep.
Energy is where this argument has always turned concrete. In 1913, as he moved the Royal Navy from Welsh coal to foreign oil, Winston Churchill told Parliament that “safety and certainty in oil lie in variety and variety alone.” Seven years later Lenin gave the opposite answer: “Communism is Soviet power plus the electrification of the whole country.” Churchill accepted dependence and spread it around. Lenin wanted the state to hold the switch. After the oil shocks of the 1970s, France took the second road and built a fleet of nuclear plants; for decades it looked costly. Germany chose imports but forgot Churchill’s condition: by 2021 more than half of its gas imports came from Russia. For decades that looked clever. Then, in 2022, it didn’t.
The same choice now runs through computing, and the chain is longer. A country with plenty of electricity but no way to turn it into intelligence is only halfway there. It also needs the chips, the data centres and, some argue, its own models. The question has moved from “where is our data kept?” to “who can turn it off?”
India meets this moment in unusually good shape. For much of the last decade its banks were repairing their books and its companies were paying down debt, so private investment lagged even as public building picked up. That repair is largely done. Gross bad loans at Indian banks, above 11% in 2018, fell to 1.8% by March 2026, the lowest in decades. The Union budget for 2026–27 sets aside ₹12.2 lakh crore for capital spending, and ₹17.1 lakh crore once support to states is counted. A domestic building cycle is meeting a global one.
There is a precedent for this kind of alignment. In 1999 and 2000, China moved about RMB 1.4 trillion of bad loans out of its four big state banks and into four newly created asset managers. Soon after, in December 2001, it joined the World Trade Organization. A repaired banking system met a world that had just opened its doors to Chinese factories. Investment rose from about 36% of GDP in 2001 to 42% by 2008, and the great commodity boom of those years ran on it.
India in 2026 rhymes with China in 2001. The banks are repaired. The state is building. And a door has opened abroad, not a trade treaty this time but a world rebuilding its physical base and looking for somewhere other than one country to build it.
A rhyme is not a repeat. India is a noisy democracy with powerful states, slow land acquisition and a smaller factory base than China had then. The rich world is also far less willing to absorb another export giant than it was in 2001. The analogy tells you the direction of the forces, not the size of the result. But when a cleaned-up domestic cycle and a global one point the same way, history suggests the cycle lasts longer than anyone expects.
Smith gave one of his chapters a title that is also an argument: “That the Division of Labour is limited by the Extent of the Market.” A village can keep one smith who makes everything. A great market can keep one man who only draws the wire and another who only sharpens the point. Depth follows size. India’s own market, and the one it is now being asked to serve, has grown large enough to carry layers of industry it never had.
Watch how an industry settles in a new country and you see the same climb each time. First it assembles: bought-in parts, local hands, thin margins. Then it makes the parts. Last, if it gets there at all, it makes the scarce layer, the piece with the hardest know-how and the fewest suppliers in the world. Each rung costs more and takes longer to reach than the one below it. The rent sits at the top.
Phones are the plainest case. About 99% of the phones used in India are now made in India, yet only 22–23% of their value is added at home, and the government is aiming for 35–40%. The assembly rung has been won. The parts rung is the work of this decade.
Solar shows where the money goes while the climb is under way. India’s approved factories can make about 193 gigawatts of solar modules a year, but only about 31 gigawatts of the cells that go inside them, roughly one-sixth. A cell line costs five to eight times as much as a module line of the same size. And since June 2026, government-backed, net-metered and open-access projects must use modules built from Indian cells. A module maker who buys cells now finds the scarce input sitting one rung above him. The margin, sits where supply is slowest.
The same shape repeats in other industries, at different heights. In chips, India starts where it is already strong, in design, where it has close to a fifth of the world's chip designers, and in assembly and testing. Fabrication, the hardest rung, comes later. In aircraft, Indian firms make lower-tier parts and are moving up to certified sub-assemblies, while the world's airlines wait on a backlog of more than 17,000 planes, nearly [twelve years of output](https://substack.com/redirect/4c3909c3-ab48-4e33-a6ee-c760f992f7a6?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o) at today's pace. Certification is slow, and slowness is what a rent is made of. Data centres look like bits, but their scarce rung is the power behind them; India's capacity is heading for about 2 gigawatts by the end of 2026, and every one of those gigawatts has to be fed by the grid. Space, opened fully to private firms only in 2020, stands on the bottom rung, where every one of these industries once stood.
Two things stand out. The first is that rules do much of the lifting: local-content lists, production incentives, preferences in government buying. This is Hamilton’s argument written into purchase orders. The second is that because each rung costs more, fewer firms reach it, and pricing power gathers in the hands of the few that do.
A rung pays only while it stays hard to climb, or protected. Module assembly was scarce a few years ago; today India can make far more modules than it installs in a year. The question to ask of any rung is not how high it is, but how long it will stay out of reach.
Interest is the price of time. — Edward Chancellor
Chancellor’s point is simple, and easy to forget. An interest rate tells you what waiting is worth. Push it to zero and the future looks almost free, so money floods toward anything that promises a payoff far away. For a decade after 2008 that is what happened. Cheap money rewarded the asset-light and the distant: software, platforms, growth stories whose profits sat years out. It was a golden age for bits, and not a coincidence.
That era also left a bill. Rich-country governments now carry debts close to the levels last seen after the Second World War, and there are only a few ways to shrink a debt: repay it, default on it, or let inflation wear it down. The market historian Russell Napier has argued for years that rich countries will choose the third. Hold interest rates below inflation for years, and the real value of the debt melts. The name for this is financial repression, and someone has to pay for it.
It is a transfer of wealth from savers…
— Russell Napier, on financial repression
The savers pay: anyone holding deposits and bonds whose yield trails prices. The borrowers gain, and the biggest borrower is the state.
Napier’s second argument is where it turns physical. When a government guarantees bank loans, it can steer where credit goes, into energy, defence, factories at home or among friends. Money is no longer created mainly at the central bank’s discretion. It is created where the state points.
We will see a boom in capital investment and a reindustrialisation of Western economies.
— Russell Napier
Put the two men together and the pieces lock. The state needs nominal growth to shrink its debt. It directs credit into the physical things it now counts as security. Savers, earning less than inflation on paper promises, go looking for things that cannot be printed: gold, land, businesses that own scarce capacity, and the names they trust to tell them which is which.
This is also why India’s shift from gold and property into financial assets matters so much. The same rupee that once sat in a locker now passes through exchanges, depositories and fund houses, each taking a small toll. The saver is choosing whom to trust with the price of time.
"a wealth of information creates a poverty of attention
— Herbert Simon, 1971
Simon wrote that more than fifty years ago. The internet proved him right, and AI is about to prove him right again at a much larger scale. When anything can be generated, a report proves nothing on its own. What carries value is the name standing behind it, and the record of whether that name has been right before.
In an essay first drafted in 1935, Walter Benjamin argued that mechanical reproduction strips a work of art of its aura: its unique presence in time and place. AI is reproduction without limit. What it cannot copy is the original: a named person, answerable for being right or wrong, with a record anyone can check.
The idea is far older than any stock exchange.
“People will speak of your dishonour for ever, and for one who has been honoured, dishonour is worse than death.”
— Bhagavad Gita 2.34
Krishna says this to Arjuna as the two armies face each other: a name, once lost, outlives the person who lost it. Markets today are paying up for two kinds of trust at once, the kind dug out of the ground and the kind built over a lifetime. Central banks keep buying gold because they trust paper promises less. Savers keep paying for names because they trust generated content less. The two moves are the same move. Gold is trusted because it is costly to dig up. A name is trusted because it is costly to build. Neither can be printed.
Charlie Munger liked a rule he borrowed from the mathematician Carl Jacobi: invert, always invert. Turn the question around. If AI makes intelligence abundant, what becomes scarce? Three things. Energy, because thinking now runs on electricity. Attention, because content is endless and the day still has twenty-four hours. And truth: knowing that a message, a face or a report is what it claims to be.
The last of these is an old problem. Around ten thousand years ago, people in the Near East began keeping accounts with small clay tokens. Later, for deals that mattered, they sealed the tokens inside hollow clay balls so no one could tamper with the count. The first records of who owed what came wrapped in a proof. When machines can sound like anyone, every payment, email and video needs a modern version of that seal. India has built one of the largest: about 1.34 billion people hold a live Aadhaar identity. Proof of who is who, human or machine, is becoming infrastructure, and infrastructure charges a toll.
Trust shows up on balance sheets as toll booths: exchanges, depositories, rating agencies, lenders people already know. CDSL’s chief executive points out that India’s investors are still only about 9% of the population. Muthoot’s managing director calls the RBI’s new gold-loan rules a structural positive for trusted, organised lenders. When a business earns a rent from trust, its managers talk in decades.
The shadow is worth naming. Those rents depend on savings moving freely into markets. If the state starts steering savings toward its own priorities, as a Hamiltonian turn would, some of that edge goes with them.
In [Building Empires on Other People’s Balance Sheets](https://substack.com/redirect/be667e44-d532-45d5-bbb6-cb3b4cfb9929?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o), I looked at wealth managers that direct enormous sums while holding very little capital of their own. What they really own is reputation, relationships and regulatory permission. A model cannot generate any of the three, and all three compound.
Draw eight of today’s big trends on a single page and something odd appears. The arrows do not scatter. They pile up at three nodes. AI, cooling, electric vehicles and robots all end at the grid: many causes, one bottleneck. Synthetic media, debased money and fractured alliances all ask one question: who can I believe? And behind almost every arrow stands a government holding a lever.
Put the pieces together and they form a single chain. Cheap thinking needs power. Power needs the grid. The grid needs inputs that rival nations control. So states build their own capacity, pay for it with debt, and push savers out of cash into real assets. That capital funds more building, and the loop turns again.
In [The Geometry of Value Creation](https://substack.com/redirect/d70b999c-3124-4c5d-9204-092f7e3ecc49?j=eyJ1IjoiOG81NG4ifQ.6oeetudtJEh-zO1onUJvtadWEdbodFIw0h4xbugrh1o), I argued that every durable business is a loop, not a line: growth adds, compounding multiplies. The same test works for an economy. This loop compounds, because each turn makes the next one easier to fund. It also carries the weak points every loop has: a regulatory choke point, a human bottleneck, a beam that bears too much load.
Every loop has a load-bearing beam. This one rests on a single assumption: that spending on AI keeps flowing. It is the largest source of demand in the chain, and more of it is being paid for with debt each quarter. If the returns disappoint, the arrows run backwards. Grids get overbuilt, orders slow, and the diamonds turn back into water.
The beam is sturdier than in most booms, for three reasons. Much of the spending comes from companies that can pay for it out of their own cash, and a growing share is driven by strategy rather than return. And a good part of the demand is already signed: Microsoft, Amazon and Google together reported about $1.69 trillion of cloud backlog by mid-2026. It is not unbreakable. Every great build-out, from railways to fibre, overshot somewhere. But the physical things it leaves behind usually outlast the bubble that paid for them.
History suggests what that residue can be. In the early 1960s the Apollo programme became the largest user of the new integrated circuit, and stayed so through 1965. Steady demand from a buyer who cared more about speed than price helped carry the chip down its cost curve until it was cheap enough for everyone. Today’s AI companies are playing Apollo for energy. They pay a premium for power that arrives fast, and that premium is funding fuel cells, new reactor designs, long-duration batteries and deep geothermal. If the AI boom disappoints, cheaper energy may be what it leaves behind.
Chancellor adds the warning that belongs beside it. Projects financed by cheap money often work only while money stays cheap, and years of easy credit can hide that for a long time. He describes central banks as having delayed a day of reckoning rather than cancelled it. The test for any beneficiary of this boom is the same: would the project still make sense if capital cost what it should?
So the honest map has two kinds of demand. Durable demand has several independent causes: the grid, cooling, defence, India’s savers moving into markets. Reflexive demand has one: anything justified only by AI capex. The first survives a stall. The second has the most upside and the least protection.
Which node does this holding sit on, and how many independent trends feed it?
Then ask whether it is water or diamond today, and what would make it swap.
Every flip has a clock. A call with no dated trigger is stranded conviction, so name the event that forces the market to look.
The phrase panta rhei, everything flows, is traditionally credited to Heraclitus. Scarcity flows too. Smith’s paradox never really went away. It only waits for the next swap. Forty years ago, information was the diamond and electricity was the water. Today the roles are reversing, and the prices are following, slowly at first.
The world is the curriculum. Markets are the exam.
