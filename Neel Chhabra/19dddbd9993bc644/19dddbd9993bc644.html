The Ten Threads Problem

The cost before the acceleration

Neel Chhabra
Apr 30
 
READ IN APP
 

“You start with a core insight, pull ten threads around it, and if you’re disciplined and lucky, one becomes a large business. The other nine aren’t failures. They’re the cost of finding the one that works.” — Deepinder Goyal

In 2008, a 25-year-old Deepinder Goyal was collecting restaurant menus on a motorbike in Delhi… scanning them, uploading them to a website, solving a problem he knew others had. He wasn’t building a business. He had no theory of scale. Zomato, the company, started two years later when advertisers came calling on a site getting just over 5,000 visitors a day.

That is not just a founding story. It is a probe.

Last year, FY26, 109 million Indians completed transactions worth over $10 billion through Blinkit, District, and Zomato. The company Deepinder accidentally started now touches how India eats, shops, and goes out. Writing to shareholders, he offered a sentence that is one of the most honest descriptions of how large businesses actually get built, and what it actually means to back one early.

You start with a core insight, pull ten threads around it, and if you’re disciplined and lucky, one becomes a large business. The other nine aren’t failures. They’re the cost of finding the one that works.

He wasn’t offering a framework. He was describing what happened. And embedded in that description is an investment thesis that most capital markets are structurally incapable of pricing.

The Map They Don’t Teach You

The conventional story of company building runs like this: founder has insight, builds product, achieves product-market fit, scales. Clean. Linear. Photogenic. It describes almost no great company that has ever existed.

The actual story is messier and more interesting. The founder has a genuine theory about how some piece of the world works… an inefficiency, a transition, a force no one has named yet. From that core, they pull threads. Not one thread. Many. Some live. Most die. The one that works often isn’t the one that looked most promising on day one.

Zomato started as a restaurant listing website. Nine years of iteration followed: reviews, discovery, table reservations, international expansion across 24 countries. The business that processes billions today… food delivery, didn’t exist within Zomato until 2017. Nine years of threads, most of which went nowhere, before the one that mattered finally ran.

Grofers, which Zomato acquired and eventually renamed, started in 2014 as a delivery logistics company. Multiple pivots. Multiple dead ends. It became Blinkit in 2021 — the quick commerce model that now defines a category globally, delivering across 2,243 dark stores at 95% YoY growth. The Grofers-to-Blinkit arc is not a pivot story. It is a probe portfolio resolving toward signal.

District — the going-out platform that recorded ₹2,736 crore in NOV this quarter at 47% YoY growth, looks like a recent initiative. Its roots go back to 2011, when the team did their first event ticketing for New Year’s Eve. Fifteen years of quiet thread-pulling before the category cohered into something worth scaling.

The pattern is the same every time. This is not coincidence. It is structure.

What the Nine Dead Threads Actually Buy

The mistake most observers make, and most investors make, is treating the dead threads as waste. Capital consumed. Time lost. Opportunities missed. Deepinder says the opposite: the nine aren’t failures. They’re the cost of finding the one that works.

Every dead thread returns information that the successful thread cannot. It tells you what the core insight is not, which implementations break under real-world contact, which adjacencies don’t hold, where the adjacent assumptions are wrong. The nine probes are calibration data. They trace the shape of the underlying reality from the outside in, narrowing toward the one configuration that holds.

Deepinder makes this concrete with what he calls the physical moat. Every order on Zomato is a real-world coordination problem — a meal prepared by a restaurant they don’t control, picked up within minutes by a delivery partner navigating traffic they can’t predict, delivered while the food is still hot. Get any one of those wrong and the experience breaks. Blinkit runs its own supply chain — 17 million square feet of warehousing and dark store space, procuring, storing, and managing inventory across thousands of stores, then delivering in minutes where the margin for error is measured in seconds and centimetres of shelf space.

That operational complexity didn’t arrive fully formed. It was purchased, thread by thread, through years of getting it wrong in progressively smaller ways. Anyone who figures out how to build reliably in India’s environment — approximate addresses, cash-and-handshake supply chains, fragmented merchant ecosystems, largely informal workforces, has built something very hard to replicate. Not because they were smarter. Because they paid the tuition.

The nine dead threads are the tuition.

The Rules of a Real Probe Portfolio

Not every portfolio of bets is a probe portfolio. Ten random threads is just diversification wearing a strategy costume.

The probe portfolio only works if the threads genuinely radiate from a single core insight. For Eternal, that core has always been the same thing stated at different levels of abstraction: the physical complexity of Indian consumer life is systematically underserved, and whoever builds reliable operating systems for it — food, groceries, going out, will build something whose replication cost keeps rising as their operational depth increases. That is a thesis about the nature of the moat, not about any specific product. And because the insight was structural rather than tactical, it could survive the deaths of individual threads and keep generating new ones.

The second rule: the probes must be designed to break fast. The discipline isn’t in running ten experiments, it’s in designing each one to produce a clear signal quickly, so dead threads get cut before they become identity-level commitments. Blinkit’s value came partly from being willing to kill the Grofers model decisively when the data demanded it. Zomato’s international retreat, from 24 countries to one, absorbing the cost of that retrenchment without narrative rationalization, is the same discipline applied at scale. Not every company can do this. Most can’t, because by the time a thread is clearly dead, it has accumulated enough internal advocates, sunk costs, and founder ego to survive on institutional momentum alone.

The third rule, the hardest one, is knowing when a thread is genuinely dead versus merely early. This is where most probe portfolios collapse. A thread that is early looks almost identical to one that is dead, except that the underlying insight still holds. The distinguishing question is not “is this working?” but “has the core mechanism been falsified?” If the answer is no, if the thread is failing for execution reasons while the structural thesis remains intact, killing it is the wrong call. If the answer is yes, if the data is telling you that the core assumption doesn’t hold, then every day you stay is a day of anti-learning.

That distinction requires a level of epistemic honesty that organizations are structurally bad at. It requires, as Deepinder puts it, the willingness to cannibalise yourself, to disrupt your own prior bets, to kill things that cost real money and real time. In India, where everything takes longer than you expect, this capacity, to remain in the game, financially, emotionally, institutionally, while cutting the threads that aren’t working, is the single biggest competitive advantage. Resilience sounds like a virtue in retrospect. In the moment, it feels like stubbornness.

The Pricing Error Embedded in Backloading

The financial profile of a company pulling threads looks, from the outside, like a money-losing sprawl. The dead threads are burning capital. The successful thread hasn’t yet separated from the noise. Without visibility into which thread is accelerating, the whole enterprise looks like unfocused spending at best and incompetent capital allocation at worst. The market hates this. The market loves focus. The market prices neat, legible narratives.

But the financial outcomes of probe portfolios are structurally backloaded. You spend years, sometimes decades, building track before the first train runs. Eternal’s own numbers make this unambiguous: the first annual $10 billion in B2C NOV took 18 years. The next doubling to $20 billion, they project, will take less than two years. It took 16 years to reach Adjusted EBITDA profitability. From there, they expect to reach $1 billion of Adjusted EBITDA by FY29, roughly five years to achieve what took 16 years to make possible.

This is not just a management talking point. It is the mathematical consequence of compound systems with long setup costs.

You don’t build the second kilometer of a highway before you’ve built the first. But once the road exists, traffic doesn’t scale linearly, it scales because each new node connects to every existing node, and the value of the network grows with the square of its connections. Blinkit’s 2,243 stores aren’t 2,243 independent businesses. They’re a network, and a network whose density in mature markets — Delhi NCR is approaching 5-6% Adjusted EBITDA margins, proves what the national economics will eventually look like when the rest of the country catches up. The NOV-per-store metric (₹768k per day in Q4FY26) is a measure of how dense existing nodes have become. The expansion cities are finding that density faster than the originals did, because the playbook is already written.

The acceleration is only legible if you understand the cost that came before it. Anyone pricing from the acceleration alone is looking at the output without the input. They see 95% YoY Blinkit growth and call it momentum. They don’t see the Grofers years, the failed hyperlocal logistics model, the multiple pivots, the identity-level commitment to a thesis that kept failing, as the reason the acceleration is real rather than fragile. The probe portfolio creates durable competitive advantage precisely because it is expensive to replicate. Most companies won’t pay the cost. Most investors won’t wait through it.

Which means the mispricing isn’t random. It is structural. It is baked into how capital is organized, into the duration mismatch between institutional holding periods and the timelines required for probe portfolios to resolve.

What Early-Stage Investing Actually Is

In early-stage investing, the conventional frame says you’re backing a team, a market, a product. The canonical question is: what’s your unfair advantage? The answer is supposed to be domain expertise, or network, or proprietary data, or some technical insight the market hasn’t priced.

The probe portfolio thesis suggests this frame is pointed at the wrong thing.

What you’re actually backing, in the thread-pulling phase, is the founder’s durability across the interval. Not their intelligence in identifying the right thread, the right thread is almost never identifiable in advance. Not their charisma or their vision or their market sizing. Their willingness — psychological, financial, institutional, to keep pulling threads after the eighth one dies. Their capacity to absorb endings without calling them verdicts. To return to the core insight refreshed rather than depleted.

This is a temperamental property, not an analytical one. And it is almost entirely invisible in a pitch deck.

The unfair advantage in the thread-pulling phase is the capacity to stay in the experiment. Which means the real underwriting question isn’t “is this the right thread?” It’s “will this founder still be pulling threads in year seven, or will they have called it quits, or worse, called one of the dead ones a success and built an identity around it?”

That second failure mode, mistaking a dead thread for a winner because the cost of abandoning it feels too high, is far more common than the first. It’s how companies end up with strategic commitments that outlive their usefulness, with organizational narratives that protect the wrong bets, with cultures that optimize for appearing correct rather than learning fast. The probe portfolio requires something rarer than intelligence: it requires the founder to be more committed to the core insight than to any particular expression of it.

Deepinder’s international retreat is the most legible example. Running threads in 24 countries. Realizing the core insight was specifically Indian. Cutting. Moving on. Not calling it failure, but not calling it success either, treating it, correctly, as information. That decision cost real money. It cost narrative coherence. Most founders can’t make it, because by the time you’ve built teams and burned capital across 24 countries, those threads have become part of how you explain yourself to the world.

The ability to cut is the rarest capability in the probe portfolio.

The Signal Hidden in the Cost

A company that has already paid the cost… that has run the threads, absorbed the deaths, refined the core insight under real-world pressure, is more valuable per unit of visible momentum than a company that is just starting to pull. The acceleration at Eternal isn’t evidence of a good business at inception. It’s evidence of a proven learning machine, one whose durability has already been tested across multiple cycles, multiple competitive pressures, multiple varieties of Indian entropy.

That is not the same thing as early-stage potential. It is something rarer and harder to find: demonstrated capacity to find the one that works, confirmed by the fact that they actually found it.

The moment to price a probe portfolio business is not during the thread-pulling phase, when the financial profile looks most alarming. It is at the inflection, when one thread has clearly separated from the noise, the platform economics are provable in at least one geography, and the acceleration is beginning but not yet priced. At that point, conventional valuation frameworks are systematically wrong, not because the business is overvalued, but because those frameworks were built for mature businesses, and they’re being applied to formative ones.

A ruler is not a bad instrument. It is the wrong instrument when you’re measuring temperature.

Most of Eternal’s early competitors didn’t make it. The company stayed. Not, Deepinder insists, because it was smarter, because it kept going. That stubbornness is the probe portfolio’s deepest lesson. Most organizations can generate hypotheses. Very few can sustain the experimental posture, the willingness to be wrong on nine out of ten bets, to absorb those endings as information rather than verdict, to return to the core insight with more precision each time, across multiple cycles and multiple years of Indian infrastructure entropy.

The companies that can do this don’t just build better products. They build learning machines. And learning machines, compounded over time, produce the kind of acceleration that looks inexplicable to anyone who wasn’t watching the cost accumulate.

Deepinder Goyal started with a motorbike and a scanner in 2008. He didn’t know which thread would survive. He just knew the insight was real, and he was willing to stay in the experiment long enough to find out.

Eighteen years later, the track is laid.

Now watch the train.

The harder question this raises is diagnostic: how do you identify mid-thread-pull, before the acceleration, which founders have the temperament to stay in the experiment? The answer has almost nothing to do with the product and everything to do with how they respond to the eighth dead thread. On that, more soon.
