"The greatest obstacle to discovery is not ignorance — it is the illusion of knowledge."
— Daniel J. Boorstin
"What an organism feeds upon is negative entropy."
— Erwin Schrödinger, What Is Life?, 1944
Everything is energy, and that’s all there is to it. Einstein’s line is usually deployed as mysticism. Read literally, it is the most useful sentence in infrastructure investing, because it forces a question that megawatt forecasts never ask: energy into what?
A human brain runs on about twenty watts… a dim bulb. Run that bulb across two hundred thousand years of humans and you get roughly 300 petawatt-hours of thinking. What came out the other side: 7,100 living languages, 158 million unique books, 2.75 billion building designs, 150 million scientific papers, and a civilisation that now burns about 180 petawatt-hours a year keeping itself running.
Hold those two numbers next to each other. Every thought any human has ever had cost about twenty months of the world’s current energy budget. Everything we built with those thoughts consumes that budget annually, forever. Energy-for-intelligence is not a good trade. It is, by an enormous margin, the best trade our species has ever made, and we have only ever been able to run it on twenty-watt hardware that takes eighteen years to boot.
Now we have a second kind of hardware. It runs on kilowatts rather than watts, boots in weeks rather than decades, and, this is the part that matters commercially, it can be bought. Which means for the first time in the history of the species, the intelligence supply curve has a price, a lead time, and a bill of materials.
Not f(atoms). Not f(capital). A data centre consumes no matter and creates no energy. What it does is arrange.
Run the accounting honestly. A hundred-megawatt facility draws a hundred megawatts and emits a hundred megawatts… as heat, continuously, to the last watt. Nothing is consumed. The mass in equals the mass out. The energy in equals the energy out. On a first-law balance sheet, the most capital-intensive building in the modern economy does nothing at all.
What changes is the second law. The electricity arrives ordered… high-grade, low-entropy, usable work. It leaves disordered, as room-temperature heat. In between, that gradient is spent building an improbable arrangement: a specific configuration of transistor states that constitutes a trained model, and then a specific sequence of tokens that constitutes an answer. The GPU is an entropy pump. It buys local order by exporting disorder to the atmosphere, and the whole edifice… the chillers, the cooling towers, the water, the fans, exists to carry that exported disorder away fast enough to keep the order from cooking itself.
Which gives you the cleanest way to read a data centre’s capital stack. Every rupee is doing one of exactly two jobs: creating order, or paying the tax on the disorder that creating order produces. Compute, memory, network and the power train that feeds them are the first. Cooling, water, redundancy and the entire mechanical plant are the second. There is no third category.
A data centre is a machine for converting a low-entropy energy gradient into a high-value arrangement of symbols, and every supplier in its chain is charging rent on one leg of that conversion.
The industry already has a ratio for the tax rate and mostly misreads it as an efficiency metric. Power Usage Effectiveness — total facility draw divided by draw that actually reaches the compute, is the negentropy tax rate. A PUE of 1.15 means fifteen paise of overhead per rupee of order created. A PUE of 1.30 means thirty. Cooling is 60 to 80 per cent of that overhead.
The consequence is not environmental. It is arithmetic, and it is brutal. Take an operator holding a 200 MW grid connection… the scarcest asset in the business, three to seven years to obtain, unpurchasable at any price once the queue is full. At a PUE of 1.15 that connection supports about 174 MW of IT load. At 1.30 it supports about 154 MW. The same connection, the same building, the same tenant demand: twenty megawatts of difference, which is thousands of GPUs, which is tens of millions of dollars of annual revenue that the second operator can never earn and cannot buy back.
Cooling, in other words, is not a cost line. It is a revenue line disguised as a cost line, which is why the cooling supply chain is the most interesting part of this build-out.
India generates roughly one fifth of the world’s data. It hosts two to three per cent of the world’s data centre capacity. Every anomaly of that size is either a mispricing or a physical law. This one is neither, it is a policy artefact, and policy has just changed.
Nine hundred and fifty million internet users. Six hundred and sixty million smartphones. Thirty-one gigabytes per person per month, still compounding at 18–20 per cent. Six hundred and thirty million UPI transactions a day. India is, by traffic, one of the two or three most data-generative societies on earth. And until very recently the overwhelming majority of that data was processed in Singapore, or Virginia, or Frankfurt, because it was cheaper to move the bits than to build the building.
That equation has now been inverted from three directions at once, and it is worth being precise about which force does what, because they have different clocks.
The RBI’s 2018 payments mandate, the SEBI cloud framework, and above all the Digital Personal Data Protection Act have converted hosting location from an engineering preference into a compliance obligation. The DPDP regime does three things at once that each independently create megawatts: it tightens cross-border transfer, it imposes audit trails and breach detection that only enterprise-grade facilities can evidence, and it sets minimum retention periods, which means storage intensity per user rises mechanically, without any new user. Localisation alone is credibly worth 1,800–2,000 MW of incremental demand by 2027.
Data centres above 5 MW of IT load received infrastructure status in the 2022–23 Budget, which re-rated the entire asset class overnight: 15–20 year tenor at 8.5–10.5 per cent instead of 11–13, access to infrastructure debt funds and InvIT structures, and the external commercial borrowing route. Then Budget 2026–27 went considerably further, announcing a tax holiday running to 2047 for qualifying foreign cloud service providers operating through Indian data centre infrastructure.
Read the conditions rather than the headline, because the conditions are the policy. The foreign CSP must not own or operate the physical infrastructure… ownership must vest with an Indian entity. The data centre must be an Indian company, notified by MeitY. All services to Indian users must route through an Indian reseller. Where the Indian facility serves the foreign parent on cost-plus, a 15 per cent safe-harbour margin applies.
This is not a subsidy for cloud. It is a structural instruction: the hyperscaler may have the customer; the Indian entity must own the building. India has looked at the global stack and legislated itself into the layer with the physical assets and the twenty-year cash flows, and left the software margin offshore. Whether that is the right layer to have claimed is a genuinely open question… it is the layer with the highest capital intensity and the shortest asset life. Hold that thought; it returns at the end.
Mumbai is one of the cheapest large markets in the world in which to build a data centre, at about $6.6 per watt against $14.5 in Singapore and $15.2 in Tokyo. Vizag is cheaper still. Power runs at roughly 7.5–8 US cents per kWh against a global fleet that mostly pays double. India crossed 500 GW of installed generation capacity in mid-2025 with about half of it non-fossil — 250-plus GW of renewables, third in the world behind China and the United States. A software engineer costs about $20,000 a year against $70,000 in Singapore.
None of the three forces is speculative. All three are documented, dated and already producing invoices.
Every forecast in this industry is denominated in gigawatts. That unit was accurate for the terrain it was drawn on — cloud, and is systematically wrong for the terrain everyone is now walking across.
Here is the failure, a megawatt is a measure of the electricity a building can take in. It tells you nothing about what is inside the building, what the building cost, what it can be re-let for, or how long it stays useful. For twenty years none of that mattered, because the contents of a megawatt barely changed. A rack drew eight to twelve kilowatts in 2010 and eight to twelve kilowatts in 2020. Two facilities of the same nameplate were genuinely comparable assets.
Then rack density went up fifty-fold in a decade, and the megawatt quietly became a container of wildly variable contents while every forecaster kept counting containers.
Read that chart as a procurement document rather than a technology chart and three things follow immediately.
First, the bill of materials per megawatt roughly doubles. A standard cloud facility costs about $8–10 million per megawatt of IT capacity to build. An AI-optimised one costs $15–20 million. Every layer thickens: more transformers, denser switchgear, heavier busway, N+N redundancy, and a cooling system that has stopped being air.
Second, the installed base stops being comparable to the new base. Only 25–30 per cent of India’s existing capacity can be upgraded to AI standards. Floor loading has to exceed 2,000 kg per square metre. Slab-to-slab height wants seven metres for overhead busway and chilled-water headers. You cannot retrofit ceiling height. Which means only about five hundred of India’s 1,900 installed megawatts can be dragged forward at all. The other 1,400 are a different asset class from what is being built next, a fact that gets averaged away every time someone writes “India has 1.9 GW.”
Third, and least appreciated: the same physical megawatt now carries a different vendor mix. Not just more spend, but spend redistributed toward suppliers who did not previously exist in the chain… liquid cooling skids, coolant distribution units, cold plates, fluorinated dielectric fluid, clean-room-spooled stainless pipe, high-fibre-count cable. Ten times the fibre of a conventional facility, on Corning’s estimate. Those categories had approximately zero Indian revenue in 2023.
The market is navigating with a map drawn for the previous terrain. Gigawatts were the right unit for cloud. For AI they measure the container and ignore the contents… which is where all the earnings are.
Vestian says 5 GW by 2030. Rubix says 6.5. KPMG says 7.0–7.5. SemiAnalysis says just under 10. Wood Mackenzie says 12. Savills, Vestian and Colliers, taken together, converge nearer 4–5. Every single one points up.
The instinct is to average them. The better move is to ask what they are disagreeing about, because they are not disagreeing about India’s demand… the demand drivers are documented and largely contractual. They are disagreeing about density. A forecaster who assumes India’s new capacity lands at 10–12 kW per rack and one who assumes 50–60 kW will produce wildly different gigawatt numbers from an identical view of workloads, because gigawatts are an output of the density assumption, not an input.
Which produces the observation that actually matters for anyone holding equipment names: the rupee spread is far wider than the gigawatt spread. The megawatt forecasts range 2.4x — 5 to 12 GW. But dollars per megawatt are simultaneously moving from 8–10 to 15–20. Multiply the two ranges against the capacity actually added from today’s 1.9 GW and the facility capex spans roughly eightfold… from about $25 billion at the low forecast and the low cost point, to about $200 billion at the high end of both. The low end brackets the published $30 billion construction estimate to FY30, which is a useful check that the arithmetic is sane rather than clever. Dispersion compounds when both terms are uncertain and correlated in the same direction… a denser build is both more megawatts of demand and more dollars per megawatt.
That asymmetry is the single most important structural feature of this cycle for a supplier. Their revenue is a product of two uncertain terms that move together.
Strip the word away. What remains is a machine with one input, one output, and eight layers of rent-taking in between.
Every one of those eight layers is doing the same job at a different scale: taking a disordered input and producing an ordered output, then charging rent on the transformation. Land into entitled site. Grid power into conditioned, redundant power. Hot air into cold air. Photons into packets. Watts into tokens. Value equals a function of arrangement, applied recursively down a stack.
The investment question is only ever: at which layer is the arrangement hardest to reproduce, and for how long?
Start with the global content per megawatt, because it tells you immediately which fights are worth having.
This is the single most clarifying chart in the industry, and it cuts both ways.
Read pessimistically: the entire Indian supply chain is fighting over roughly 27 per cent of the equipment bill. The chip, the memory and the server are procured offshore by the tenant and shipped in. India buys the arrangement’s most valuable component and does not make it.
Read structurally: that 27 per cent is the part that scales with the building, not the chip. It is bought by the operator rather than the tenant. It has to be delivered locally because a 40-tonne transformer and several hundred kilometres of cable are freight-disadvantaged. And it is the part whose per-megawatt content is doubling. A supplier does not need to win the chip. It needs to be qualified on a line item whose content per megawatt is rising while the number of megawatts is also rising.
Now scale it to India. The cumulative construction-related opportunity across the value chain is estimated at about $30 billion by FY30, rising to roughly $90 billion by FY35, and, crucially, the split is not what most investors assume.
Two things are worth noticing as you read. First, how many of these companies had no data centre line item three years ago. Second, how consistently the language is about qualification rather than price.
Before the detail, the map. Almost every Indian position in this sector is defined by its relationship to a global incumbent… as its licensee, its supplier, its qualified second source, or its domestic competitor. Reading the Indian name without the global one above it produces a systematically wrong view of both the ceiling and the risk. Aeroflex is interesting because of Vertiv. KRN is interesting because of Schneider. Dee is interesting because of Nooter Eriksen. The relationship is the asset.
Grid interconnection is a three-to-seven-year queue, so the world’s largest compute buyers have started generating their own. That has pulled an entire gas-turbine supply chain… sold out, in most cases, into 2028, and India sits inside it as a component supplier rather than a turbine maker. This is the layer where Indian companies address the global build-out, not the domestic one.
Generation, the demand
“the power requirement of the data center market for U.S. alone is projected to be somewhere around 100 gigawatts in size. And the expected time frame… is going to take 5 to 7 years. And this is only the U.S. market… this number could well go up to at least double the size. … This is only AI.”
— Nikhil Kumar, Managing Director, TD Power Systems
Generation, the queue reverses
“It’s been 180 degrees reverse. We have our turbine customers and our engine customers monitoring on twice a week basis our deliveries, and are taking the machines as soon as they’re ready… even like a 3-day delay, 4-day delay is being severely questioned… we are not having a single machine sitting in our shop.”
— Nikhil Kumar, Managing Director, TD Power Systems
When a customer audits your shop floor twice a week, you are no longer a vendor. You are a constraint. TD Power’s FY26 order inflow rose 51 per cent; its capacity agreement with engine maker INNIO runs to 2030.
Generation, the boiler pipework
“apart from GE, like Nooter Eriksen they have fixed our capacities just for this HRSG piping also. We are discussing with other people also like Siemens… we are expecting a lot many orders from them also for this particular segment.”
— Krishan Lalit Bansal, CMD, Dee Development Engineers
Every gas turbine from GE, Siemens or Mitsubishi needs heat-recovery steam generator piping. Nooter Eriksen reserved 60 per cent of Dee’s capacity at roughly $15 m a year from 2027 to 2029, a forward commitment sized against a company whose market capitalisation began 2026 near $160 m.
Generation, the gas line
“All of our plants are almost booked till FY 2028. They are power guzzlers... looking at the situation that the national grid reliability being poor, they are putting up independent power plants beside their data centers. And these power plants are gas-based power plants for which the gas need to be transported... you require pipelines.”
— Vipul Mathur, Managing Director & CEO, Welspun Corp
Generation, the fuel cell shortcut
“the clean energy sector would be around close to about 70%, and the rest would be all the other verticals... when I said multifold capacity expansions, … the numbers are significantly higher.”
— Srinivas Reddy, Managing Director, MTAR Technologies
MTAR builds the solid-oxide “hot box” at the core of Bloom Energy’s fuel cells, roughly 55–60 per cent of its revenue. Bloom’s entire pitch is time: cells install in about ninety days against two-to-five years for a grid connection and turbine order books sold out through 2027.
Generation, the renewables book
“Data and AI we’ve seen tremendous growth, it is 42% of our total contracted capacity up from 14% two years ago, and it’s grown from about 260 MW to 2,400 MW. … Apple is investing INR 104 crores of equity for a 49% equity stake in 150 MW of projects.”
— Kuldeep Jain, Founder & Managing Director, CleanMax
Data centres went from a seventh to nearly half of one renewables developer’s contracted book in twenty-four months. The offsetting risk is visible in the same disclosure: a 525 MW Rajasthan project now seeing roughly 30 per cent grid curtailment.
Gensets, the domestic mix
“for the full year, the data center business would have contributed between 30% to 35% of our overall power generation domestic revenue… Inquiry pipeline right now after October last year has picked up. Both Hyperscalers, more than that, Colo players.”
— Shveta Arya, Managing Director, Cummins India
A large Indian facility carries forty to sixty backup units at two to three megawatts each, with 48 hours of on-site diesel reserve. Cummins is the specification incumbent; the switching cost is spare-parts fragmentation across a fleet that must never fail.
The power transformer is the most acute bottleneck in the entire global build-out, and the shortage predates AI. Grain-oriented electrical steel has concentrated supply. The manufacturing is partly artisanal. The winding labour takes years to train. Lead times went from around fifty weeks in 2021 to a hundred and twenty on average by 2024, and in the US and EU now run eighteen to twenty-four months. That is the gap through which Indian manufacturers walked.
The grid, India
“Looking at the data center market in India, which we are primarily catering to is less than 2 gigawatts. And the projections are anywhere between 13 to 18 gigawatts… So, for every data center, 15% of data center capex is Hitachi Energy. And… the addressable market is expanding by anywhere 6 to 8x.”
— N Venu, Managing Director & CEO, Hitachi Energy India
Note what is being claimed: not a share of the market, but a share of every project. A record ₹29,555 crore order book, up 53.6 per cent, and a ₹2,000 crore Vadodara plant doubling capacity by end-2028.
Transformers, the export
“CG secured about INR 900 crores worth of power transformer export order from one of the US customer… It’s for the data centre in United States… CG will supply the power transformers specifically engineered to meet the stringent reliability, efficiency, and uptime requirements of hyperscale data centre applications.”
— Amar Kaul, Managing Director & CEO, CG Power
The largest single order in the company’s history, for 330 kV units built and tested in India. Asked whether it was a one-off, the CEO said the game has just started. Domestic transformer capacity went from roughly 17,000 to nearly 65,000 MVA in a year and the company still hears “give us more.”
Transformers, the new lane
“the U.S. market is booming with the expansion of data centers… we also have orders coming directly from the data center expansions, meaning that SEIL is taking an active role in the U.S. market as well.”
— Guilherme Mendonca, Managing Director & CEO, Siemens Energy India
The global constraint, stated by the sellers
“The gap between demand and supply is not really closing, and we still have rather long waiting times.”
— Andreas Schierenbeck, CEO, Hitachi Energy
“Total data center backlog has grown to 228 gigawatts or 12 years of backlog at a 2025 build rate, up from the 11 years in our last update.”
— Paulo Ruiz, CEO, Eaton, Q1 2026 earnings call, May 2026
Twelve years of backlog is not just a cycle. It is a queue, and queues reward whoever is already qualified to stand in them.
Past the transformer, power still sits at medium voltage and has to reach a server at 48 volts. That chain… switchgear, panels, UPS, busway, PDU, is where the redundancy multiplier lives. A facility that cannot tolerate a hundredth of a per cent of downtime does not buy one power path. It buys four or five.
Globally this layer belongs to Schneider Electric, ABB, Eaton, Vertiv, Siemens, Legrand and Delta Electronics, and the Indian names sit inside that structure in two different ways. ABB India and Siemens India are the incumbents, listed locally, which is why their disclosure is the cleanest read available on Indian data centre order flow. Everyone else is competing against those same parents for the panels and distribution the incumbents choose not to chase. NVIDIA’s 800 VDC architecture, scheduled for 2027, is the event that could reorder the whole layer, because it changes what a rack expects to be fed.
Electrification, the backlog
“Data center orders would be up to 12% to 13%. Many of these hyperscalers has already signed right contracts with us, and we are building up the capacity to meet their demand.”
— ABB India management, Q1 CY2026 earnings call
Electrification orders rose 36 per cent year on year, led by data centres, on an order book of roughly ₹11,000 crore. ABB’s addressable share of a non-AI Indian build is around 40–50 per cent of capex per megawatt excluding real estate and IT, and rises on AI builds as substations, redundancy and automation grow as a share of the total.
Electrification, the software edge
Siemens India disclosed 8–10 per cent data centre salience in Smart Infrastructure orders for the twelve months to September 2025, and differentiates on digital twins and white-space cooling optimisation rather than hardware alone.
— Siemens India, company disclosure
The distinction matters. As AI raises the share of new capacity, the market shifts toward integrated, software-enabled power and cooling management, which is a different competition from selling switchgear by the panel.
Electrification, the challenger
Marine Electricals has disclosed ₹284 crore from Digital Edge, over ₹44 crore across multiple orders from Princeton Digital Group, and ₹7.91 crore from STT Global — from a company with FY25 revenue of ₹770 crore.
— Marine Electricals (India), order disclosures
Worth being honest about: this is not a deeply moated technical position. ABB, Siemens and Schneider all compete here. What Marine Electricals has is qualification, delivery speed, and a naval heritage in systems that are not permitted to fail. A ₹284 crore order is transformational at this scale and rounding error at ABB’s.
Storage, the UPS cycle
“On the industrial side, we had robust growth in the UPS segment, which also bolstered by the data center growth here in India. It’s also an export market for us that we continue to build on.”
— Harshavardhana Gourineni, Executive Director, Amara Raja Energy & Mobility
An ESS plant starting at 5 GWh by end-2026 and doubling to 10. A battery company choosing stationary storage for grids and data centre backup over EV cells. That is a capital allocation decision that only makes sense if you believe this demand is structural.
Storage, where the margin actually is
“in case of contingency, they use BESS like an UPS, where the battery component is less than 1% and the converters are much larger.”
Bharanidharan Pandyan, Joint Managing Director, Quality Power
Battery pass-through earns 2–3 per cent. Converter margins run 10–12, rising to 17–18. The same box, and a sixfold difference in margin depending on which component you actually make. Chinese suppliers are barred from trusted US, European and Indian grids, which is the entire reason the position exists.
A quarter of the FY35 Indian opportunity, the fastest-growing subsystem in the building, and until about eighteen months ago, an industry India did not have.
Here is the physical fact that created an industry. Beyond roughly 30 to 40 kilowatts per rack, air cannot carry heat away fast enough. At 120 kilowatts it is not inefficient. It is impossible. So the transition to liquid is not a purchasing preference that a value-conscious operator might defer. It is a thermodynamic mandate, arriving on the chip vendor’s schedule rather than the operator’s.
And because it arrived suddenly, the supplier set is not yet settled… which is exactly why it is interesting. At COMPUTEX 2024 more than eighty liquid-cooling suppliers showed product. Very few have been qualified by a hyperscaler. The barrier is not manufacturing capability. It is trust, and trust is slow.
Cooling, the exclusive factory
“we have an exclusive agreement for the India market, where whatever skid assemblies that we manufacture, we will be supplying it to them. And then obviously, they will be supplying to their customers. So their customers are all the companies who are setting up data centres.”
— Asad Daud, Chairman & Managing Director, Aeroflex Industries
A skid is the pre-plumbed manifold, pipe and valve assembly that pushes coolant through AI racks. Aeroflex is the exclusive Indian manufacturer for Vertiv, which to date has carried effectively all direct-to-chip deployments in India. Capacity is going from 2,000 toward 15,000 units a year against a market management sizes at ₹300 crore today and ₹2,100 crore by the early 2030s.
Cooling, the audit
“we are supplying the cooling distribution unit. It is a critical component… even existing vendor is not able to… they are not meeting the quality standard which we have already… And this is not only for this Vertiv, it will be go for entire data center industry.”
— Vinayak Parab, Chief Financial Officer, United Heat Transfer
A Nashik welding shop won a global OEM’s business because the incumbent failed a weld-quality audit. The order is ₹10–20 crore a year. The precedent is worth considerably more than the order.
Cooling, why nobody price-shops the loop
“they require the reputed player to supply the pipe fitting because it is in the critical operation because single penny of the dust also can destroy their CPU system… in India, we are first, who is the first reputed data center has given us this order.”
— Arun Kothari, Managing Director, Venus Pipes & Tubes
The economics behind that sentence: a current GPU is $40–50k; the server around $300–400k; a 72-GPU rack $1.5–2 m. A small leak kills a chip, possibly a server, possibly the rack, and a warranty replacement in a shortage can take six months to a year. Nobody optimises the last two per cent of a pipe fitting against that.
Cooling, the fluid
“we are the only manufacturing site for Chemours given the nature of the product… it is essential to support the AI growth… that we are seeing around us.”
— Anish Ganatra, Chief Financial Officer, Navin Fluorine
The chemistry layer of the cooling loop… the fluorinated dielectric fluid that lets servers run immersed. Chemours co-funded 35 per cent of the ₹120 crore plant. Sole-site status for a global chemical major is the most durable form of qualification there is.
Cooling, the coil
“For data center, we are only supplying like fin and tube heat exchanger… really in domestic we can say our biggest is the Schneider Electric… in Q4 we achieve around 18.7% from data center only.”
— Santosh Kumar Yadav, CMD, KRN Heat Exchanger & Refrigeration
Data centres went from 7 per cent to 16 per cent of revenue in a year, and 18.7 per cent in the final quarter. Management also says the honest thing: micro-channel coils and plate exchangers for the liquid loop arrive commercially in 2027. No liquid-cooled kit is manufactured in India yet.
Cooling, the MEP leader
“the data centre, the MEP part, where we are leaders, we would estimate the market size to be somewhere around RS.3,500 Crore and we do a business of around RS.1,000 Crore there. This is likely to more than double within 3 years.”
— B. Thiagarajan, Managing Director, Blue Star
Roughly a third of India’s data centre mechanical, electrical and plumbing market, with a data centre order book near ₹1,500 crore and an expectation that data centres reach 10 per cent of total revenue. Voltas, from the other side of the same market, calls the funnel “very healthy.”
Cooling, the technology-agnostic bet
“whatever the technology for data centre cooling is, whether it’s air cooling, whether it’s water cooling, whether it’s direct to chip, whether it is immersion cooling, whatever technology is going to be there, you always need a chiller to chill either the air or the secondary coolant.”
— CMDE Sunil Kaushik, Whole-Time Director, Shree Refrigerations
The strongest structural argument in the section, and the weakest current position: nothing is booked, FY27 is a single reference installation with no revenue, and material sales begin only in FY28. A correct thesis with no clock.
The pattern to extract. Every one of those positions was won on qualification, not price, an exclusivity, a passed weld audit, sole-site status, a cleanliness regime, a fifteen-second chiller restart specification. In a facility where a two-dollar failure destroys a two-million-dollar rack, the procurement function stops being a purchasing exercise and becomes a risk-transfer exercise. That is a fundamentally different competitive game, and it is the one Indian businesses have been unexpectedly good at.
Prysmian, Nexans, Southwire, NKT and LS Cable are the global names here, and they matter less than in any other layer, because this is the least glamorous line in the bill and one of the most reliable. Cable demand scales linearly with capacity because no efficiency gain reduces the metres a facility needs. Cable cannot be salvaged from an older building. And cable is heavy relative to its value, which gives domestic manufacturers a structural freight advantage that no amount of foreign capacity erodes.
Cable, wired five times over
“they cannot afford even 0.01% of power failure. They have four or five kind of power backup and therefore there is lot of cables and wires are applied.”
— Rajesh Jain · COO, Wires & Cables, RR Kabel
This is the redundancy multiplier in one sentence. Nameplate megawatts understate cable metres by a factor of four or five, which is why cable revenue outgrows capacity growth mechanically.
Cable, redesigned for each chip
“we are seeing that the bill of materials is also evolving as NVIDIA’s new chips are being utilized, and the electrical requirements are also starting to change.”
— Kushal Desai, Chairman & Managing Director, Apar Industries
The most important sentence in this piece, and it is about wire. The bill of materials is being re-specified on the chip vendor’s cadence, which means the qualified cable supplier gets re-qualified with every generation rather than displaced by one. A single US site takes $25–30 m of cable, about a hundred times an Indian site of the same nameplate.
Cable, the oil monopoly
“the 3 main players in this are Hitachi Energy, your GE and Siemens. And APAR is the principal and only supplier to all of these 3 companies so far in terms of the HVDC transformer oil… the business will run through FY27, FY28.”
— Kushal Desai, Chairman & Managing Director, Apar Industries
Cable, the conversion rate
“in power sector alone if INR 100 is spent on transmission and distribution, the translation to cable requirement is around 15% to 20%.”
— Shashank Yagnick, Head of Strategy & IR, Polycab India
“extra high-voltage cable is INR 559 crores against INR 308 crores previous year, a growth of 82%... we are already operating at almost full capacity.”
— Anil Gupta, Chairman & Managing Director, KEI Industries
The rule and the proof, one quarter apart. India added roughly 55 GW of generation last year, twice the prior year; the cable conversion showed up as an 82 per cent increase in extra-high-voltage sales against full capacity.
The layer investors think about least and the one that increasingly decides where useful computation can be done. An AI cluster grows three ways… up inside a rack, out across racks, across between buildings, and every one of them adds glass. Corning’s estimate is that a generative-AI facility needs about ten times the fibre connections of a conventional data centre.
Fibre, why glass wins
“GPU architectures are rapidly shifting from 400-gig to 800-gig and all the way to 1.6-Terabit, speeds where copper reaches this bandwidth threshold and fiber becomes essential... It’s a step change in fiber intensity.”
— Ankit Agarwal, Managing Director, Sterlite Technologies
Order inflows doubled to ₹7,687 crore, led by North American data centres, taking North America from a quarter of revenue to 39 per cent. Enterprise and data centres were 19 per cent of FY26 revenue with guidance to 30 per cent, and the mix lifts margin as it rises.
Fibre, the contract
“we secured a landmark long-term global optical fiber cable supply contract valued at approximately USD 1.1 billion, equivalent to INR 10,159 crore… This is probably the highest ever single contract secured by any Indian telecom company.”
— Mahendra Nahata · Promoter & Managing Director, HFCL
That single order was roughly equal to the company’s entire market capitalisation at the start of 2026. High-fibre-count cable… the dense multi-thousand-strand kind AI facilities need, is now over half of fibre-cable revenue, on a record ₹21,200 crore book equal to about 4.3 times FY26 sales.
Fibre, the transport layer
“We were selected to build a nationwide, multi-terabit DWDM network for a hyperscaler data center application in India.”
— Arnob Roy, Managing Director & CEO, Tejas Networks
Everyone else in this section sells the glass. Tejas sells the equipment at each end that lights it. Order book up 49 per cent to ₹1,514 crore.
Fibre, the shortage is everywhere
“there is an explosion of data center applications in the U.S. and in Europe, which is practically eating up all available fiber supply. Most capacities in China and Japan are blocked completely.”
Mahesh Viswanathan, Deputy CEO & CFO, Finolex cables
“We are significantly under shipping demand, and we’re having to make choices as to who we support.”
Michael Hurlston, President & CEO, Lumentum
When a supplier begins choosing customers, the scarcity has moved from price to allocation. Vindhya Telelinks is multiplying specialty-cable capacity sixfold by March 2027 on the back of it.
India’s weakest layer, and worth stating plainly. The silicon belongs to NVIDIA, AMD, Broadcom and Marvell; the fabrication to TSMC; the high-bandwidth memory to SK hynix, Micron and Samsung, all of whom sold out their production years in advance; the systems to Supermicro, Dell, HPE, Lenovo and the Taiwanese ODMs. There is no Indian HBM, no Indian leading-edge logic, no Indian GPU. What exists is assembly, integration, distribution, and a small merchant-compute business… plus the first serious attempt at packaging.
Servers, the transformation
“The defining highlight of financial year ‘26 has been the performance of our AI segment, which grew by 459.6% year-on-year... AI systems contributed 43.4% of our total operating revenue in financial year ‘26, a transformational shift.”
— Sanjay Lodha, Chairman & Managing Director, Netweb Technologies
Netweb opened FY27 with a ₹2,400 crore order book larger than all of FY26 revenue, ₹1,600 crore of it a single government sovereign-AI order. Sovereign demand is the anchor tenant of the Indian compute layer, which is a different risk profile from commercial demand… better visibility, worse pricing, political duration.
Compute, the speculative build
“we have been validated in putting up speculative GPU infrastructure. Where the world is divided between haves and have-nots, we are the haves in terms of having access to speculative GPU capacity where capacity is running out everywhere.”
— Tarun Dua, Managing Director, E2E Networks
FY26 revenue up 50 per cent at a 61 per cent Q4 EBITDA margin and roughly 80 per cent utilisation, with a reported loss because GPU depreciation runs ahead of use. That is the neocloud model in miniature: buy the depreciating asset before the customer exists, and be right about the shortage.
Compute, the counter-example
“component economics turned in distributors favor with memory DRAM, NAND, GPU processors all have sustained pricing firmness through FY ‘26, driven by AI data center demand absorbing global capacities.”
— Kapal Pansari, Managing Director, Rashi Peripherals
And the same shortage read from the other end of the table: at Dynacons, which assembles and runs bank and government data centres, hyperscaler demand made components scarce and pushed Q4 operating margin from nearly 12 per cent to about 9. Identical macro force, opposite sign, depending entirely on whether you hold inventory or consume it.
Component suppliers sell into someone. In India, that someone is a small set of operators: two foreign-owned leaders in NTT and ST Telemedia GDC, three large domestic players in Sify Infinit Spaces, CtrlS and Nxtra, and behind them a tier positioning specifically for hyperscale and AI — Yotta, AdaniConneX, Digital Connexion (the Reliance–Digital Realty–Brookfield venture), Web Werks, with CapitaLand, Neysa, Princeton Digital and L&T Cloud arriving. Only three are listed or about to be.
Every transformer, chiller and kilometre of cable in this piece is bought by one of those names. Which makes their disclosure the most informative in the sector, and the most uneven.
Operator, the announcement gap
“We are also working towards our gigawatt scale data centers. And that is something that in the next few quarters, we will see more progress on and then we will update you.”
— Anshuman Thakur, Head of Strategy, Reliance Jio
Set against Adani, which has one hard number, a 358 MW hyperscale order in Hyderabad, over 560 MW now tied up, ground breaking next quarter. Gigawatt-scale plans at Jamnagar attach no megawatts, no GPUs and no capex. The distinction between a signed power supply agreement and a stated ambition is, right now, the single highest-value piece of analysis available in this sector.
Operator, the convert
“By 2030, our takeaway is that the data center or digitization will become the face of this company and transmission will be the second side of the coin, which is presently the face of the situation... have patience; we’ll outperform the industry.”
— P. P. Gupta, Chairman & Managing Director, Techno Electric & Engineering
And in the same call: 5.6 MW live in Chennai, 15–20 MW by end-2027, FY27 data centre revenue guidance cut to ₹40–50 crore from ₹125, and an admission that the roughly 100 MW hyperscaler anchor it needs has not been signed. A transmission EPC’s engineering competence sits exactly where a data centre’s electrical spine does. That is a real advantage and it does not substitute for an anchor tenant.
Operator, the developer’s arithmetic
“50 MW IT Load to be executed in 2 phases with approx. direct investment of INR 4,500 Cr by ARCPL... Incremental Capacity of 35 MW IT Load at Manesar and Rai to be operationalised in next financial year along with Cloud services.”
— Anant Raj management
₹4,500 crore for 50 MW is about ₹90 crore per megawatt, roughly $9.5 m/MW, which is a cloud-spec number, not an AI-spec one, even with liquid cooling in the design. Twenty-eight megawatts live against a 357 MW FY32 target, entered debt-light with net debt of ₹50 crore. Almost the entire capital programme is ahead of it, which is both the risk and the option.
The data centre is the visible object. The ancillary complex around it is where most of the durable Indian revenue actually sits, and it decomposes into eight distinct businesses with eight distinct economics.
Strip the sector down and there are only five distinct models operating here. They look adjacent on an org chart and behave nothing alike on a balance sheet.
One. The landlord. Secure land, power and permits; build shell and core; lease megawatts to a hyperscaler on a ten-to-fifteen-year contract. Revenue per kW per month, tenant concentration extreme, returns a function of pre-leasing and cost of debt. Infrastructure status made the debt work. What it does not solve is that the asset was specified for a chip generation that the tenant will replace three times inside the lease.
Two. The qualified component. Manufacture a part that a global OEM or operator has audited, certified and designed around. Revenue moves with megawatts built; margin moves with how painful re-qualification would be. This is the Aeroflex model, the Navin Fluorine model, the Azad model. Small revenue, disproportionate durability.
Three. The linear consumable. Cable, pipe, fibre, coils, gensets. Demand scales mechanically with capacity, no efficiency gain reduces it, and freight economics protect the domestic incumbent. Lower margin, far lower thesis risk. The KEI, Polycab, RR Kabel model.
Four. The integrator. Design, EPC, MEP, commissioning. Sells schedule certainty, not product. Thin margins, working-capital heavy, but it is the layer that touches every project and accumulates operating knowledge nobody else has. Blue Star’s third of the MEP market is this.
Five. Merchant compute. Buy the depreciating asset, rent it by the hour. Highest capital intensity, lowest switching cost, and a business whose entire margin is a bet on the shortage lasting longer than the depreciation schedule. E2E’s disclosure: a reported loss because depreciation runs ahead of use, at 61 per cent EBITDA margin and 80 per cent utilisation, is the model in one line.
Geoffrey West’s finding is that biology and urbanism scale in opposite directions. Organisms scale sublinearly… double the mass and you get less than double the metabolism, which is why nothing grows forever and everything dies. Cities scale superlinearly… double the population and you get more than double the patents, the wages, the crime, the ideas. Companies, West found, behave like organisms. They have mortality curves.
Apply that to this sector honestly and it produces the sharpest question available.
The operator has an organism’s profile. The asset has a finite life, and worse, a finite specification life. The floor loading, the slab height, the PUE, the power density were all fixed at design against a chip generation that will be superseded three times inside the lease. Only 25–30 per cent of the installed base can be dragged forward to AI standards. Scale brings diminishing returns: the second hundred megawatts on a campus does not cost meaningfully less than the first, because the binding inputs — grid connection, land, water, are consumed proportionally rather than shared. And the tenant concentration is severe. Every megawatt of unlet capacity is depreciation without revenue.
The supplier can have a city’s profile, but only conditionally. Here is the mechanism, and it is worth being precise because the whole argument rests on it. Qualification is a stock, not a flow. When Vertiv certifies a skid manufacturer, when NVIDIA names four approved cold-plate suppliers to its own thermal specification rather than the server OEM’s, when Chemours designates a sole manufacturing site, when a global OEM reserves 60 per cent of a fabricator’s forward capacity, those are not orders. They are entries in a register that the next chip generation reads from rather than rewrites.
And the next generation raises the specification. Apar redesigns its cable for each new NVIDIA chip. That could be read as a treadmill. Read it the other way: the incumbent is the one being asked to redesign. Rising specification is a barrier that the qualified supplier is paid to climb and the unqualified supplier has to climb for free, on spec, hoping.
The operator’s asset gets less suitable with every chip generation. The qualified supplier’s position gets more necessary with every chip generation. Same cycle, opposite second derivative.
The city analogy fails if qualification is easier to re-run than I have claimed. And there is direct evidence in this piece that sometimes it is. Marine Electricals competes on execution and delivery speed in a market where ABB, Siemens and Schneider all play, that is not a moat, it is a race. Johnson Controls has more than tripled physical capacity in chillers and air handlers. Over eighty liquid-cooling suppliers showed product at one trade show. When capacity arrives faster than demand, qualification stops being scarce and becomes table stakes, and the superlinear curve flattens into a commodity.
The operator case is also stronger than the organism framing suggests. An operator holding land, a grid connection, fibre and an anchor tenant in the same location is not an organism at all… it is an agglomeration, and agglomerations are the thing that scale superlinearly. Ashburn is not valuable because of any one building. Adani’s integrated position — own renewables, own land, own grid, is an explicit attempt to build the city rather than the organism.
So the discriminator is not “operator versus supplier.” It is sharper than that, and it is the test I would actually apply:
A correct thesis with no clock is stranded conviction. Six events, all dated, that convert this from a story into a number.
FY27 order books: The first full year in which data centre disclosure becomes a separately reported segment for the companies rather than a commentary line. Salience is the metric: ABB at 12–13%, Cummins at 30–35%, KRN at 18.7%. Watch the direction of the second derivative, not the level.
Liquid cooling localisation 2027: No liquid-cooled kit is manufactured in India today. KRN guides micro-channel coils and plate exchangers commercially available in 2027; Venus commissions its spooling plant in Q3 FY27; Navin’s Chemours line in Q1 FY27. The year the category becomes domestic.
The Sify listing: India’s only pure-play colocation operator becoming publicly priced. It will produce, for the first time, a market-clearing ₹/MW for Indian data centre capacity, which immediately re-prices every operator and every land bank pivot behind it.
Power supply agreements: The gap between announced gigawatts and contracted megawatts is the cleanest arbitrage in the sector. Adani has 560+ MW tied up. Jamnagar has none published. Every PSA signed or not signed over the next four quarters settles that.
The tax-holiday notifications: The Budget 2026 regime runs on notification: MeitY must notify the data centre entity, the centre must notify the CSP. The first tranche of notified pairs tells you which operators the state has actually selected — a list that does not yet exist.
Transformer capacity, end-2028: Hitachi Energy’s ₹2,000 crore Vadodara plant doubles capacity; CG has gone from ~17,000 to ~65,000 MVA. If the twelve-year global backlog starts compressing, pricing power in the most constrained component in the chain begins to normalise.
The returns gap. The five largest hyperscalers are guided to something on the order of $700–760 billion of capital expenditure in 2026, roughly a fifth of India’s entire GDP, in a single year, from five companies, against AI revenue across the ecosystem that remains a fraction of it. Meta is spending roughly $125 billion against AI-attributable revenue plausibly nearer $18 billion. A material share of Azure’s remaining performance obligations traces back to a single counterparty. That circularity is real, and every Indian company in this piece is a third-order derivative of it. If the capex cycle breaks, transformer manufacturers do not need orders from data centres that are never built.
Efficiency could eat the volume. Token costs have fallen roughly tenfold in three years. Custom silicon at Google, Amazon and Microsoft targets the same workloads at lower power. DeepSeek demonstrated competitive capability at a fraction of the assumed training cost. If the compute required per unit of delivered intelligence falls faster than demand for intelligence rises, the exact inversion of what has happened so far, the megawatt forecasts overshoot in both terms simultaneously.
India is not going to be a global hub, and shouldn’t be modelled as one. Latency to US and European users, the multi-year lead time to deliver gigawatts to a single site, and sovereignty rules that pin workloads to customer geographies all keep global hyperscaler capacity outside India. What India has is a domestic market growing quickly from a low base, anchored by hyperscaler commitments to serve Indian users. Any model that treats Indian capacity as competing for global workloads is modelling the wrong demand curve.
The grid may not cooperate. One renewables developer with 2,400 MW contracted to data centres is simultaneously reporting roughly 30 per cent curtailment on a 525 MW project, about 13 per cent of run-rate EBITDA. India has the generation capacity. Whether it has the evacuation, the transmission and the firming to deliver 24×7 industrial-grade power to specific coordinates is a different question, and it is the one that determines whether 12 GW or 5 GW is the right number.
Note what none of these four break. Even in the bear case, the domestic localisation demand — DPDP, RBI, SEBI, sovereign AI, is legally mandated rather than economically optional. That floor is roughly 1,800–2,000 MW by 2027 on its own. The bear case compresses the ceiling. It does not remove the floor.
Zoom back out to the twenty-watt bulb.
For two hundred thousand years, the intelligence supply curve was fixed by biology. You could not buy more of it. You could educate, organise, print, and network… every one of those a way of arranging existing twenty-watt units more effectively, but the units themselves were not for sale. Every institution we have, from the university to the corporation to the nation-state, is fundamentally a technology for arranging brains, invented because the brains could not be manufactured.
That constraint has just been relaxed, and the entire physical apparatus described in this piece… the transformers with two-year lead times, the fluorinated coolant, the clean-room-welded pipe, the twelve-year backlog of grid equipment, is what relaxing it costs. This is the price list of the second intelligence substrate. It turns out to be denominated in copper, steel, glass, water and grain-oriented electrical steel, which is a deeply unfashionable answer to a deeply fashionable question.
Three things follow, and they generalise well beyond data centres.
Value lives in arrangement, and arrangement is bought with energy. Nothing in this economy is created; things are ordered. A GPU orders electrons into symbols. A transformer orders wild voltage into usable voltage. A chiller orders heat out of a room. A qualification regime orders a chaotic supplier universe into a short, trusted list. Every one of those is negentropy, every one of them costs energy, and every one of them earns rent proportional to how hard it would be for someone else to do the same arranging. That is not a data centre insight. It is what every business is.
The unit of measurement is where the mispricing hides. The market is counting megawatts because megawatts were the right unit for the last terrain. They are the container, not the contents, and the contents doubled. Whenever an industry undergoes a physical regime change while keeping its old metric, the gap between the metric and the mechanism is where the money is. Ask what the metric was designed to measure and whether that thing is still what is being measured.
And the deepest structural question in any build-out is which curve you are on. Organisms and cities are made of the same stuff and scale in opposite directions, and the difference is not size, it is whether growth consumes your inputs or compounds them. The operator with one site consumes. The operator with land, power, fibre and an anchor compounds. The supplier competing on price consumes. The supplier whose qualification is re-read rather than re-run by each new generation compounds. Nobody tells you which one you own. The financials will not say. Only the mechanism will.
India will build its gigawatts. The direction is the one thing every forecaster agrees on. The question worth spending a decade on is not how many… it is who, at the end of it, still has to be asked.
